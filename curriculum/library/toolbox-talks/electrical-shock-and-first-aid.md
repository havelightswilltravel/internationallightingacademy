*Toolbox talk (about 10 minutes) - adapted from our company's monthly safety meetings.*

## Why it matters

Electricity kills and injures hundreds of workers every year, and most electrical deaths
happen in trades that are not "electrical" at all. Lighting work puts us next to 120 V and
277 V circuits every day. Most accidents happen because people do not look, do not think, or
do not respect what current can do.

## Key points

- **Current is what hurts you.** Voltage pushes current; your body's resistance limits it.
  People have died from shocks at low voltages. Shock only happens when your body completes a
  path - between two conductors, or a conductor and ground.
- **Keep your resistance high: stay dry.** Dry skin resists current; wet or sweaty skin
  hardly does. Our company rule is **dry gloves at all times** - wet gloves are an accident
  waiting to happen. For work that cannot be done dead, use the rated rubber insulating gloves
  with leather protectors the company provides, and only if you are qualified for that work.
- **Four ways electricity injures:** burns (contact, arc and fires), shock, electrocution
  (heart stops), and **falls** - a minor shock on a ladder becomes a serious fall. Up high,
  de-energize first.
- **Lock out and verify** before touching anything. If there is any risk of contact you are not
  sure about, stop and call your supervisor.
- **Overhead lines:** stay at least 10 ft away (more for higher voltages) with you, your
  ladder and your lift.
- **Check cords** before, during and after use. Do not overload circuits. Keep electrical
  equipment away from water.

## Helping a shock victim

1. **Do not touch the person** if they may still be in contact with current.
2. **Shut off the power** at the switch, breaker or plug if you can.
3. If you cannot, push them free with something **dry and nonconductive** - dry rope, a dry
   board or fiberglass pole, dry clothing.
4. **Call 911.** Check breathing and pulse; start CPR and use an AED if trained and one is
   available. Every shock victim needs a medical evaluation, even if they feel fine.

**Electrical fires:** never use water. Use a Class C-rated extinguisher (most of ours are
ABC) and shut off power as fast as possible.

## What happened

A lineman for a utility was hospitalized after an arc-flash explosion at an electrical
service on a site where our crew was working. Our crew was not involved, but they saw how fast
it happened. Electrical energy does not give second chances.

## Discussion questions

1. Why do we treat a 120-V circuit with the same respect as a 277-V circuit?
2. Where is the fire extinguisher on your truck, and when was it last inspected?
3. What nonconductive items do you carry that you could use to free a victim?
