*Toolbox talk (about 10 minutes) - adapted from our company's monthly safety meetings and safety manual.*

## Why it matters

A damaged or overloaded cord can shock you, start a fire or trip a customer. Cords are so
ordinary that we stop seeing them. Our safety manual has a whole list of cord rules, and one
of them comes from a hazard many techs never think about: **a closing metal door.**

## Key points

- **Inspect before each use**: jacket, plug, ground pin, strain relief. Failed cords are red
  tagged and the **ends are cut off**; tell the shop so it gets replaced. Never use a cut or
  damaged cord.
- **Grounded cords only.** Cords must be three-wire (two conductors plus ground) with the
  ground pin intact, except for double-insulated tools. Never remove, bend or modify a prong.
  Use cords with a UL (or other NRTL) listing mark.
- **GFCI protection** (or the company's assured equipment grounding program) on job-site
  power. Do not use cords in wet conditions.
- **Size the cord to the load.** Check the tool's rating against the cord's label. Our
  company rule of thumb: a 50-ft 14-gauge cord is good for about 12 A (about 1,440 W) of
  sustained load, and a 100-ft cord about 15% less. Heavy loads need a heavier cord.
- **No daisy chains.** Do not plug one extension cord into another.
- **Not through doorways, holes in walls, ceilings or floors.** A door closing on a cord can
  crush the insulation and **energize a metal door or frame**. If you must pass a doorway, prop
  the door open yourself and put out cones and caution signs so nobody can close it.
- **Never drive over, drag, step on or set objects on a cord.** Route it where it will not
  trip anyone, and tape or cover it across walkways.
- **Unplug by the plug, not the cord.** We have ruined more than one lift battery charger by
  yanking the cord.
- **Temporary only.** Unplug when finished. A cord is never a permanent power source.

## Scenario (for discussion)

*(A teaching example, not a reported company event.)* Picture a cord run under a self-closing
steel door to reach an outlet in a back room. By the end of the day the door has pinched the
jacket down to bare copper, and the next person to grab the door handle completes the circuit.
What should have been done? (Prop the door yourself and cone it, or find another outlet.)
