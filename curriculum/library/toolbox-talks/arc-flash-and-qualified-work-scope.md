*Toolbox talk (about 10 minutes) - adapted from our company's monthly safety meetings and safety manual.*

## Why it matters

An arc flash can reach temperatures hotter than the surface of the sun in a fraction of a
second. Our company's first choice is always the same: **turn it off.** Arc-rated clothing is
the last line of defense, not a license to work hot.

## Key points

- **Know your scope (company policy).**
  - **Lighting technicians** who have had safety and task training are limited to working
    **inside the lighting fixture**. Anything outside that scope needs supervisor or
    management approval.
  - **Electricians** are limited to panels, switchgear and cabinets **480 V and below with
    incident energy of 4 cal/cm² or less**, unless management has qualified them for more.
  - **Medium-voltage switchgear is outside our work.** We do not have the PPE or the
    qualification for it. If you find yourself in a switchgear room or bus room, stop.
- **De-energize first.** If equipment cannot be shut off, energized work requires a
  **written safety plan (energized work permit) approved by your supervisor before work
  starts**, a qualified person, and PPE selected for the hazard.
- **Match the PPE to the hazard.** Read the equipment's arc-flash label (or use the NFPA 70E
  PPE category tables). The **arc rating of your clothing must be equal to or higher than the
  incident energy**. Our standard-issue arc-rated coveralls are only for low-energy exposures;
  above their rating, the job plan has to change - find a way to shut it off.
- **Cat 2 kit means the whole kit.** When the risk is real, wear the Category 2 arc-rated
  suit **and** an arc-rated face shield or hood, balaclava, rubber insulating gloves with
  leather protectors, hearing protection and safety glasses. A suit without a face shield is
  not Category 2 protection.
- **Never alone above 2 cal/cm².** Company rule: no working alone on panels above 2 cal/cm².
- **Bus rooms:** never enter or work alone, do not leave the doors open, do not carry tools
  or material above your waist, and never work on any bus, cable or disconnect unless it is
  de-energized and grounded.
- **Do not touch equipment you do not recognize.** Our crews have come across industrial
  equipment carrying radiation warning labels. Steer clear and ask.

## What happened

At a site where our crew was working, a utility lineman was hospitalized after an arc-flash
explosion at the service equipment. Our safety meetings have also shown video of a
medium-voltage switchgear failure. In both cases the lesson is the same: the energy released
is far beyond anything daily-wear clothing can handle.

## Discussion questions

1. Where do you find the incident energy for a panel? What if there is no label?
2. Which breakers and disconnects is a lighting technician allowed to operate?
3. What do you do if a customer insists the power cannot be shut off?
