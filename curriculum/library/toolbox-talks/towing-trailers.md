*Toolbox talk (about 10 minutes) - adapted from our company's monthly safety meetings.*

## Why it matters

We tow scissor lifts, man lifts and box trailers all over the region. A heavy trailer without
working brakes can push the truck through an intersection. Our company has had a near miss
for exactly that reason: a new vehicle went out towing a heavy trailer **with no trailer brake
controller installed.**

## Key points

- **No brake controller, no heavy trailer.** Most of our trucks have one, but new vehicles and
  some lift trucks may not. If yours does not, do not tow a braked trailer with it.
- **Set the brake controller every time you hook up.** Each truck and trailer combination is
  different. Follow the company setup guide (and video): adjust the gain so the trailer brakes
  firmly without locking up. Some controllers have no dial showing the setting - only plus and
  minus buttons - so you must set it each time. Small utility and box trailers have no brakes.
- **The trailer gets its own DVIR:** coupler latched and pinned on the receiver; **safety chains
  crossed under the coupler**; breakaway cable attached to the truck separately from the chains
  and the breakaway battery charged; lights and reflectors working; registration, insurance
  and inspection current; tires and load secured or covered.
- **Weight matters.** Registered weight must exceed actual weight, including the trailer. A
  scissor lift alone is about 3,300 lb.
- **Pins in holes.** Every hitch pin and clip goes in, every time.
- **No homemade repairs.** If a spare-tire mount breaks, **remove the spare** until it is
  repaired the manufacturer's way - do not strap or wire it on. Stop at a tire or trailer shop.
- **Check the jack and tongue welds** after any bump or scrape; damage there can drop the
  trailer.
- **Report all trailer damage**, even if it looks minor.

## What happened

A tech found a trailer spare-tire mount broken and improvised a fix to hold the tire on. The
photo made it to our safety meeting: the only safe repair is the manufacturer's original
design. He should have removed the spare or stopped at a trailer shop. In another case a
trailer jack was bent in a scrape; the bend was minor, but the welds holding it on had to be
checked before it went back on the road.

## Discussion questions

1. How do you know your brake controller gain is set right?
2. Why are the safety chains crossed?
3. What do you do if you are on the road and find a trailer light out?
