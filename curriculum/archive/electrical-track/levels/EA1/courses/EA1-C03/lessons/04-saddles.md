## When to Use a Saddle

A **saddle** carries conduit over an obstruction that crosses its path — a pipe, a small duct, a
beam flange — and returns it to the original line on the other side.

- A **three-point saddle** works well for **round or narrow** obstructions such as pipes.
- A **four-point saddle** is used for **wide or flat-topped** obstructions such as ducts, cable
  trays and beams. It is simply two offsets.

## Three-Point Saddles

A three-point saddle has a **center bend** and two **outside bends** in the opposite direction.
The most common combination is a **45° center bend with two 22½° outside bends** (the outside
bends add up to the center bend so the conduit returns to its original direction).

| Item | Value for 45° / 22½° saddle |
|---|---|
| Distance from center mark to each outside mark | **2.5 × obstruction height** |
| Shrink | **3/16 in per inch** of obstruction height |
| Total bend degrees | 90° (45 + 22½ + 22½) toward the 360° limit |

Some electricians use a 60° center with two 30° outside bends for taller obstructions in tight
spaces; that uses a different multiplier and shrink, so check a chart and remember it counts 120°
toward the 360° limit.

### Which Bender Mark?
Many hand benders have a **rim notch** or **teardrop** mark used for the **center** bend of a
three-point saddle, and the **arrow** used for the outside bends. Other benders use different
marks. Confirm with your bender's instructions — using the wrong mark shifts the saddle off the
obstruction.

## Worked Example 1 — Three-Point Saddle Over a Pipe

A 1/2 in EMT run must cross over a pipe that is **2 in** tall (outside diameter, plus clearance as
needed). The center of the pipe is **30 in** from the end of the conduit.

1. **Shrink:** 2 × 3/16 = **3/8 in**
2. **Center mark:** 30 + 3/8 = **30-3/8 in** from the end
3. **Outside marks:** 2 × 2.5 = 5 in on each side of the center mark:
   - 30-3/8 − 5 = **25-3/8 in**
   - 30-3/8 + 5 = **35-3/8 in**
4. **Bend the center** to 45° using the center mark on the bender (rim notch/teardrop on many
   benders).
5. **Rotate the conduit 180°** and bend one outside mark to 22½° using the arrow, with the bend
   going the opposite direction to the center bend.
6. **Rotate again as needed** and bend the other outside mark to 22½°, making sure it lies in the
   same plane.
7. Lay the conduit on a flat surface over a test piece of the obstruction. The straight sections
   should sit flat on the floor and the peak should clear the obstruction with the center over its
   center line.

### Worked Example 2 — Taller Obstruction
A 3 in obstruction centered 48 in from the end:

- Shrink = 3 × 3/16 = 9/16 in
- Center mark = 48 + 9/16 = **48-9/16 in**
- Outside marks = 3 × 2.5 = 7-1/2 in each side: **41-1/16 in** and **56-1/16 in**

## Four-Point Saddles

A four-point saddle is two offsets: one up and over the obstruction, one back down. You need:

- **Height** of the obstruction (plus clearance)
- **Width** of the obstruction (this sets the distance between the two inside bends)
- **Bend angle** for each offset (often 30° or 45°; both offsets usually use the same angle)

### Worked Example 3 — Four-Point Saddle Over a Duct
A duct is **4 in high and 10 in wide**. Its near edge is **36 in** from the end of the conduit.
Use 30° bends.

1. Distance between bends for each offset: 4 × 2 = **8 in**
2. Shrink per offset: 4 × 1/4 = **1 in**
3. **First offset:** the second bend (top of the rise) should land at the near edge of the duct.
   Mark bend 2 at 36 + 1 (shrink) = **37 in**; mark bend 1 at 37 − 8 = **29 in**.
4. **Second offset:** bend 3 (start of the drop) goes at the far edge of the duct. Measure the
   duct width from bend 2: 37 + 10 = **47 in**. (Many electricians add the second offset's shrink
   here as well when the far end must land at a fixed point; if only clearance matters, the
   width measurement alone is enough.) Mark bend 4 at 47 + 8 = **55 in**.
5. Bend 1 and 2 as an offset, then 3 and 4 as an offset in the opposite direction, keeping all
   four bends in one plane.

Total degrees: four 30° bends = **120°** toward the 360° limit.

> **Safety:** Obstructions such as pipes may be hot, cold or carrying hazardous contents; ducts
> may have sharp edges. Wear gloves and do not strap conduit to piping, ductwork or ceiling
> support wires that are not intended to support raceways — the NEC generally prohibits using
> ceiling grid support wires as the sole support for raceways unless permitted by the specific
> rules of 300.11. If a saddle must cross near energized equipment or existing circuits, verify
> those circuits are de-energized (LOTO, live-dead-live) before working close to them.

## Troubleshooting Saddles

| Symptom | Likely cause |
|---|---|
| Saddle peak not centered over obstruction | Wrong bender mark for center bend, or shrink not added |
| Ends not in line after the saddle | Outside bends unequal or not half the center bend |
| Conduit won't lie flat | Dog-leg — conduit rolled between bends |
| Saddle too low | Obstruction height measured wrong or no clearance allowed |

## Key Takeaways
- Use a three-point saddle for round/narrow obstructions; four-point (two offsets) for wide ones.
- 45°/22½° three-point saddle: outside marks at 2.5 × height from center; shrink 3/16 in per inch.
- Add shrink to the distance to the obstruction's center to locate the center mark.
- Check which bender mark your bender uses for the center bend.
- Four-point saddle: offset up, width, offset down — keep all bends in one plane.
- Count saddle degrees toward the 360° limit (90° for a 45/22½ three-point).
