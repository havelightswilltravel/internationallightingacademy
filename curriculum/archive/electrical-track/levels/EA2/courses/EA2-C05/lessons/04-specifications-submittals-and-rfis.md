## Drawings Show Where; Specifications Show What and How

The **specifications** ("specs") are a written part of the contract documents. They define the
quality of materials, approved manufacturers, installation methods, testing and closeout
requirements. When the drawings say "panel LP-1A," the specs say what kind of panel, which
manufacturers are acceptable, what the bus must be made of, and how it must be labeled.

## CSI MasterFormat

Most commercial specifications are organized by **CSI MasterFormat**, a numbering system of
divisions and sections. The electrical-related divisions:

| Division | Title |
|---|---|
| 26 | Electrical |
| 27 | Communications |
| 28 | Electronic Safety and Security (including fire alarm in many projects) |

Common Division 26 sections (titles vary slightly by project):

| Section | Typical title |
|---|---|
| 26 05 00 | Common Work Results for Electrical |
| 26 05 19 | Low-Voltage Electrical Power Conductors and Cables |
| 26 05 26 | Grounding and Bonding for Electrical Systems |
| 26 05 29 | Hangers and Supports for Electrical Systems |
| 26 05 33 | Raceways and Boxes for Electrical Systems |
| 26 05 53 | Identification for Electrical Systems |
| 26 24 16 | Panelboards |
| 26 27 26 | Wiring Devices |
| 26 51 00 | Interior Lighting |

### Section Format: Three Parts
Each section is typically divided into:
1. **Part 1 — General:** scope, references to standards, submittals required, quality assurance
2. **Part 2 — Products:** acceptable manufacturers, materials, ratings
3. **Part 3 — Execution:** installation methods, field quality control and testing

> **Field tip:** Part 3 is where installers find the requirements that exceed the NEC — for
> example, "minimum ¾ in. conduit," "no MC cable home runs," "provide a green insulated EGC in all
> raceways," "minimum 12 AWG for branch circuits," "maximum voltage drop of 3%," or color coding.
> Read Part 3 of the raceway, conductor and grounding sections before rough-in.

## Order of Precedence and Conflicts

The general conditions of the contract usually establish which document governs when drawings and
specs conflict, and many contracts say the more stringent requirement applies. Apprentices should
never decide a conflict themselves — bring it to the foreman.

## Submittals

Before equipment is ordered, the contractor sends **submittals** — product data sheets, shop
drawings and samples — to the engineer for review. Reviewed submittals come back stamped, for
example:

| Stamp | Meaning |
|---|---|
| No exceptions taken / Approved | Order and install as submitted |
| Make corrections noted | Proceed, incorporating the engineer's comments |
| Revise and resubmit | Do not order; correct and resubmit |
| Rejected | Not acceptable; submit a different product |

Install what was **approved**, not just what's on the truck. Check model numbers, voltage, AIC
rating and options on delivered equipment against the approved submittal.

## RFIs — Requests for Information

An **RFI** is a formal, written question to the design team when the documents are unclear,
conflicting or don't work in the field. A good RFI includes:
- A clear reference (sheet, detail, spec section)
- A description of the conflict, with photos or marked-up sketches
- A **proposed solution**
- The date a response is needed to avoid delaying work

### Worked Example — Writing an RFI
*Conflict:* Sheet E2.03 shows four 2-wire, 20 A receptacle circuits (eight current-carrying
12 AWG THHN conductors) in one raceway through a mechanical room with a specified design ambient
of 40°C. Using EA2-C03 methods: 30 A × 0.91 × 0.70 = 19.1 A,
which is less than the 20 A OCPD protecting multi-outlet receptacle circuits.

*Proposed solution:* "Provide 10 AWG conductors for circuits LP-2A-2, 4, 6, 8 in this raceway, or
split into two raceways of four current-carrying conductors each. Please confirm."

Responses to RFIs may come as **ASIs** (Architect's Supplemental Instructions), bulletins or
revised sheets. Changes that affect cost or schedule may result in a change order — that is the
project manager's responsibility, not the installer's.

## As-Built (Record) Drawings

As work progresses, the crew marks up a dedicated field set — the **as-builts** — showing what was
actually installed:
- Actual routing of underground and concealed raceways, with dimensions from fixed references
- Changes in circuit numbers, panel locations or device locations
- Added junction and pull boxes
- RFI and ASI changes incorporated

Owners and future electricians rely on record drawings for maintenance, troubleshooting and
renovations. Inaccurate as-builts have led to cut energized conductors during later work.

> **Safety:** Underground and in-slab raceway locations on record drawings are used by others to
> plan excavation and core drilling. Record them accurately. When your crew drills or cuts into an
> existing slab or wall, scan or verify for embedded conductors first, and assume any embedded
> raceway may be energized until proven otherwise.

## Key Takeaways
- Specifications define materials, manufacturers, methods and testing; Division 26 covers electrical.
- Each section has Part 1 General, Part 2 Products, Part 3 Execution — read Part 3 before rough-in.
- Install what the approved submittal shows; check delivered equipment against it.
- Use RFIs for conflicts and unclear documents, with references and a proposed solution.
- Keep accurate as-builts — they protect the next electrician.
