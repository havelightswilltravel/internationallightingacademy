## From Awareness to Qualified

In the lighting track you learned NFPA 70E at the awareness level. As an EA3 apprentice you
start doing tasks that **require** you to be a qualified person for that task: measuring
voltage in panels, verifying absence of voltage on three-phase feeders, troubleshooting control
circuits. This course uses **NFPA 70E, 2024 edition**. Your employer's electrical safety program
and the edition referenced by OSHA enforcement and your employer govern; check which edition
your company has adopted.

### OSHA and NFPA 70E
- **OSHA** regulations (29 CFR 1910 Subpart S for general industry, 1926 Subpart K for
  construction) are law. They require employers to protect workers from electrical hazards but
  are written in general terms.
- **NFPA 70E** is a consensus standard that explains *how* to meet those obligations. OSHA
  frequently points to it as evidence of recognized hazards and feasible protective methods.
- **The NEC (NFPA 70)** governs installation. **NFPA 70E** governs safe work practices for
  employees working on or near electrical equipment. **NFPA 70B** covers maintenance.

## What "Qualified" Means

NFPA 70E defines a **qualified person** as one who has demonstrated skills and knowledge related
to the construction and operation of electrical equipment and installations and has received
safety training to identify the hazards and reduce the associated risk (paraphrased).

Key points:
- Qualification is **task-specific**. You may be qualified to measure voltage on a 208Y/120 V
  panel and *not* qualified to rack a breaker in 480 V switchgear.
- A license or job title does not make someone qualified; training and demonstrated ability do.
- An employee undergoing on-the-job training who has demonstrated the ability to perform duties
  safely at their level, and who is under the **direct supervision** of a qualified person, is
  considered qualified for those duties.

### What a qualified person must know (summary)
- How to distinguish exposed energized parts from other parts
- How to determine the nominal voltage of exposed parts
- The approach distances and how to apply them
- How to recognize decision-making and hazard-assessment requirements: selecting PPE,
  planning the job, and recognizing when to stop
- How to select and use test instruments, including verifying their operation
- How to establish an electrically safe work condition

## Training and Retraining

| Requirement (NFPA 70E concept) | Frequency |
|---|---|
| Electrical safety training — classroom, on-the-job, or both | Before assignment; matched to the risk of the task |
| Retraining in safety-related work practices | At intervals not exceeding **3 years**, and sooner when supervision or inspection shows the worker isn't following practices, new technology or procedures are introduced, or the worker changes jobs |
| Emergency response training (contact release, first aid/CPR/AED where expected) | **Annually** verified/refreshed |
| Employer documentation of training | Content, name, and dates of training |

## The Electrical Safety Program (ESP)

Every employer must implement and document an overall electrical safety program that directs
activity appropriate to the risk. Elements include:

- **Inspection** of new or modified equipment before it is placed in service
- **Condition of maintenance** considered in every risk assessment — equipment that is poorly
  maintained may not operate as designed during a fault
- **Hazard identification and risk assessment** procedures
- **Job briefings** before each job (and when conditions change)
- **Incident investigation** procedures
- **Lockout/tagout** program, audited at least annually
- **Auditing**: the ESP at intervals not exceeding 3 years, and field work audited at least
  annually to verify procedures are followed

### Hierarchy of risk controls
NFPA 70E requires risk control methods to be applied in this order of preference:

1. **Elimination** — de-energize (establish an electrically safe work condition)
2. **Substitution** — e.g., lower-voltage control circuits, non-contact testers
3. **Engineering controls** — remote racking, arc-resistant switchgear, finger-safe covers,
   infrared windows
4. **Awareness** — signs, labels, barricades
5. **Administrative controls** — procedures, permits, training, job briefings
6. **PPE** — the last line of defense

PPE does not prevent an arc flash or a shock; it only reduces injury severity. Most electrical
injuries are prevented by **eliminating** the hazard.

## Host and Contract Employers

When you work in a customer's facility, the **host employer** must inform contract employers
of known hazards and facility safety program information, and the **contract employer** must
make sure its workers follow both the contractor's and the host's applicable requirements.
Ask about site-specific rules, labels, studies, and energized work approvals before work starts.

## Job Briefings

Before starting each job, the person in charge conducts a job briefing covering:
- Hazards associated with the job
- Work procedures involved
- Special precautions
- Energy source controls
- PPE requirements
- The information on the energized electrical work permit, if required

Additional briefings are held when conditions change significantly during the job.

> **Safety:** Being qualified includes knowing when to **stop**. If the equipment doesn't match
> the drawings, a label is missing, the condition of maintenance is doubtful, or you are unsure
> of the voltage, stop and ask. Any worker may stop a job they believe is unsafe.

## Key Takeaways
- OSHA is the law; NFPA 70E (2024) explains how to work safely; the NEC covers installation.
- Qualification is task-specific and based on training and demonstrated ability.
- Retrain at intervals not exceeding 3 years; refresh emergency response training annually.
- The ESP includes risk assessment, job briefings, LOTO, incident investigation and audits.
- Apply the hierarchy of controls: eliminate first, PPE last.
- Hold a job briefing before every job and stop when conditions change.
