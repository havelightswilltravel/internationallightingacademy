## There Is No Single "Journeyman Exam"
Electrical licensing in the United States is controlled by **states**, and in some places by
cities or counties. Each licensing authority decides who may sit for the exam, which code
edition is tested, what references are allowed, how long the exam is, and what score passes.
A few states have no statewide journeyman license at all and leave it to local jurisdictions;
some states have reciprocity agreements with others.

What is common:
- **Experience requirement:** most jurisdictions require roughly **8,000 hours** (about four
  years) of supervised work under a licensed electrician, often with a minimum amount of
  classroom or related instruction. Some accept fewer hours with a registered apprenticeship
  completion certificate.
- **Exam vendor:** many states contract with a testing company such as **PSI** or **Prometric**
  (others use Pearson VUE or run their own exams). The vendor publishes the official
  **candidate information bulletin** (sometimes called a candidate handbook).
- **Format:** usually multiple choice, computer-based at a testing center, **open book** (the
  NEC is allowed), commonly about **80 to 100 questions** in roughly **4 to 5 hours**. Some
  states add a separate business-and-law or state-rules portion, or a practical component.
- **Passing score:** commonly **70% or 75%**, sometimes a scaled score.

These are typical figures only. **Your state's current candidate bulletin is the only source
that counts.** Bulletins change when a state adopts a new code edition, so download the
current one every time you schedule.

## The Code Edition
Exams are based on the edition of the NEC adopted in that jurisdiction, which may lag or lead
the edition you trained on. This program teaches the **2023 NEC (NFPA 70)**. As of this writing
some jurisdictions are still on the 2020 or 2017 edition and others are moving to the **2026
NEC**, which made major structural changes — for example, branch-circuit, feeder, and service
load calculations moved from Article 220 to a **new Article 120**, and other content was
reorganized and renumbered. If your exam uses a different edition than your study material:

1. Buy (or borrow) the exact edition tested.
2. Re-tab it — section numbers you memorized may have moved.
3. Re-check key numeric values in the new edition rather than trusting memory.

## Reading the Candidate Bulletin
Build a one-page "My Exam" fact sheet from the bulletin:

| Item | What to record |
|---|---|
| Licensing authority | Board name, website, application deadlines |
| Eligibility | Hours, documentation, affidavit forms, fees |
| Exam vendor | Company, scheduling method, testing sites |
| Code edition | e.g., "2023 NEC" — exactly as stated |
| Other references | Some states allow other books (e.g., a calculations reference, state rules) |
| Book rules | Bound books only? Tabs allowed? Highlighting allowed? Handwritten notes prohibited? |
| Calculator | Non-programmable, silent, battery-powered — model restrictions |
| Questions / time | Number of questions and total time; any separate sections |
| Passing score | Raw percentage or scaled |
| Content outline | Percent of questions by topic |
| Test-day rules | ID requirements, arrival time, breaks, retake waiting period |

### Example Content Outline (Illustrative Only)
Bulletins typically publish a weighted outline like this one. Your state's will differ.

| Topic area | Approx. % of exam |
|---|---|
| General knowledge, definitions, theory | 5–10% |
| Services, feeders, branch circuits, and calculations | 15–20% |
| Grounding and bonding | 8–12% |
| Wiring methods and materials | 15–20% |
| Equipment for general use (motors, transformers, etc.) | 15–20% |
| Special occupancies, equipment, and conditions | 15–20% |
| Communications, tables, and annexes | 3–5% |

Use the outline to allocate study time: if motors and transformers are 15% of a 100-question
exam, that is about 15 questions — far more than the share of most "fun" topics.

## How the Exam Is Different From the Field
- Answers are based on the **code text**, not on local practice or "how we always do it."
- Many questions ask for a **minimum** or **maximum** — read which one.
- Exceptions matter. A question may be written so the exception applies.
- Informational notes are **not** enforceable requirements; an exam answer will normally rely on
  the rule itself.
- Calculation answers are often listed in close pairs (one with a common mistake built in).

> **Safety:** Exam preparation does not end your safety obligations on the job. Fatigue from
> late-night studying is a real hazard on a job site. Plan study time so you still get adequate
> sleep, and never rush energized-work tasks because your mind is on the exam.

## Key Takeaways
- Licensing is state (or local) controlled; requirements and exams vary.
- Typical: ~8,000 hours of experience; PSI/Prometric computer-based, open-book exams of about
  80–100 questions in 4–5 hours; passing around 70–75%.
- The current state candidate bulletin is the only authoritative source — download it and build
  a fact sheet.
- Study and tab the exact NEC edition tested; the 2026 NEC renumbered major content, including
  moving load calculations to Article 120.
- Exam answers follow code text, including exceptions; read "minimum" vs. "maximum" carefully.
