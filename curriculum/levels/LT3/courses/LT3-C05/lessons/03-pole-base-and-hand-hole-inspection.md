## Why Pole Inspection Matters
A light pole is a tall steel, aluminum, concrete, fiberglass or wood structure with a heavy
luminaire at the top. When poles fail, they fall — onto cars, people and buildings. Poles also
carry energized conductors inside a metal shaft that people touch every day. Every time you
service a pole, you are also its inspector. Report defects promptly; you are often the only
trained person who looks closely at it.

## Parts of a Typical Steel or Aluminum Pole
| Part | Description |
|---|---|
| Concrete base (foundation) | Cast-in-place pier with embedded anchor bolts |
| Anchor bolts | Threaded rods cast into the base |
| Leveling nuts and top nuts | Nuts below and above the base plate hold and level the pole |
| Base plate | Plate welded to the bottom of the pole shaft |
| Base cover | Decorative shroud covering the plate and nuts |
| Hand hole | Access opening near the base with a cover; conductors, fuses, splices, ground terminal |
| Shaft | The pole itself; may be square, round, tapered |
| Arm / tenon / slip fitter | Supports the luminaire(s) at the top |
| Luminaire | The fixture |

Some poles are **direct-embedded** (buried in the ground) — common with wood, fiberglass and some
concrete and steel poles. These rot or corrode at the ground line.

## Inspection Checklist
Start from a distance and work in.

**1. Approach and contact-voltage check**
- Before touching the pole, use a non-contact voltage tester near the pole and hand-hole cover
  (and a meter if your company procedure includes it). A failed splice or conductor touching the
  inside of a pole with a missing ground can energize the whole pole. **Any indication of voltage
  on a pole is an emergency:** keep people away, barricade, and de-energize the circuit before
  investigating.

**2. Overall structure (from a distance and with binoculars)**
- Lean or bend in the shaft; a pole out of plumb may have a failing base or bolts.
- Missing, loose or tilted luminaires and arms; loose access doors on fixtures.
- Damage from vehicles (dents, creases) — a creased steel pole is weakened.
- Vibration: does the pole sway excessively in moderate wind? Excessive vibration fatigues welds.

**3. Base area**
- Missing, loose or corroded anchor nuts; anchor bolts bent or rusted.
- Corrosion of the base plate and shaft where it meets the plate — water and debris collect
  there. Rust-through near the bottom is a serious defect.
- Cracks in welds at the base plate and around the hand hole — fatigue cracks often start there.
- Concrete base cracked, spalled, undermined, or with exposed rebar.
- Grout under the base plate: if present, it should not trap water. Owners and engineers
  differ on grout; report cracked grout or blocked drain holes rather than altering it.
- For direct-embedded poles: decay, insect damage or corrosion at and just below the ground line.

**4. Hand hole**
- Cover present, secured, and gasketed. A missing hand-hole cover leaves energized conductors
  within reach of the public — make it safe immediately.
- Inside (after LOTO and verification): fuse holders, splices, grounding terminal, and
  conductor condition (abrasion against the hand-hole edge, rodent damage, water damage).

## What's Inside the Hand Hole
- **In-line fuse holders:** Each pole's luminaire tap is commonly fused in the hand hole with
  waterproof in-line fuse holders. These let a single fixture fault clear without tripping the
  branch breaker for the entire run. **Breakaway** fuse holders are used on knockdown/breakaway
  poles (near roadways) so the conductors separate cleanly if the pole is struck.
- **Splices:** The branch circuit enters, taps to the luminaire, and leaves for the next pole.
  Splices must be listed for wet locations or direct burial as applicable (e.g., gel-filled or
  heat-shrink kits) — ordinary twist-on connectors and tape are not adequate in a hand hole
  that can be wet.
- **Grounding:** The NEC requires metal poles supporting luminaires to have a handhole (with
  exceptions for some short poles) and a grounding terminal accessible from it, and the pole must
  be bonded to the **equipment grounding conductor** of the circuit. A ground rod at the pole may
  be present or required by specification, but it is supplementary — **the earth is not an
  effective ground-fault current path.** Without an EGC back to the panel, a fault to the pole
  may not trip the breaker and the pole stays energized.

> **Safety:** Before opening the hand hole, check for contact voltage. Then apply LOTO at the
> panel for the circuit feeding the pole, verify absence of voltage on every conductor in the
> hand hole (several circuits may pass through one pole), and only then handle fuses, splices or
> conductors. Watch for wasps, ants, snakes and sharp edges.

## Rating and Reporting Defects
Use a simple severity scale your company agrees on, for example:

| Severity | Example | Action |
|---|---|---|
| **Immediate hazard** | Contact voltage on pole; missing hand-hole cover with exposed conductors; pole leaning badly or cracked through; luminaire hanging | Make safe now (barricade, de-energize), notify customer and supervisor before leaving |
| **Priority** | Loose/missing anchor nuts; significant base corrosion; cracked welds; damaged concrete base | Report same day with photos; recommend engineering evaluation/repair |
| **Routine** | Missing base cover; minor surface rust; faded paint; minor gasket issues | Note on report; schedule repair |

Take **photos** of every defect with a pole ID or location reference. Structural repair or
replacement of poles and foundations is engineered work — your job is to identify and report,
not to judge structural adequacy.

## Key Takeaways
- Treat every pole as a structure that can fall and a metal shell that can become energized.
- Check for contact voltage before touching; voltage on a pole is an emergency.
- Inspect lean, damage, welds, base plate, anchor bolts/nuts, concrete and the hand hole.
- Hand holes contain fuses, wet-rated splices and the grounding terminal; the EGC — not a ground
  rod — provides the fault path.
- Classify, photograph and report defects; make immediate hazards safe before leaving.
