## Why This Matters on Our Jobs

Pole lights and signs often stand right under or beside utility lines, and repairing them means
putting a bucket truck, boom truck, ladder or pole in the air and sometimes digging at the base.
The most common outcome of contact with a power line is **electrocution**, and most of those
injuries are fatal. Shock, fire and falls from the shock are the others.

Applicable standards include 29 CFR 1910.333(c)(3) (unqualified persons and vehicles near
overhead lines), 1926.416(a) (protection from electric power circuits on construction work),
1926 Subpart CC including 1926.1408 (cranes and boom trucks near power lines), 1926.651(b)
(locating underground installations before excavating), and 1926 Subpart V (utility work, which
the utility does, not us). Lifting and rigging near lines is covered in the rigging course.

## Myths Our Training Busts

| Myth | Truth |
|---|---|
| Birds sit on lines, so they are safe to touch | A bird is not a path to ground. You on a truck, ladder or pole are. |
| Lines are insulated | Many are bare. A weatherproof covering keeps water off; it is **not** rated insulation. |
| A fiberglass ladder is safe near lines | Wet or dirty fiberglass can conduct. |
| If I don't touch it, I'm safe | High voltage can **arc** across a gap. |
| Phone and cable lines are harmless | Communication lines can be energized by contact with power lines. Treat every line as energized. |

## Our Company's Overhead Line Rules

From our handbook's sign and pole lighting practices:

1. **Keep aerial equipment at least 10 feet from power lines of 50 kV and under.** That includes
   the bucket, boom, you, your tools and anything you are holding.
2. **Lines above 50 kV must be shielded by the power company before any work under them.**
3. **Never work within 10 feet of lines that have not first been covered ("booted") by the
   power company.** If you cannot keep your margin, stop and call your supervisor.
4. **Even when lines are covered, treat them as if they are not** and stay well clear.
5. **De-energize the sign or pole** whenever possible, and **lock and tag** the circuit (LT1-C06).
6. **Wear your harness, with the lanyard attached** to the aerial equipment, before going aloft.
7. **Protect people below** from falling objects; cone off the work area.

OSHA requires greater distances as voltage rises. For unqualified workers and equipment, the
minimum is 10 feet up to 50 kV, plus 4 inches for every 10 kV above that (1910.333(c)(3)). For
cranes and boom trucks, OSHA's Table A gives:

| Line voltage (AC) | Minimum clearance |
|---|---|
| Up to 50 kV | 10 ft |
| Over 50 to 200 kV | 15 ft |
| Over 200 to 350 kV | 20 ft |
| Over 350 to 500 kV | 25 ft |
| Over 500 to 750 kV | 35 ft |
| Over 750 to 1,000 kV | 45 ft |
| Over 1,000 kV | Set by the utility or a registered engineer |

If you do not know the voltage, **assume the higher distance and call the local utility** to
identify it. Do not guess from the size of the pole.

## Before Work: Examine the Site

Our pre-job site investigation looks at:

- **Every line near the work**: look up at each pole, around signs and **into trees**, where
  foliage can hide lines just as it hides pole lights.
- **Voltages** of lines and equipment, the presence of **induced voltage**, and the location of
  circuits, transformers and grounds.
- **The whole path of the equipment**: where the truck will park, the swing of the boom, and long
  objects (poles, arms, ladders) that will be raised or carried.

Then:

- **Tell everyone at the pre-job briefing** where the lines are.
- **Set a clearance boundary** with cones or portable barriers.
- **Use a spotter** whose only job is watching the clearance and warning the operator. Our training
  says spotters **spot, not guide**: someone holding a tag line or the load can be shocked if it
  contacts a line.
- Reposition the truck if you cannot keep your distance through the whole job.

> **Safety:** Assume every overhead line is energized and dangerous until the utility tells you,
> in person, that it is de-energized and grounded.

## If Contact Happens

- **Stay on the equipment** if there is no immediate danger such as fire. Warn everyone to stay
  away; ground near the truck may be energized.
- If possible, move the equipment away from the line. Have someone **call 911 and the utility**.
- If you **must** get off, **jump clear** with feet together, never touching the truck and ground at
  the same time, then shuffle away with feet close together.
- **Never touch a coworker in contact** with a line or the truck. You become part of the circuit.
- Fallen lines can start fires and stay energized. Keep at least 35 feet away and keep others back.

## Underground Lines

Hitting a buried power, gas or communication line can injure or kill and causes costly repairs.
Driving a ground rod, setting a pole base or anchor, or trenching for a circuit all count as digging.

1. **Call 811** (the one-call center) before you dig. Most states require notice a set number of
   working days ahead (often two). Our training's advice: **describe the area clearly**, and mark the
   dig area with **white paint or flags**.
2. **Wait for marks** or the all-clear from each utility. Colors: **red** electric, **yellow**
   gas/oil, **orange** communications, **blue** water, **green** sewer.
3. **Hand-dig inside the tolerance zone** (commonly 18 to 24 inches on each side of a mark, set by
   state law). As you get close, expose the line by careful hand or vacuum excavation (1926.651(b)).
4. **Remember private lines.** 811 locators mark utility-owned lines. A site's own lighting circuits
   usually need drawings or a private locator (Lesson 4).
5. **Stay away from pad-mount transformers** (the green metal boxes). Never open, lean on or dig
   beside one.

If you strike a line, stop, move away, keep people back and call 911 and the utility. Report it to
your supervisor even if nothing seems damaged.

## Key Takeaways
- Company rule: 10 ft minimum from lines 50 kV and under; higher voltages must be shielded by the
  utility first; uncovered lines within 10 ft means stop.
- Treat every line as energized and uncovered, even communication lines and booted lines.
- Survey the site, brief the crew, set barriers, and use a spotter who spots, not guides.
- After contact: stay on the equipment, warn others, or jump clear and shuffle.
- Call 811 before any digging, wait for marks, hand-dig in the tolerance zone, and avoid pad-mounts.
