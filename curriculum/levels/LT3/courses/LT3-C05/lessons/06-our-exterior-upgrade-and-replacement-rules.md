## Why This Lesson Exists

Lessons 1–5 covered how exterior lighting works and how to service it safely. This lesson
collects **our company's field rules for exterior upgrades and replacements**, gathered from
our monthly safety meetings. Most of them exist because the company paid for a mistake: blown
new fixtures, a second trip, or an argument with a customer about damage we did not cause.

> **Scope varies by state and city.** In some areas a lighting technician may replace exterior and
> pole-mounted fixtures under the company's license; in others it must be done by, or under the
> supervision of, a licensed electrician, and some jobs need a permit and inspection. Before you do
> this on a job, confirm with your supervisor that it is allowed in that location and that you are
> authorized for it. When in doubt, stop and ask.

## Rule 1: Meter First, Every Exterior Project

**Measure the supply voltage with your meter at the start of every exterior project**, before
you install or energize anything. Our company has destroyed many brand-new fixtures by assuming
the voltage. A 120–277 V fixture on a 480 V or 347 V circuit is ruined the instant power comes
on.

- Measure **line-to-neutral and line-to-line** where two hots feed the fixture.
- Compare the reading with the **new fixture's label**, not the old one's.
- A non-contact tester ("sniffer") is a warning device. **You cannot troubleshoot or verify
  voltage with a sniffer.**
- Write the reading on the work order ("277 V L-N, pole 4").

Then lock out and verify absence of voltage before you touch conductors (Lesson 1; LT1-C06).

## Rule 2: Write Up Replacements So the Office Can Order Once

When a pole or area light needs a new fixture rather than a repair, the office needs:

| Item | Why |
|---|---|
| **Measured primary voltage** | Orders the right driver voltage |
| **Location on the property**: property line, building edge or mid-lot | Picks the light distribution (Lesson 1: a property-line pole should not get a Type V head) |
| **Pole shape (round or square)** and size, and the mounting (arm, tenon, slip-fitter) | Picks the mounting kit |
| **Photocell needed?** And how the site is controlled now | Avoids double controls |
| **Photos**: the head, the label, the pole and base, and a wide shot of the area | Confirms all of the above |
| **Count of matching fixtures** on the site | Lets the office quote the whole site, not one head |

## Rule 3: Watch for Emergency Wall Packs

Exterior **emergency (battery) wall packs** look like standard ones from the ground. Check each
label or test button and **count EM units separately** before anything is quoted. On one of our
jobs, 13 of 20 wall packs sold as standard turned out to be emergency units (LT3-C04).

## Rule 4: Disconnect the Redundant Factory Photocell

Many new exterior fixtures ship with a **built-in (factory) photocell**. If the site already
switches the circuit with a contactor, time clock or central photocell, the second control is
redundant and causes trouble: fixtures that stay off when the time clock calls for them, or
that cycle at dusk. **Company rule: disconnect the factory photocell on new exterior fixtures
unless you have been told otherwise**, following the manufacturer's instructions (often a
shorting cap or a lead to cap off), and note it on the work order. If a fixture is the only
control for itself (a stand-alone wall pack with no contactor), leave its photocell in service.

## Rule 5: Test Function, or Say Why You Couldn't

After an exterior upgrade, **prove the fixtures work**: cover the photocell, use the
contactor's hand/override position if the site allows it, or come back after dark if that was
scheduled. If you truly cannot test (daylight, no override, customer refuses), write **"unable
to test"** on the work order with the reason. A blank tells the office nothing and usually
means a callback.

## Rule 6: Report Existing Damage First

Before replacing a pole or a fixture, **look at the base, anchor bolts, hand hole and pole**
(Lesson 3). One of our technicians noticed a damaged pole base during a replacement and reported
it to the site manager and the office **before** the work. That good catch protected the company
from being blamed and protected the next person from a pole that could fall. Photograph and
report damage first, then work.

## Rule 7: Clear Access the Right Way

Overgrown trees and shrubs often hide wall packs and pole heads. **Do not fight branches from a
ladder.** Write it up, take pictures, and come back with the right tool (pole saw or chainsaw,
used only if you are trained on it) and the customer's permission to trim. A ladder against a
tree limb or a wall full of branches is a fall waiting to happen.

Before anyone trims, check three things:

1. **Is there a power line in or near the tree?** Look up into the canopy and along the
   service drop. If any branch you would cut, or any part of you, a tool or the lift, could come
   **within 10 ft of an overhead line**, **stop.** That is line-clearance work. Only qualified
   line-clearance tree trimmers may do it (29 CFR 1910.269(r) and 1910.333(c)(3)). Tell the
   customer to call the utility or a line-clearance contractor, note it on the work order, and
   photograph it. A branch falling onto a line can energize the branch, the ladder and you.
2. **Are you trained and equipped for the saw?** Our company's rule: **only a tech who has been
   trained on our chainsaw uses it.** Wear the PPE in the saw's manual: eye and face protection,
   hearing protection, gloves, **cut-resistant chaps**, sturdy boots, and a hard hat when cutting
   anything above you. Start the saw on the ground with the chain brake on.
3. **Where will the cut fall, and who is under it?** Never run a chainsaw from a ladder or above
   shoulder height, and never cut a limb you are standing on or leaning against. Cone off the drop
   zone, keep the public and your partner out of it, and cut small pieces you can control. Large or
   high limbs are a job for a tree service; write it up.

Take before-and-after photos and note what you trimmed and that the customer approved it.

## HID Calls

Metal halide ballasts in the common wattages are no longer stocked. For a failed HID fixture,
our first choices are an **LED HID-replacement lamp** sized to fit the fixture (check the lamp
envelope, for example a larger BT-shape versus a smaller ED-shape, before choosing) or a **new
fixture**. Some customers do not allow ballast-bypass lamps in exterior fixtures; know the
customer's standard. On color-selectable lamps, set and record the color. The wiring details are
in LT2-C05.

> **Safety:** Exterior work stacks hazards: 277 V or 480 V circuits, height, traffic and weather.
> Meter first, lock out and verify, cone off the work zone, and keep your distance from overhead
> lines (Lesson 5).

## Key Takeaways
- Measure supply voltage at the start of every exterior project; never trust a sniffer.
- Replacement write-ups: measured voltage, location on the property, pole shape, photocell,
  photos and a fixture count.
- Count exterior EM wall packs separately.
- Disconnect redundant factory photocells unless told otherwise.
- Test function after an upgrade, or write "unable to test" with the reason.
- Report pole-base and fixture damage before you replace anything.
- Don't fight branches from a ladder; write it up and come back equipped.
- Branch within 10 ft of an overhead line: stop; that is work for a line-clearance trimmer or the
  utility. Chainsaw only if trained, in chaps and face protection, never from a ladder.
