## Why This Lesson Exists

Lessons 1 to 4 explained how LED drivers work and how to match a replacement. This lesson adds
what our company has learned in the field over the last few years - at our monthly meetings, the
LED agenda items keep coming back to three things: **drivers are getting harder to match**,
**new fixtures are often the better buy**, and **the write-up decides which option the customer
gets.**

## The Market Has Flipped

Two trends have changed how we service LED fixtures:

- **Replacement drivers keep getting more expensive and harder to find.** Exact replacements for
  older fixtures are often discontinued, and whole driver and ballast product lines disappear.
- **New LED fixtures keep getting cheaper**, especially building-mounted fixtures such as wall
  packs, and they come with **warranties of five years or more.** A replacement driver usually has
  a short warranty or none.

> **Example figures only:** a replacement driver might cost about the same as a complete new
> wall pack - but the driver comes with little or no warranty while the new fixture comes with a
> multi-year warranty. Real prices come from the office, never from this lesson.

The result: on many building-mounted fixtures, **a new fixture is the better value for the
customer.** That is the customer's decision, guided by the office - but the office can only offer
it if your write-up includes what is needed to price both options.

## What to Capture When You Write Up a Driver

For the driver (Lesson 3, Step 2):

- **Full label photo and the complete part code.** "24 V driver" or "LED driver" is not a part
  code.
- Input voltage range, output current (or voltage for constant-voltage), wattage, dimming type,
  and **the output connector**. For plug-in drivers and low-voltage supplies, **photograph the
  output tip and the secondary cord cap** - there are many different tips. Best practice is to
  bring back a labeled sample.

For the fixture:

- **Label photo** (make, model, wattage, voltage).
- **A full-elevation photo** showing the fixture, its mounting and what surrounds it.
- **A count of matching fixtures** in the area, and their condition. If several are the same age,
  more failures are coming.
- **Emergency (battery-backup) units.** Exterior wall packs with emergency batteries need
  emergency-rated replacements. On one of our jobs, a quote for new wall packs had to be redone
  because most of the "standard" wall packs turned out to be emergency units. Look for test
  switches, indicator lights and battery labels.
- For pole fixtures: measured primary voltage, location on the property (which decides the
  distribution), pole shape and whether a photocell is needed.

## Programmable Drivers

Our company stocks **wide-range programmable constant-current drivers** (for example, a single
driver covering a wide span of output current and wattage) that are configured from a phone app,
usually through NFC. They let one stocked part replace many fixed-output drivers. To use one
correctly:

1. **Find the original driver's output current** (mA) and output voltage range from its label
   or the fixture data. The new driver's voltage window must cover the LED board's forward
   voltage (Lesson 3).
2. **Program the output current with the driver unpowered**, before installation. On most
   NFC-programmed drivers, the new setting is stored immediately but **does not show or take
   effect until the driver is powered up.**
3. **Mark the programmed current** on the driver label and record it on the work order, so the
   next tech knows the setting.
4. After installing (locked out), restore power and **verify** light output and stability.

> **Safety:** Program and wire drivers with the circuit locked out and verified dead. Never
> program a higher current than the LED board's rating - over-driving shortens LED life and can
> overheat the fixture (Lesson 1).

Programmable drivers and new fixtures overlap in price. Where a whole fixture costs about the same
as a programmable driver and comes with a better warranty, our company may stock fixtures
instead of drivers - another reason your write-ups must include fixture information.

## Diagnose Before You Replace

All of this assumes the driver is actually the problem. Company rules from Lesson 4 and LT2-C06
apply: measure hot to neutral first; **full-brightness strobing** usually means the driver, while
**flicker on a single-diode or single-board fixture** points to the board or fixture; never leave
a fixture strobing.

## Key Takeaways

- Replacement drivers are getting costlier and harder to match; new fixtures often come with five-year or longer warranties.
- Every driver write-up needs the full part code, label photos, the output connector, fixture label, a full-elevation photo and a count of matching fixtures.
- Check exterior wall packs for emergency battery units before anything is quoted.
- Program programmable drivers unpowered, to the original output current, then mark and record the setting.
- Never program more current than the LED board is rated for.
- Confirm the driver is the problem before replacing it.
