## Where You Find LED Signage
LED has replaced neon and fluorescent lamps in most new signs. On the service route you will
find it in:

- **Channel letters** — rows of small LED modules stuck or fastened to the inside of each letter
  can, shining through an acrylic or polycarbonate face (face-lit) or against the wall
  (halo/reverse-lit).
- **Sign cabinets (lightboxes)** — modules or LED strips/bars mounted on the back of the box or on
  rails, lighting a flexible or rigid face from behind or from the edges.
- **Monument and pylon signs**, menu boards, and **LED neon** decorative lighting.
- **Specialty architectural lighting** — tape/strip light in coves, under shelves, in display
  cases, and linear fixtures (covered in Lesson 3).

## How an LED Sign Is Built
A typical LED sign is a simple system:

1. **Line voltage** (120 V or 277 V) comes from a branch circuit through the **sign disconnect**,
   usually controlled by a time clock or photocell.
2. A **Class 2 LED power supply** (techs and the company guide often call it the
   "transformer") converts line voltage to low-voltage DC — most often **12 V or 24 V constant
   voltage**.
3. **Low-voltage leads** run from the power supply to the modules, often through a hole in the
   letter back or raceway.
4. **Modules** are connected in parallel along a pair of wires, like beads on a string. Each
   module has its own small LEDs and current-limiting resistors or regulator.

Because modules are in **parallel**, one failed module normally does not darken the rest — unlike
neon. But a break in the wire or a failed connector darkens every module *beyond* that point on
the string.

## The Components (from the company component list)

| Component | What it does | How it fails |
|---|---|---|
| **LED modules** | Small encapsulated boards with 1–4 (or more) LEDs, a lens and wire leads | Dead LEDs, water damage, discoloration (color shift), dim output, broken leads |
| **Power supply ("transformer")** | Converts line voltage to Class 2 constant voltage DC | No output, low output, shutting down on overload or overheating, water damage |
| **Connectors** | Join module strings to the supply leads and to each other (IDC snap connectors, gel-filled connectors, soldered splices) | Corrosion, loose or partially pierced wires, water entry — **the most common failure** |
| **Wire** | Low-voltage feed wire, usually stranded, sized for the load and distance | Broken conductors, rodent damage, chafing, voltage drop on long runs |
| **Straps / adhesive ("sticky") tape** | Hold modules in place | Adhesive lets go in heat; modules fall and leads pull apart |
| **Flex** | Raceway for line-voltage wiring to the power supply | Loose connectors, damage, water entry |
| **Outdoor/indoor enclosures** | Protect the power supply and connections | Missing covers, failed gaskets, water accumulation |

## Reading the Labels
**Power supply label — record:**
- Input voltage range (e.g. 100–277 V) and input current.
- Output voltage (12 V or 24 V DC) and maximum output current or wattage (e.g. 60 W, 100 W).
- "Class 2" marking and number of outputs.
- Location rating (dry, damp, wet; IP rating), and listing marks (sign power supplies are commonly
  evaluated under UL 48 / UL 1310 / UL 8750 depending on the product).

**Module label or spec sheet — record:**
- Manufacturer and series (modules from different series rarely match exactly).
- Voltage (12 V or 24 V) — **a 12 V module on a 24 V supply will burn out**.
- Watts per module, color (white, red, blue…), color temperature for white (e.g. 6500 K, 7100 K),
  lumens per module, and beam angle.
- Maximum modules per run from one feed point.

> **Safety:** The Class 2 output is low voltage, but the power supply's input terminals, the flex
> and the sign disconnect carry line voltage, often inside the same raceway or cabinet. Lock out the
> sign disconnect and verify absence of voltage (live-dead-live) before opening the power supply
> compartment or handling input wiring.

## Class 2 — Why It Matters
"Class 2" is an NEC category for power-limited circuits. A Class 2 power supply limits its output
voltage and power (roughly 100 VA per output for typical LED sign supplies), so the low-voltage
wiring presents a reduced fire and shock hazard. That is why:

- Large signs use **several** power supplies, or supplies with **multiple Class 2 outputs**,
  rather than one huge supply.
- Two Class 2 outputs must not be paralleled (tied together) to get more power — that defeats the
  power limit.
- Class 2 wiring must be kept separated from line-voltage conductors unless the equipment
  label permits otherwise.

## Key Takeaways
- LED signs are line voltage → Class 2 constant-voltage power supply (12/24 V) → parallel strings
  of modules.
- One bad module usually does not darken others, but a bad wire or connector darkens everything
  beyond it.
- Connectors and wiring fail far more often than power supplies or modules.
- Record power supply and module data before you order or replace anything.
- Line voltage is present at the supply input — lock out before opening it.
