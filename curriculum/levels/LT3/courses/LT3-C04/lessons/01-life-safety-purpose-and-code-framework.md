## Why Emergency Lighting Exists
When normal power fails — a utility outage, a tripped feeder, a fire that damages wiring —
people must still be able to find their way out. Emergency lighting and exit signs are
**life-safety equipment**. They are not convenience lighting, and mistakes on these systems
can contribute to injuries or deaths during an evacuation. Treat every emergency lighting job
with that in mind.

Emergency lighting must:
1. Come on **automatically** when normal lighting power fails.
2. Light the **means of egress** (exit access, exit, and exit discharge) well enough for people
   to move safely.
3. **Last** long enough for evacuation — 1.5 hours under NFPA 101.
4. Identify the exits with **exit signs** that remain illuminated.

## Who Writes the Rules
| Document | What it does for emergency lighting |
|---|---|
| **NFPA 101, Life Safety Code** | *Where* emergency lighting and exit signs are required (by occupancy), *how much* light, *how long*, and *how often to test* |
| **International Building Code / International Fire Code** | Adopted in many jurisdictions instead of, or alongside, NFPA 101; contain similar requirements |
| **NFPA 70, National Electrical Code** — Article 700 (Emergency Systems) | *How to wire and power* emergency systems: sources, transfer equipment, wiring separation, unit equipment, marking, testing |
| Articles 701 and 702 | Legally required standby and optional standby systems — not life-safety emergency systems |
| **OSHA 29 CFR 1910.37** | Workplace exit routes must be adequately lit and exits marked by visible signs |
| **UL 924** | Product safety standard for emergency lighting and power equipment (unit equipment, emergency drivers, exit signs, and emergency lighting control devices) |
| **NFPA 110 / 111** | Performance of generators and stored-energy systems that supply emergency power |

The **Authority Having Jurisdiction (AHJ)** — usually the fire marshal or building official —
decides which code editions apply and enforces them. The edition adopted locally governs, not
necessarily the newest one. Healthcare, assembly and other special occupancies often have
added requirements (for example, NFPA 99 in healthcare).

## How Much Light, and How Long (NFPA 101)
NFPA 101's emergency lighting performance requirements (Section 7.9) are commonly summarized as:

| Requirement | Value |
|---|---|
| Duration | At least **1.5 hours (90 minutes)** after normal power fails |
| Initial illumination along the egress path (floor level) | Average **at least 1 footcandle**, and at least **0.1 footcandle** at any point |
| End of the 1.5 hours | May decline to an average of 0.6 footcandle and 0.06 footcandle minimum |
| Uniformity | Maximum-to-minimum ratio not more than **40 to 1** |
| Transfer | Illumination must be provided within **10 seconds** of loss of normal lighting |

As a technician you won't usually design the layout, but you will **replace and relocate**
fixtures. Moving or removing an emergency head, swapping a battery-equipped fixture for a
standard one, or "temporarily" disconnecting a unit can leave a dark spot on the egress path.
Never reduce emergency lighting without approval from the customer and, where required, the
AHJ.

## Outside the Door: Exterior Egress Lighting
The egress path does not end at the exit door. The **exit discharge** is the part of the route
from the end of the exit (usually the exterior door) to a **public way** or other safe place.
People leaving in a blackout still have to cross a landing, steps, a sidewalk or a parking lot
in the dark, so codes may require emergency illumination outside the building too.

How far that requirement reaches is **an AHJ decision**, and it varies:
- Some jurisdictions want light only at the **exterior landing** right outside each exit door
  (recent IBC editions, for example, call for emergency illumination at the exterior landing of
  exit discharge doors in buildings required to have two or more exits).
- Others want the walkway lit all the way to the street, sidewalk or an approved
  **safe dispersal area**. What counts as the "public way" on a campus, a strip mall or a
  building set far back from the street is often a judgment call by the fire marshal.

What this means for you in the field:

| Situation | What to do |
|---|---|
| Replacing an exterior wall pack or canopy light next to an exit door | Check whether the old fixture had a battery or was fed from an emergency source; the replacement must do the same |
| Exterior battery units and emergency drivers | Use products listed for **wet or damp locations** and rated for the site's **low temperature**; standard batteries lose capacity or will not charge in the cold |
| Exterior egress fixtures on a photocell or time clock | The control must not leave the egress path dark during a night-time outage; use the wiring and listed control devices the manufacturer specifies (Lesson 3) |
| Unsure whether a fixture is part of the egress lighting | Ask the supervisor or customer before removing, relocating or downsizing it; the AHJ has the final say |

Write it up on the work order if you notice an exit door with no exterior light at all, or an
exterior emergency fixture that fails its test. Don't redesign it yourself.

## Ways Emergency Power Is Provided
| Source | Description | Typical sites |
|---|---|---|
| **Unit equipment** (battery packs, "bug-eye" units, emergency drivers in fixtures, combo exit/emergency units) | Each unit has its own battery and charger | Small and mid-size buildings, retrofits |
| **Central battery / central lighting inverter** | One battery system feeds many emergency fixtures over dedicated circuits | Larger buildings, where serviceability matters |
| **Generator with automatic transfer switch** | Generator starts and the emergency branch transfers within 10 seconds | Hospitals, high-rises, large facilities |

## Article 700 Concepts Every LT3 Should Know
- **Emergency circuits are kept independent** from normal wiring — they generally may not share
  raceways, boxes or cabinets with normal circuits, except where the NEC specifically permits
  (for example, transfer equipment, unit equipment and certain luminaires supplied by both
  sources).
- **Boxes and enclosures** of emergency circuits must be permanently marked so they can be
  identified as emergency system components. Don't splice a normal circuit into a box marked
  emergency.
- **Unit equipment** is connected to the branch circuit that serves the normal lighting in the
  area, ahead of any local switches, so the unit senses a loss of that circuit (details in
  Lesson 2).
- **No switches that defeat emergency lighting.** Emergency lighting must not be able to be
  turned off by occupants in a way that leaves it dark during an outage; control devices used
  with emergency lighting must be listed for that purpose (Lesson 3).
- **Testing and records** — the NEC requires periodic testing and a written record; NFPA 101
  specifies the monthly and annual tests (Lesson 4).

> **Safety:** Emergency equipment can be energized from **more than one source**. A unit or
> emergency driver has its own battery; an inverter-fed fixture is powered when the normal
> breaker is off; a generator-fed fixture can be re-energized automatically. Identify every
> source, lock out each one, disconnect batteries per the manufacturer's instructions, and
> verify absence of voltage before working.

## Key Takeaways
- Emergency lighting is life-safety equipment that must light egress paths automatically for
  at least 90 minutes.
- NFPA 101 (or the IBC/IFC) sets where, how much and how often to test; NEC Article 700 sets how
  to wire and power it; UL 924 lists the products; the AHJ decides what applies.
- NFPA 101 baseline: 1 fc average, 0.1 fc minimum, 40:1 max-to-min, within 10 seconds.
- Egress continues outside to the public way; how much exterior emergency lighting is required
  is an AHJ decision, and exterior units must be rated for wet locations and cold.
- Emergency circuits are kept independent, marked, and never defeated by local switches.
- Expect multiple energy sources — LOTO all of them and disconnect batteries.
