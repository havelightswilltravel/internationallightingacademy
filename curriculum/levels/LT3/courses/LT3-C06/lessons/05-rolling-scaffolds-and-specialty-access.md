## When a Scaffold Beats a Lift

A rolling scaffold (mobile scaffold, "baker" or narrow-frame scaffold, tower scaffold) is often the
right choice when:

- The floor cannot carry a scissor lift, or the lift will not fit through the doors or elevator
- Many fixtures are close together and the work takes a long time at each one (retrofit kits,
  cleaning)
- A wide, stable platform is needed for parts, tools and two workers
- The site bans engine or battery equipment, or the floor finish must be protected

It is the wrong choice when it would have to be moved constantly over a long run of fixtures, over
uneven or sloped floors, or when ceiling height is beyond what the manufacturer allows without tying
it to the structure.

## The Rules That Apply

OSHA's construction scaffold rules are in **29 CFR 1926 Subpart L**, and OSHA requires scaffolds
used in general industry (for example routine maintenance) to meet those same requirements. Key
points you must know:

- A **competent person** inspects the scaffold for visible defects before each shift and after
  anything that could affect it.
- Workers who erect, move or use the scaffold must be **trained** in its hazards.
- **Company practice:** we use a scaffold only when a lift or ladder can't do the job safely, and
  only people trained by a competent person erect, move or take one down. LT1 technicians do not
  erect scaffolds. Anyone whose work shows they've lost the skill is retrained.
- Each scaffold and component must support its own weight plus **at least four times** the maximum
  intended load. Never exceed the manufacturer's rated load – people, tools and material together.
- **Fall protection** (guardrails or personal fall arrest) is required above 10 ft. Many
  manufacturers and companies require guardrails on every working level regardless of height – follow
  the stricter rule.
- A supported scaffold whose height exceeds **four times its minimum base width (4:1)** must be
  restrained (tied, guyed or braced) – outriggers widen the base. The manufacturer's instructions may
  be stricter; always follow them.
- Cross braces are **not** a means of access – use the built-in frame ladder, an attached ladder or
  a stair unit.
- Keep the required clearance from overhead power lines (at least 10 ft for typical lines under
  50 kV unless de-energized).

## Assembly Procedure

1. **Read the manufacturer's instructions** and the load/height label. Use only that manufacturer's
   parts – never mix brands.
2. **Check the floor**: level, solid, free of holes, cords, ramps and floor openings. Cone off the
   area in occupied spaces.
3. **Install casters** fully seated and pinned, and **lock every caster** (wheel and swivel lock if
   provided) before climbing.
4. **Level the base** with the adjusting screws (not with blocks or bricks). Keep screw extension
   within the manufacturer's limit.
5. **Install end frames and braces**; confirm every brace lock or pin is engaged.
6. **Install outriggers or stabilizers** when the height-to-base ratio or the manufacturer requires
   them.
7. **Deck the platform fully** with the manufacturer's planks or decks, locked down, with no gaps.
8. **Install guardrails** (top rail and midrail) and **toeboards** on all open sides before working,
   and close any gate or chain at the access point.
9. **Tag the scaffold** per company practice (for example green = ready, red = do not use) after the
   competent person's inspection.

## Pre-Use Inspection Checklist

| Item | Look for |
|---|---|
| Frames and braces | Bends, cracks, rust, bad welds, missing pins or locks |
| Casters | Locks hold, wheels turn freely, stems seated and pinned |
| Platforms/decks | Cracks, warping, missing hooks or locks, overloading |
| Guardrails and toeboards | Present on all open sides, secure, correct height |
| Base | Level, adjusting screws within limits, outriggers installed |
| Surroundings | Floor condition, overhead obstructions, power lines, traffic |

Remove any damaged scaffold from service and report it.

## Moving a Rolling Scaffold

- **Never move it with anyone on it.** Everyone climbs down first – this is our company rule, even
  though OSHA allows riding under narrow conditions.
- Remove or secure tools and material on the platform.
- Check the path for holes, floor openings, cords, slopes and overhead obstacles.
- Push at or near the base, never from the top, and use enough people to control it.
- **Relock every caster** and re-check level before anyone climbs back up.

> **Safety:** A scaffold puts you inside arm's reach of the fixture. Apply LOTO and verify absence
> of voltage (live-dead-live) at the fixture before working, exactly as you would from a lift.
> Metal scaffolds conduct electricity – keep clear of energized parts and overhead lines.

## Specialty Access

| Access tool | Good for | Watch for |
|---|---|---|
| **Push-around vertical mast lift** (one-person) | Offices, retail, 15–25 ft ceilings | It is a MEWP: ANSI A92 training, pre-use inspection, outriggers where required |
| **Telescoping or tower-style lifts** | Narrow aisles, tight rooms | Level floor, outriggers, platform capacity |
| **Compact tracked ("spider") lifts** | Atriums, auditoriums, uneven or stepped floors | Outrigger setup, floor loading, operator familiarization |
| **Ladders over obstacles and in stairwells** | Short tasks above stairs | Use only manufacturer-approved levelers or articulating ladders; never planks across stairs or ladders on boxes |
| **Luminaire lowering (disconnecting) hangers** | High-bay and atrium fixtures | Fixture is lowered to the floor for service; follow the device's instructions |
| **Catwalks and pole lamp changers** | Theaters, arenas, sign and pole work | Fall protection on catwalks; changer tools for lamp swaps only, not wiring |

## Planning Access for a Retrofit Job

Before the bid or kickoff, record for each area: ceiling and fixture height, floor type and load
limit, door and elevator sizes, slopes and stairs, occupancy hours, and obstacles (desks, racks,
pews, machinery). Then pick the access method, count how many units you need and how often they move,
and include setup and move time in the labor estimate. High-bay warehouses usually need scissor or
boom lifts; occupied offices with dense troffers often go faster on rolling scaffolds or
push-around lifts; atriums and stairwells may need a specialty lift or a lowering device.

## Key Takeaways
- Rolling scaffolds fall under OSHA 29 CFR 1926 Subpart L; a competent person inspects before each shift.
- Lock all casters, level with screws, deck fully and install guardrails and toeboards before working.
- Above a 4:1 height-to-base ratio, restrain the scaffold or add outriggers per the manufacturer.
- Never move a scaffold with anyone on it; climb only on the built-in ladder, never the braces.
- Plan access by ceiling height, floor, doors and occupancy, and price it into the job.
