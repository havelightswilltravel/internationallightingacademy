## Overhead Power Lines — the #1 Killer
Contact with overhead power lines (or electrical arcing to the machine) is a leading cause of MEWP
fatalities. Power-line voltages arc across air gaps, so you don't have to touch a line to be
electrocuted. When a MEWP becomes energized, **everyone touching the machine or standing near it
on the ground is at risk** too (touch and step potential).

**Assume every overhead line is energized** until the line's owner (usually the utility) confirms
it is de-energized and grounded. Insulation on overhead conductors (weatherproof coverings) is
**not** rated to protect you. Service drops to buildings and street-lighting circuits count too.

## Minimum Approach Distance
For **unqualified persons** (that includes lighting technicians — you are not qualified line
workers), OSHA requires that you and any conductive object you're handling, including the MEWP,
stay at least:

| Line voltage (to ground) | Minimum clearance (OSHA 1910.333, unqualified persons) |
|---|---|
| 50 kV or less | **10 feet** |
| Over 50 kV | 10 feet **plus 4 inches for every 10 kV over 50 kV** |

ANSI A92.22 gives a similar table of minimum safe approach distances that increase with voltage.
The practical rule: **if you don't know the voltage, don't guess — call the utility, and keep well
beyond 10 feet.** Most distribution lines along streets operate in the range where 10 feet
applies, but transmission lines need much more.

**Plan the work to stay clear:**
1. During the workplace inspection, look up and identify all overhead lines near the work and the
   travel route.
2. If the job can't be done while maintaining clearance, contact the utility **before** the job to
   arrange de-energizing, relocating, or covering the lines (the utility decides what's safe).
3. Set up a visible boundary (flagging, cones) and use a **dedicated spotter** whose only job is
   to watch clearance and warn the operator.
4. Remember the platform and boom **swing and drift** — measure from the closest point the machine
   could reach, not where you intend to stop. Lines also **sag and sway** in heat and wind.
5. Range-limiting devices or insulating links do not replace clearance.

**If contact occurs:**
- **Operator:** stay on the platform if possible; try to move the machine away from the line using
  the controls; warn everyone to stay away. Don't climb down.
- **Ground personnel:** stay away from the machine — at least 35 feet if possible; do not touch the
  MEWP or anyone in contact with it; call 911 and the utility. If you must move away from an
  energized machine, **shuffle with small steps, feet together** to reduce step-potential risk.
- If the machine is on fire and the operator must leave, **jump clear** with feet together, never
  touching the machine and ground at the same time, and shuffle away.

## Ground Conditions and Stability
Tip-overs happen when the ground fails or the machine is on too steep a slope.

| Hazard | What to do |
|---|---|
| Slopes beyond rating | Check decal; level the area or choose a machine rated for the slope (some booms have higher gradeability for travel, but elevating limits differ) |
| Soft soil, mud, recent rain | Avoid, or use manufacturer-approved ground mats/cribbing under outriggers; check with a supervisor |
| Curbs, drop-offs, loading docks | Keep a safe distance; barricade edges; use a spotter |
| Hidden holes, utility vaults, manholes, valve boxes | Inspect; avoid driving over covers not rated for the load |
| Asphalt in hot weather | Outriggers/wheels can sink — use pads |
| Elevated slabs, decks, finished floors | Verify floor load capacity with the owner/engineer |
| Frozen ground | Can thaw during the day and lose bearing capacity |

**Watch for changes during the job.** Rain softens soil, a parked truck blocks your exit, wind
picks up. Re-evaluate whenever conditions change.

## Weather
- **Wind:** Don't operate above the machine's rated wind speed (on the decal; commonly 28 mph /
  12.5 m/s for outdoor-rated machines). Gusts matter. Wind on large loads or signs adds force.
  Indoor-only machines (zero wind rating) stay indoors.
- **Lightning:** Lower and stop work at the first sign of thunderstorms; follow your company's
  lightning policy (commonly 30 minutes after the last thunder before resuming).
- **Ice and rain:** Slippery platforms and reduced braking.

## Rescue Planning
ANSI A92.22 requires a rescue plan for MEWP work. If the operator becomes incapacitated, the
platform fails to lower, or someone is suspended in a harness, help must arrive fast.

**The plan should cover:**
1. **Who on the ground** is trained to operate the **ground controls** and **emergency/auxiliary
   lowering** for this machine (someone must be — don't work alone at height without a rescue
   option).
2. **How to call for help** — phone numbers, site address, gate codes.
3. **Self-rescue** where possible (lowering from the platform).
4. **Suspension trauma:** A worker hanging in a harness can lose consciousness within minutes as
   blood pools in the legs. Rescue must be prompt. Suspension relief straps on harnesses let the
   worker stand in loops to relieve pressure until rescued.
5. **Assisted rescue** by another MEWP or by emergency services for situations beyond the team's
   ability.
6. **Practice** — walk through the plan before the job, especially on a new machine.

> **Safety:** The ground person's job is critical. They must stay at the machine, keep the area
> clear, know how to use the ground controls and emergency lowering, and know how to summon help.
> "Watching" from another part of the site is not a rescue plan.

## Key Takeaways
- Treat all overhead lines as energized; insulation on them won't protect you.
- Unqualified persons: at least 10 ft for lines up to 50 kV, plus 4 in. per 10 kV above 50 kV.
- Use a dedicated spotter, plan for swing and sag, and call the utility early.
- If contact occurs, stay on the machine if you can; ground workers keep away and call for help.
- Check ground conditions, slope, floor load and weather — and re-check as they change.
- Every MEWP job needs a rescue plan and a trained ground person.
