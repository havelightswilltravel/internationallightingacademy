## Working Load Limit
The **working load limit (WLL)**, also called the **rated capacity**, is the most weight a piece of
rigging is allowed to hold in a given way. It is not the breaking strength. Manufacturers build in a
design factor (5 to 1 for synthetic slings, so a sling rated 3,000 lb is designed not to break below
about 15,000 lb), but that margin is there for wear, shock and mistakes you can't see. **Never plan to
use it.**

Rules we live by:
- The rigging is only as strong as its **weakest part**: sling, shackle, hook or lifting machine.
- The WLL on the tag depends on the **hitch**. Read the right column.
- **Shock loading** (a jerk, a drop, a snag that lets go) can multiply the force. Lift and lower
  smoothly.

## The Three Basic Hitches

| Hitch | How it's rigged | Capacity compared with vertical |
|---|---|---|
| **Vertical** | One eye on the hook, load hanging from the other eye | 100% (the base rating) |
| **Choker** | Sling wraps the load and passes through its own eye or a shackle, then cinches tight | About **75–80%** of vertical; less if the choke angle is sharp |
| **Basket** | Sling cradles the load; both eyes on the hook | Up to **200%** of vertical when both legs hang straight up; less as the legs spread |

**For a pole light we use a choker.** It grips the shaft and keeps the sling from sliding. Our shackle
makes the choke point: the sling goes around the pole, the pin goes through one eye, and the other end
of the sling runs through the bow up to the hook. Let the choke form naturally as the load comes on.
Don't force it down tight with a hammer or by hand; that sharpens the choke angle and lowers capacity.

**Example figures only.** Suppose a sling tag reads: vertical 3,200 lb, choker 2,500 lb, basket 6,400
lb. For a pole light in a choker, your limit is **2,500 lb**, not 3,200. Your sling's own tag governs;
these numbers are an example.

## Sling Angles
When two sling legs (a basket, or a two-leg bridle) hold a load, each leg pulls both **up** and
**sideways**. The flatter the legs, the more sideways pull, and the higher the tension in each leg,
even though the load hasn't changed. The angle is measured **between the sling leg and horizontal.**

| Sling angle (from horizontal) | Multiply the share each leg carries by |
|---|---|
| 90° (straight up) | 1.00 |
| 60° | 1.16 |
| 45° | 1.41 |
| 30° | 2.00 |

These factors are simple geometry (1 divided by the sine of the angle). Between two listed angles, use
the **flatter** angle's factor.

**Worked example:** a 600 lb pole is lifted horizontally with a two-leg hitch at 45°. Each leg carries
half the load, 300 lb, times 1.41 = **about 425 lb per leg.** At 30° it would be 600 lb per leg, as
much as the whole pole.

Keep sling angles at **60° or steeper** when you can, and **never below 30°** unless a qualified
person approves (ASME B30.9). Longer slings give steeper angles; that is one reason our company stocks
8 and 10 ft slings as well as 6 ft.

For a pole lifted upright from its bolts with a single choker, the sling hangs vertically, so the
angle factor is 1. Angles matter when you lift the pole horizontal (to load it on a trailer) or use a
basket.

## Center of Gravity
The **center of gravity (CG)** is the balance point of the load. A load hangs with its CG directly
under the hook.
- To lift a pole **upright**, choke it **above its CG**, usually high on the shaft just below the
  arms or tenon, so the pole hangs straight and can't flip. A choke below the CG will let the top
  swing down.
- On a pole with heads still attached, the CG moves toward the top.
- To carry a pole **horizontal**, use two pick points (or a basket) on either side of the CG.

## Estimating Load Weight
You can't pick the right rigging if you don't know the weight. In order of preference:
1. **Manufacturer data:** the pole catalog or spec sheet often lists the weight.
2. **Calculate it:** volume × material weight.
3. **Round up** and add the heads, arms, base plate and anything stuck to the pole.

Typical weights (any engineering reference):

| Material | Approx. weight per cubic foot |
|---|---|
| Steel | 490 lb |
| Aluminum | 165–170 lb |
| Concrete | 145–150 lb |
| Water | 62 lb |

**Worked example (example figures only):** a 25 ft square steel pole, 4 in on a side, 11-gauge wall
(about 0.12 in).
- Steel area of the cross-section: outside square 4 × 4 = 16 sq in, minus the inside 3.76 × 3.76 =
  14.1 sq in, leaves about **1.9 sq in**.
- One foot of pole: 1.9 sq in × 12 in = 23 cubic in. Steel weighs about 0.28 lb per cubic inch
  (490 ÷ 1,728), so about **6.5 lb per foot.**
- 25 ft × 6.5 = about **160 lb** for the shaft.
- Base plate (about 11 × 11 × 1 in, less the opening): about **25 lb.**
- Two heads and arms: check the fixture spec sheets; call it **80 lb.**
- Total about 265 lb. **Plan for 300 lb.**

If a knocked-down pole still has a **chunk of concrete** attached, add it: a 1 ft cube of concrete
weighs about 150 lb. An aluminum pole of the same size weighs about a third as much as steel.

> **Safety:** If you can't estimate the weight with confidence, or the numbers come close to any
> limit, stop and call your supervisor. Never "try it and see" with a load overhead.

## Key Takeaways
- WLL is the most you may lift; it changes with the hitch, and the weakest part limits the lift.
- Pole lights are lifted with a choker, rated at about 75–80% of vertical; read the choker column.
- Flatter sling angles raise leg tension: 1.41× at 45°, 2× at 30°. Stay at 60° or steeper; never
  below 30°.
- Choke above the center of gravity so the pole hangs upright.
- Estimate weight from the spec sheet or volume × density (steel about 490 lb/cu ft), then round up.
