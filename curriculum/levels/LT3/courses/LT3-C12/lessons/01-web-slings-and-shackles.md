## Why a Lighting Tech Needs Rigging
Most of our pole work is done from a lift with the pole standing. But when a pole has been hit by a
vehicle, is leaning, or has snapped at the base plate, we take it down. A 25 ft steel pole with two
heads can weigh several hundred pounds, and it does not fall gently. **Rigging** is how we attach that
load to lifting equipment so it goes up and comes down under control. Our company's kit for this job
is simple: **a web sling and a shackle.**

![Our company's pole-removal rigging kit: a yellow 2-inch by 6-foot twisted-eye polyester web sling and a 3/4-inch screw-pin anchor shackle](/media/rigging/web-sling-and-shackle.jpg)

## The Web Sling
The sling in the photo is a **2 in wide, 6 ft long, eye-and-eye polyester web sling with twisted
eyes.** Our company stocks 6, 8 or 10 ft lengths; pick the length that wraps the pole and still
reaches the hook without a steep angle.

- **Eye-and-eye:** a loop (eye) sewn at each end.
- **Twisted eye:** each eye is turned a quarter turn so it lies flat on a hook or shackle and chokes
  neatly around a pole.
- **Polyester** stretches very little (a few percent) and handles most outdoor conditions. Nylon
  slings stretch more and are weakened by acids; polyester is weakened by strong alkalis.
- **Ply:** slings come in one or two layers of webbing. More plies = more capacity, but **only the
  tag tells you the rating.**

**The tag is the sling's ID.** OSHA requires every synthetic web sling to be marked with the
manufacturer, the rated capacity for each type of hitch, and the material (29 CFR 1910.184(i) and
1926.251(e)). ASME B30.9 adds that **a sling with a missing or unreadable tag is removed from
service.** Color is not a rating; one maker's yellow 2 in sling is not the same as another's.

## The Shackle, or "Clevis"
The steel U in the photo is a **3/4 in screw-pin anchor shackle.** Around our shop it's called a
**"clevis,"** and our company's pole procedure lists a "3/4 in clevis." Both names point at the same
idea, a U with a pin across the open end, but in rigging the correct word is **shackle**, and the
difference matters:

| | Rated lifting shackle | Generic clevis |
|---|---|---|
| Where you see it | Rigging supply houses | Farm, towing and hardware hitches |
| Markings | **Manufacturer and WLL forged into the bow**, size, often grade | Often none |
| OK for lifting? | Yes, up to its WLL | **No**, unless it is rated and marked for lifting |

So when the paperwork says "clevis," **bring a rated, marked shackle.** The one in the photo is
marked with its working load limit (4-3/4 tons for that size), which is far more than a pole light
needs, and that is the point: you want a large margin.

**Parts and types:**
- **Bow** (the rounded body) and **ears** (the two ends with holes).
- **Anchor (bow) shackle:** a rounder bow that accepts loads from several directions, good for a
  sling eye. A **chain (D) shackle** has a straight, narrow body for in-line loads only.
- **Screw pin:** threads into one ear. Fine for short lifts like ours. A **bolt-type** shackle uses a
  bolt, nut and cotter pin and is used where the pin could rotate loose or for long-term rigging.

**Using a screw-pin shackle:**
1. Screw the pin in **all the way**, so its shoulder seats against the ear. Hand-tight, then snug.
2. Load it **in line** through the bow and pin; avoid side-loading.
3. Never replace a lost pin with a bolt. Use only the manufacturer's pin for that shackle.
4. In a choker, place the **pin through the sling eye** and let the **bow bear on the running part**
   of the sling. If the moving webbing rides on the pin it can turn the pin and unscrew it.
5. The web eye should sit flat in the bow. A narrow shackle that bunches a wide sling reduces the
   sling's capacity.

## Inspect Before Every Use
OSHA requires slings and their fittings to be inspected **each day before use** by a competent person
(29 CFR 1910.184(d); 1926.251(a)(6)). Do it every time you rig. Run the sling hand-over-hand along its
full length, both faces and both edges, under good light.

**Remove a web sling from service if you find any of these** (OSHA 1910.184(i)(9); ASME B30.9):
- Missing or unreadable tag
- Acid or caustic burns; melting, charring or weld spatter
- Holes, tears, cuts or snags; a cut edge
- Broken or worn stitching in the load-bearing splices
- Heavy abrasion or fuzzing; webbing that feels stiff or brittle from sun damage
- Knots in the sling
- Red warning yarns showing (on slings that have them)
- Fittings that are pitted, cracked or distorted
- Anything else that makes you doubt it

**Remove a shackle from service if:** the WLL or manufacturer marking is missing or unreadable; the
bow or pin is bent, twisted, stretched or cracked; there is heat damage, deep nicks, gouges or heavy
corrosion; wear has reduced the original size by 10% or more; the pin won't seat fully; or the threads
are damaged (ASME B30.26).

> **Safety:** A damaged sling looks almost normal until it fails. Never "use it one more time." Cut a
> rejected sling in half so nobody can use it again, or tag it "Do Not Use" and give it to your
> supervisor. Rejected shackles go in the scrap bin, not back in the truck.

## Care and Storage
- **Protect slings from sharp edges.** The corners of a square pole and the edge of a base plate can
  cut a loaded sling. Use corner protectors, wear pads or a sleeve.
- Keep polyester and nylon slings away from heat; OSHA limits them to about 180°F.
- Store slings dry and out of the sun, away from battery acid and chemicals. Don't leave them riding
  loose in a truck bed with tools on top.
- Keep the shackle pin in the shackle so it doesn't get lost.

## Key Takeaways
- Our kit is a 2 in twisted-eye polyester web sling (6, 8 or 10 ft) and a 3/4 in screw-pin anchor
  shackle, which our company calls a "clevis."
- Bring only a rated, marked lifting shackle; a hitch clevis is not lifting gear.
- The tag is the rating; no readable tag means no lift.
- Inspect the sling and shackle every time and remove anything on the removal list.
- Seat the pin fully, load in line, and keep the moving sling off the pin.
- Protect slings from sharp edges and heat.
