## What Neon Is and Where You Will Find It
"Neon" is the trade name for **cold-cathode gas-discharge lighting**: a sealed glass tube,
filled with a small amount of gas at low pressure, with a metal electrode sealed into each end.
When a high voltage is applied across the electrodes the gas ionizes and conducts, and the
moving electrons make the gas glow. The electrodes are not heated by a filament (that is why it
is called *cold* cathode), so the tubes can be bent into letters and shapes and can last for
decades.

On a lighting service route you will find neon in:

- **Exposed neon** — open-air tubing mounted on a backer, raceway or building wall, inside
  restaurant windows ("OPEN" signs), on canopies and marquees, and in decorative borders.
- **Channel-letter neon** — tubing hidden inside fabricated metal letters, behind a plastic
  face (face-lit) or shining onto the wall behind the letter (reverse/halo-lit).
- **Cold-cathode architectural lighting** — larger-diameter tubes in coves and soffits in older
  buildings and some high-end interiors.

Many existing neon signs are being retrofitted to LED (Lesson 4), but thousands of neon signs
are still in service and you need to recognize and respect them.

## Gas and Color

| Gas fill | Natural color | How other colors are made |
|---|---|---|
| Neon | Orange-red | Colored (tinted) glass changes the shade, e.g. deeper red |
| Argon with a drop of mercury | Pale blue / UV | Phosphor coating inside the glass produces white, pink, green, gold and many others |

Argon/mercury tubes can be sluggish or dim in very cold weather until they warm up; that is
normal behavior, not always a failure. Pure neon tubes start well in cold weather.

## Why Neon Needs High Voltage
A neon tube behaves very differently from a resistor:

1. **Striking.** To start, the gas needs a high voltage to break down and begin conducting.
   Longer tubes and smaller tube diameters need more voltage.
2. **Running.** Once lit, the tube's resistance drops sharply. Without something to limit
   current, it would draw more and more current and destroy itself.
3. **Current limiting.** The neon transformer or electronic power supply provides both the high
   striking voltage and a limited operating current — typically in the tens of milliamps (for
   example 30 mA or 60 mA ratings).

Because the units are wired **in series**, all of the glass on one transformer carries the same
current. If any one tube breaks, loses its gas, or a connection opens, **the whole series string
goes dark** — like old-style series Christmas lights. That one fact drives most of the
troubleshooting in this course.

## The Components (from the company component list)

| Component | What it does | Common failures |
|---|---|---|
| **Glass units** | The bent tubes, each with an electrode sealed in each end | Cracked or broken glass, leaks (tube goes dim, flickers or turns a different color), "burned" electrodes, blackened ends |
| **Transformer (NST) or electronic power supply** | Steps line voltage (120/277 V) up to the high secondary voltage, limits current | Shorted or open windings, tripped/failed secondary ground-fault protection, overheating, water damage |
| **GTO cable** | High-voltage insulated wire from the transformer to the glass and between units | Insulation breakdown ("burn-through") to metal, cracked jacket, UV damage |
| **Electrode boots ("booties")** | Silicone or rubber covers over each electrode/GTO connection | Cracked, missing or carbon-tracked boots allow arcing and water entry |
| **PK housings** | Insulating sleeves/receptacles that let an electrode pass through a sign face or letter back to the GTO behind it | Cracked housings, water entry, carbon tracking |
| **Glass standoffs (tube supports)** | Hold the glass off the mounting surface | Broken supports let the glass sag, touch metal or crack |
| **Tie wire** | Soft wire (often copper or with an insulated coating) securing glass to supports | Over-tightening cracks the glass; broken ties let glass move |
| **Channels** | The metal letter cans that house channel neon | Water collection if drain holes are blocked, rust, loose mounting |
| **Indoor/outdoor enclosures** | Housings for transformers; outdoor types are rated for wet locations | Water intrusion, corrosion, missing covers |
| **Flex and flex connectors** | Raceway for primary (line-voltage) wiring to the transformer | Loose connectors, damaged flex, lost grounding continuity |

## Reading a Transformer Label
Before you do anything with a neon sign, read the transformer label. Write down:

- **Primary (input) voltage** and current (for example 120 V, 2.5 A).
- **Secondary open-circuit voltage** (for example 9,000 V or 15,000 V).
- **Secondary current** rating (for example 30 mA).
- Whether it is **midpoint grounded** (most magnetic NSTs are — a 15 kV unit puts about 7.5 kV
  between each output lead and ground).
- Whether it has **secondary ground-fault protection (SGFP)** and any test/reset instructions.
- **Listing mark** (neon transformers and power supplies are evaluated under UL 2161) and
  indoor/outdoor rating.

> **Safety:** A neon transformer's secondary voltage is lethal. A typical NST can produce
> thousands to 15,000 volts, and high voltage can jump (arc) through air, cracked boots and
> damaged insulation before you touch anything. Never touch glass, electrodes, boots, PK
> housings or GTO on an energized sign — for any reason.

## Key Takeaways
- Neon is cold-cathode gas-discharge lighting: sealed glass, low-pressure gas, an electrode at
  each end.
- It needs a high voltage to strike and a current-limited supply to run; the NST or electronic
  power supply does both.
- Units on one transformer are in series — one broken tube or open connection darkens the whole
  string.
- Know every component on the company list and what its failure looks like.
- Read and record the transformer label before any work; treat every secondary component as
  lethal high voltage.
