## Chemicals on an Electrical Job

Electricians use more chemicals than most people realize: PVC primer and solvent cement, wire-pulling
lubricant, cutting oil, cleaning solvents, spray paint for marking, firestop sealants, battery
electrolyte, and dusts from cutting concrete, drywall and insulation. OSHA's **Hazard
Communication Standard (HCS), 29 CFR 1910.1200** — which applies to construction through 1926.59 —
gives you the **right to know** about the hazards of chemicals you work with. The HCS is aligned
with the **Globally Harmonized System (GHS)**.

## Employer Responsibilities

- Maintain a **written hazard communication program**.
- Keep a list of hazardous chemicals on site.
- Make **Safety Data Sheets (SDS)** readily accessible to employees on every shift.
- Ensure containers are properly **labeled**.
- **Train** employees on hazards, how to read labels and SDSs, and protective measures.

## Shipped Container Labels

A GHS-compliant label from the manufacturer includes six elements:

| Element | Example |
|---|---|
| **Product identifier** | "PVC Medium-Body Cement" |
| **Signal word** | **"Danger"** (more severe) or **"Warning"** (less severe) |
| **Hazard statements** | "Highly flammable liquid and vapor." "Causes serious eye irritation." |
| **Pictograms** | Red-bordered diamonds with black symbols |
| **Precautionary statements** | "Keep away from heat, sparks and open flames." "Wear eye protection." |
| **Supplier identification** | Manufacturer name, address and phone number |

If you transfer a chemical into a secondary container (like a spray bottle), that container must
be labeled per your employer's program unless it is for your immediate use only.

### Common Pictograms

| Pictogram | Meaning | Job example |
|---|---|---|
| Flame | Flammable | Solvent cement, primer, aerosols |
| Exclamation mark | Irritant, skin sensitizer, narcotic effects | Some pulling lubricants, cleaners |
| Health hazard (person silhouette) | Carcinogen, respiratory sensitizer, organ toxicity | Crystalline silica, some solvents |
| Corrosion | Skin burns, eye damage, corrosive to metals | Battery electrolyte |
| Gas cylinder | Gas under pressure | Compressed gas cylinders, some aerosols |
| Skull and crossbones | Acute toxicity (severe) | Some pesticides, certain chemicals |

## The 16-Section SDS

Every SDS follows the same 16-section format. The sections you'll use most:

| Section | Content |
|---|---|
| 1 | Identification |
| **2** | **Hazard identification** — classification, signal word, pictograms |
| 4 | **First-aid measures** |
| 5 | Fire-fighting measures |
| 7 | Handling and storage |
| **8** | **Exposure controls / personal protection** — exposure limits, ventilation, PPE |
| 11 | Toxicological information |

Sections 12–15 (ecological, disposal, transport, regulatory) are included but not enforced by
OSHA.

## Respirable Crystalline Silica

Concrete, block, brick, mortar and stone contain crystalline silica. Drilling, chipping, chasing,
cutting and grinding them creates **respirable dust** fine enough to reach deep into the lungs.
Silica exposure causes **silicosis** (incurable lung scarring), lung cancer, COPD and kidney
disease.

OSHA's construction silica standard, **29 CFR 1926.1153**, sets:
- A **permissible exposure limit (PEL)** of **50 µg/m³** as an 8-hour time-weighted average, and an
  **action level** of 25 µg/m³.
- **Table 1**, which lists common tasks with specified **engineering controls**, work practices and
  respiratory protection. Employers who fully implement Table 1 controls for a task don't need to
  do exposure monitoring for that task.
- A **written exposure control plan** and a competent person to implement it.
- Housekeeping limits: **no dry sweeping or compressed air** for cleaning silica dust where it
  would contribute to exposure; use wet methods or HEPA vacuums.

### Electrician Tasks in Table 1
- **Handheld and stand-mounted drills** (including impact and rotary hammer drills) — use a
  shroud or cowling with a dust collection system (with a HEPA-filtered vacuum and a filter-cleaning
  mechanism) as specified.
- **Core drilling** — use water delivered to the bit (wet methods).
- **Handheld grinders / chasing for conduit** — use water or a shroud with dust collection; may
  require respiratory protection depending on duration and location.

Read the current Table 1 for exact conditions — some entries require respirators when work is
indoors or exceeds 4 hours per shift.

> **Safety:** Before drilling, coring or saw-cutting into concrete or masonry, confirm there are
> no embedded conductors, post-tension cables or other utilities — use the drawings and scanning
> (GPR) where required. Striking an energized conductor in a slab can be fatal. If existing
> circuits may be present, have them identified, locked out and verified de-energized before
> cutting.

## Other Common Health Hazards

| Hazard | Source | Controls |
|---|---|---|
| Lead | Old paint on steel, demolition | Avoid sanding/torch cutting; follow lead program |
| Asbestos | Old pipe insulation, floor tiles, transite panels, some old wire insulation | Do not disturb suspect material; stop work and report |
| Noise | Hammer drills, saws, powder-actuated tools | Hearing protection |
| Mercury / PCBs | Lamps, old ballasts (lighting-track review) | Universal waste and hazardous waste handling |

## Key Takeaways
- HazCom gives you the right to know: labels, SDSs and training (1910.1200, applied via 1926.59).
- GHS labels have six elements; "Danger" is more severe than "Warning."
- SDSs have 16 sections — Section 2 hazards, 4 first aid, 8 exposure controls/PPE.
- Silica PEL is 50 µg/m³ (8-hr TWA); follow Table 1 controls for drilling, coring and chasing.
- No dry sweeping or compressed air for silica dust — use water or HEPA vacuums.
- Scan and verify before cutting concrete; never disturb suspect asbestos.
