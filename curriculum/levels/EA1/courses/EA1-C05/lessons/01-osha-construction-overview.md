## From General Industry to Construction

Most lighting service work falls under **OSHA 29 CFR 1910** (general industry). As an electrical
apprentice you will spend much of your time on construction sites, which fall under **29 CFR
1926** — the construction standards. Many topics overlap, but the rules and terminology differ in
important ways.

| Subpart (1926) | Topic |
|---|---|
| C | General safety and health provisions (training, competent person inspections) |
| E | Personal protective and lifesaving equipment |
| K | Electrical |
| L | Scaffolds |
| M | Fall protection |
| P | Excavations |
| X | Stairways and ladders |
| Z | Toxic and hazardous substances (including respirable crystalline silica, 1926.1153) |
| AA | Confined spaces in construction |

### The Competent Person
Many 1926 standards require a **competent person**: someone capable of identifying existing and
predictable hazards and who has **authority to take prompt corrective measures** to eliminate them
(1926.32(f)). A competent person inspects excavations, scaffolds and fall protection systems. As an
apprentice you are not usually the competent person, but you must know who it is on your site and
follow their direction.

## The Focus Four

OSHA identifies four hazards responsible for most construction worker deaths:

| Hazard | Electrical-trade examples | Key controls |
|---|---|---|
| **Falls** | Ladders, scaffolds, roof edges, floor openings, MEWPs | Fall protection at 6 ft (1926.501), guardrails, covers, proper ladder use |
| **Struck-by** | Falling tools, swinging loads, vehicles, flying debris from powder-actuated tools | Hard hats, tool lanyards, high-visibility vests, barricades |
| **Caught-in/between** | Trench collapse, rotating equipment (threaders, pipe benders), unguarded machinery | Protective systems in trenches, guards, keeping clothing clear |
| **Electrocution** | Contact with overhead lines, energized circuits, damaged cords, temporary wiring | LOTO, GFCI/AEGCP, clearances, insulated tools |

## Fall Protection Basics (Subpart M)

- In construction, fall protection is generally required at **6 ft** or more above a lower level
  for unprotected sides and edges (1926.501(b)(1)).
- Holes (including skylights) must be covered or guarded; covers must be secured and marked "HOLE"
  or "COVER."
- Personal fall arrest systems must be anchored to an anchorage capable of supporting 5,000 lb per
  employee attached, or designed by a qualified person with a safety factor of two.
- **Ladders (Subpart X):** portable ladders used to access an upper landing must extend at least
  **3 ft above** the landing (1926.1053(b)(1)); non-self-supporting ladders are set at about a
  **4:1** ratio; ladders near energized equipment must have nonconductive side rails.

## Temporary Power on Construction Sites

Construction-site temporary wiring is exposed to water, physical damage and abuse — one of the
most common sources of electrocution.

### OSHA Requirements (1926.404 and 1926.405)
- Employers must protect workers using **either** GFCIs **or** an **assured equipment grounding
  conductor program (AEGCP)**. Under 1926.404(b)(1), all 120 V, single-phase, 15 and 20 A receptacle
  outlets on construction sites that are not part of the permanent wiring and are used by employees
  must have **GFCI** protection when the GFCI option is chosen.
- An **AEGCP** is a written program with a competent person, visual inspections before each day's
  use, and periodic continuity and terminal tests of cords and receptacles, with test records.
- Flexible cords must be the hard- or extra-hard-usage type, protected from damage, and not run
  through doorways or holes without protection. Cords must not be fastened with staples or hung in a
  way that damages them.

### NEC Requirements (Article 590)
NEC Article 590 covers temporary installations. 590.6 requires GFCI protection for receptacle
outlets used by personnel during construction, remodeling, maintenance and similar activities; the
2023 edition expanded the scope to cover more receptacle ratings. Temporary wiring must be removed
promptly when the construction is complete (590.3).

> **Safety:** Inspect every extension cord before use. Remove from service any cord with a
> missing ground pin, cut or abraded jacket, exposed conductors, taped repairs (unless the
> employer's program permits a proper repair), or a damaged plug. Test the GFCI on the
> temporary panel or cord set at the start of each shift. Never daisy-chain cords to reach a
> work area or run cords through standing water.

## Training and Rights

- Employers must train workers to recognize and avoid unsafe conditions (1926.21(b)(2)).
- Workers have the right to a safe workplace, to receive information and training, to report
  injuries and hazards, and to file a complaint with OSHA without retaliation.
- **Stop-work authority:** most contractors give every worker the authority — and expectation — to
  stop work they believe is unsafe. Use it.

## Job Hazard Analysis (JHA)

Before each task, the crew should review a **JHA** (also called JSA or pre-task plan): break the job
into steps, identify the hazards of each step, and list controls. Apply the **hierarchy of
controls**: elimination, substitution, engineering controls, administrative controls, and PPE —
in that order of effectiveness.

## Key Takeaways
- Construction work falls under 29 CFR 1926; know the major subparts.
- A competent person can identify hazards and has authority to correct them.
- Focus Four: falls, struck-by, caught-in/between, electrocution.
- Fall protection starts at 6 ft in construction; ladders extend 3 ft above the landing.
- Temporary receptacles need GFCI protection (or an AEGCP under OSHA); inspect cords daily.
- Use JHAs and the hierarchy of controls; use your stop-work authority.
