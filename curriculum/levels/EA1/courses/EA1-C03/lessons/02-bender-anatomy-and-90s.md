## Know Your Bender

A hand bender (often called a "hickey" by some old-timers, though a true hickey is a different
tool for rigid conduit) is sized for the conduit it bends. Most 1/2 in EMT benders also bend
3/8 in rigid conduit, and most 3/4 in EMT benders also bend 1/2 in rigid — check the markings
cast into the shoe.

| Mark | What it means |
|---|---|
| **Arrow** | Used for stub-ups and as the reference point for offset and saddle marks |
| **Star** (or other back-of-90 symbol) | Marks the back (outside) of a 90° bend — used for back-to-back 90s |
| **Rim notch / teardrop** | Center point used for the center bend of a three-point saddle (on many benders) |
| **Degree marks** (10°, 22½°, 30°, 45°, 60°) | Sight lines on the side of the shoe for checking bend angle while bending |
| **Hook** | Grips the conduit; the stub forms on the hook side of the bender |

Marks vary by manufacturer. Always read the instructions cast or printed on your bender.

## Technique Basics

- Bend on a **firm, flat surface** — concrete, plywood or a bending table. Soft ground lets the
  conduit roll and causes dog-legs.
- Keep **heavy foot pressure on the heel** of the bender. Most bending force should come from your
  foot, not just the handle. Inadequate foot pressure flattens or kinks the tubing.
- Keep the conduit **in the same plane** throughout. Rolling the conduit even slightly between
  bends creates a **dog-leg** — a twist where both bends don't lie flat on the floor.
- Overbend a degree or two to allow for **spring-back**, then check with a level.

> **Safety:** Clear the swing area around the handle before bending, especially with long
> sticks that can whip around and strike someone. Wear safety glasses, gloves and safety-toe
> boots. Use proper body mechanics — keep your back straight and let your leg do the work.

## Take-Up

When you bend a 90, the curve of the bend "uses up" some of the conduit's length. The **take-up**
is the amount you subtract from the desired stub height to find where to put the arrow mark.

| EMT trade size | Typical hand-bender take-up |
|---|---|
| 1/2 in | 5 in |
| 3/4 in | 6 in |
| 1 in | 8 in |
| 1-1/4 in | 11 in |

These are common values for typical hand benders — **verify against your bender's markings or
instructions**, and confirm with a test bend when using an unfamiliar bender.

## Bending a Stub-Up 90

**Stub height** is measured from the end of the conduit to the back (outside) of the 90.

### Worked Example — 12 in Stub in 1/2 in EMT
1. Desired stub: 12 in. Take-up for 1/2 in EMT: 5 in.
2. Mark: 12 − 5 = **7 in** from the end.
3. Place the conduit on the floor with the hook facing the end you want to stub up, and align
   the **arrow** with the 7 in mark.
4. Apply steady foot pressure and pull the handle until the conduit is at 90°. Check with a
   level on the stub.
5. Measure from the floor to the end of the stub: it should read **12 in ± 1/8 in**.

### Worked Example — 3/4 in EMT, 18 in Stub
Mark = 18 − 6 = **12 in** from the end.

## Back-to-Back 90s

A back-to-back 90 is a U shape — two 90s in the same plane, such as conduit that comes down one
wall, crosses a floor and goes up another. The critical dimension is the distance between the
**backs** (outsides) of the two 90s.

### Worked Example — 40 in Back-to-Back
1. Bend the first 90 (for example, a stub-up as above).
2. From the **back** of the first 90, measure along the conduit **40 in** and mark.
3. Align the **star** (back-of-90 mark) with the 40 in mark, with the bender facing so the
   second bend forms on the far side of the mark from the first 90. Orientation is the most
   common back-to-back mistake — if the bender faces the wrong way, the dimension will be off by
   roughly twice the bend radius. Check your bender's instructions before bending.
4. Keep the first stub plumb (pointing straight up) while you bend so the bends stay in one
   plane.
5. Measure outside-to-outside: **40 in ± 1/8 in**.

## Gain and Cut Length

Because a bend follows a curve rather than a sharp corner, a 90° bend needs **less** conduit than
the two straight legs added together. This difference is called **gain**. Bender manufacturers
publish gain tables for each size; you'll use gain when you need to cut a piece to exact length
before bending (for example, a piece with a 90 at each end fitting between two fixed boxes).

Formula: **Developed length = leg 1 + leg 2 − gain** (for each 90 in the piece).

## Common Problems

| Problem | Likely cause | Prevention |
|---|---|---|
| Stub too long/short | Wrong take-up or measuring to the wrong point | Confirm take-up; measure to the back of the bend |
| Dog-leg | Conduit rolled between bends | Keep first stub plumb; bend on a flat surface |
| Kinked or flattened | Not enough foot pressure; bending too fast | Heavy heel pressure; smooth, steady pull |
| Bend over 90° | Overbending | Check with level often; small corrections can be made with the bender reversed or by hand on short stubs |

A kinked or flattened bend reduces the internal diameter and violates 358.24. Cut it out and
start over — don't install it.

## Key Takeaways
- Know your bender's arrow, star, rim notch and degree marks; they vary by brand.
- Stub mark = desired stub height − take-up (typically 5 in for 1/2 in EMT, 6 in for 3/4 in).
- Back-to-back 90s are measured outside-to-outside, using the star mark.
- Keep the conduit in one plane to avoid dog-legs; use heavy foot pressure.
- Gain is the length saved by a bend's curve — use the manufacturer's table.
- Never install a kinked or flattened bend.
