## What the NEC Is — and Isn't

The **National Electrical Code (NEC)** is published by the National Fire Protection Association
as **NFPA 70**. It is revised on a three-year cycle (2017, 2020, 2023, 2026…) through a public
process in which anyone can submit proposed changes, and code-making panels of industry experts
vote on them.

Article 90 explains the Code's purpose in its opening section: the **practical safeguarding of
persons and property** from hazards arising from the use of electricity. Article 90 also makes
two points apprentices often miss:

- Compliance with the Code results in an installation that is **essentially free from hazard**,
  but **not necessarily efficient, convenient or adequate for future expansion**. The NEC is a
  minimum safety standard, not a design guide.
- The Code is **not a design specification** and **not an instruction manual for untrained
  persons**. It assumes the reader is trained in the trade.

That's why a job's plans and specifications frequently require more than the NEC does — larger
conductors for voltage drop, more receptacles, dedicated circuits, specific raceway types. You
must meet the Code *and* the project documents.

## The NEC Is Not Law Until It Is Adopted

NFPA writes the NEC, but NFPA cannot enforce it. The Code becomes a legal requirement only when a
**governmental body adopts it** — a state, county or city — usually by reference in a statute or
ordinance. Adoption varies widely:

| Situation | What it means for you |
|---|---|
| State adopts the latest edition statewide | Use that edition everywhere in the state, plus any state amendments |
| State adopts an older edition | You may legally install to (for example) the 2020 or even 2017 NEC |
| State adopts, cities may amend | A city can add stricter local rules (e.g., no NM cable in commercial buildings) |
| No statewide adoption | Each local jurisdiction decides which edition, if any, applies |

**This program teaches from the 2023 NEC unless noted.** The edition adopted by your local
jurisdiction — with its amendments — is what actually governs your work. Before starting any
job, find out which edition and amendments apply there.

> **Note on editions:** The 2026 NEC has been published and is being adopted by some
> jurisdictions. It reorganized some content (moving and renumbering certain articles and
> sections), so a section number from the 2023 edition may not match the 2026 book. When you
> look something up, always confirm you are using the edition your AHJ enforces.

## The Authority Having Jurisdiction (AHJ)

The **Authority Having Jurisdiction** is the organization, office or individual responsible for
enforcing the Code and approving equipment, materials, installations and procedures. In practice
the AHJ is usually the **electrical inspector** or building official, but it can also be a fire
marshal, an insurance inspector, a facility's own engineering department, or a federal agency on
federal property.

Under Article 90, the AHJ:
- **Interprets** the Code's rules,
- **Approves** equipment and materials (the Article 100 definition of *approved* is "acceptable to
  the AHJ"),
- May grant **special permission** (written consent) for alternative methods, and
- May **waive** specific requirements or permit alternative methods where equivalent safety is
  achieved.

If you and the inspector disagree about an interpretation, the professional move is to ask the
inspector to show you the section they are citing, read it together, and — if a disagreement
remains — let your supervisor or contractor follow the jurisdiction's appeal process. Arguing on
site rarely helps and can damage the company's relationship with the inspection office.

## Permits and Inspections

Most jurisdictions require a **permit** before electrical work begins, and licensing laws
determine who may pull it (usually a licensed contractor or master electrician). Typical
inspections:

1. **Underground / slab** — before trenches are backfilled or concrete is poured.
2. **Rough-in** — after boxes, raceways and cables are installed but before walls are covered.
3. **Service** — before the utility energizes a new service.
4. **Final** — after devices, luminaires and equipment are installed and labeled.

Covering work before it is inspected can mean tearing out drywall at the contractor's expense.
As an apprentice, never cover or backfill work unless your supervisor confirms the inspection has
passed.

## Listing, Labeling and Testing Laboratories

Inspectors rely heavily on product **listing**. A product is *listed* when it appears on a list
published by an organization acceptable to the AHJ — a **Nationally Recognized Testing Laboratory
(NRTL)** such as UL, Intertek (ETL) or CSA — indicating it meets appropriate standards. *Labeled*
equipment carries that organization's mark.

NEC 110.3(B) requires listed or labeled equipment to be installed and used according to any
instructions included in the listing or labeling. That makes the **manufacturer's installation
instructions part of the Code requirement** — ignoring them is a violation, not just bad
practice.

## Other Codes You Will Work Alongside

| Document | What it covers |
|---|---|
| NFPA 70E | Electrical safety in the workplace — how workers stay safe (EA3 covers it) |
| OSHA 29 CFR 1910 / 1926 | Federal workplace safety law (general industry / construction) |
| International Building/Residential Codes (IBC/IRC) | Building construction; the IRC includes electrical chapters based on the NEC |
| NFPA 72 | Fire alarm and signaling systems |
| NFPA 101 | Life Safety Code (egress, emergency lighting) |
| Energy codes (IECC, ASHRAE 90.1) | Lighting power, controls, efficiency |

## Key Takeaways
- The NEC (NFPA 70) is a minimum safety standard revised every three years — not a design manual.
- It has legal force only where a government adopts it; adopted editions and amendments vary.
- This program uses the 2023 NEC; the AHJ-adopted edition always governs, and the 2026 edition
  reorganized some content.
- The AHJ interprets the Code and approves equipment and installations.
- Listed equipment must be installed per its listing instructions (110.3(B)).
- Never cover work before it passes inspection.
