## Why Optics Matter to a Technician

Two luminaires with the same LED board can put very different amounts of useful light on the floor.
The **optic** – reflector, lens, diffuser or louver – decides where the light goes, how much is lost
inside the housing and how much glare people see. When you recommend a retrofit kit, swap a lens,
or describe a fixture on a scope of work, you need the vocabulary and the judgment covered here.
The manufacturer's photometric file (IES/LM-63 format) and report are always the source of truth.

## The Three Ways an Optic Controls Light

| Principle | What happens | Where you see it |
|---|---|---|
| **Reflection** | Light bounces off a surface. **Specular** (mirror-like) surfaces aim light precisely; **spread** (semi-specular, hammered) surfaces soften it; **diffuse** (matte white) surfaces scatter it evenly | Polished parabolic louvers, high-bay reflectors, white-painted troffer housings |
| **Refraction** | Light bends as it passes through clear material at an angle. Prisms and shaped lenses redirect it | Prismatic acrylic lenses, LED TIR (total internal reflection) lenses, roadway optics |
| **Diffusion (transmission)** | Light passes through a translucent material and scatters in all directions | Opal/frosted lenses, volumetric troffers, linear diffusers |

Every pass through or bounce off a surface loses some light. Diffusers trade efficiency for low
brightness and a soft look; specular optics are efficient and controlled but can create bright
images and harsh contrast.

## Common Optical Components

- **Reflectors** – collect light that would otherwise go sideways or up and send it toward the task.
  Retrofit reflector inserts were common in the fluorescent era to remove lamps; they narrow
  distribution and can create dark walls.
- **Lenses** – prismatic lenses control glare and spread light; clear lenses protect the source.
- **Diffusers** – hide the source and individual LED "dots," at a cost of transmission loss.
- **Louvers** – grids or blades that block direct view of the source at high angles, cutting glare
  on screens. Deep parabolic louvers can leave upper walls dark.
- **LED optics** – each LED or LED cluster often has its own small lens or TIR optic, letting the
  manufacturer produce narrow, wide or asymmetric beams from the same board. Swapping optics changes
  the distribution completely, so match the catalog suffix exactly when ordering.

## Classifying Distribution

### Indoor: share of light going up vs down

| Class | Approximate downlight share | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Direct | 90–100% down | Troffers, downlights, most high-bays |
| Semi-direct | 60–90% down | Pendants with a small uplight slot |
| General diffuse / direct-indirect | 40–60% down | Suspended linear with up and down components |
| Semi-indirect | 10–40% down | Pendants aimed mostly at the ceiling |
| Indirect | 0–10% down | Cove lighting, uplight pendants |

Distribution can also be **symmetric** (same pattern all around) or **asymmetric** (wall washers,
shelf lighting in retail, aisle optics in warehouses).

### Outdoor: IES types and BUG

Area and roadway luminaires are described by **IES lateral distribution types** – Type I and II
(narrow, along a path or roadway), Type III (wider, forward throw, often for lot edges), Type IV
(strong forward throw, building or perimeter mounting) and Type V/VS (round or square pattern,
center of a lot). The **BUG rating** (lesson 03) adds how much light goes behind the pole,
upward into the sky and toward glare angles. Lower B and U values reduce light trespass and
skyglow – many ordinances and energy codes limit them, and replacing a fixture with a
different type can put light into a neighbor's windows.

### Describing a fixture on a scope

Use a consistent shorthand: **mounting** (recessed, surface, pendant, pole, wall, track), **size and
shape** (2×4, 6 in round, 4 ft linear), **optic** (prismatic, opal, parabolic, Type III), **source
and output** (LED, 4,000 lm, 4000K), **controls** (0–10 V, sensor) and **listing** (damp, wet).
For example: "2×4 recessed troffer, prismatic lens, LED 4,000 lm 3500K, 0–10 V, damp location."

## Efficiency, Efficacy and CU – Three Different Things

| Term | Definition | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| **Source efficacy** | Lumens from the lamp or LED ÷ watts | A bare LED package can exceed 180 lm/W |
| **Luminaire efficiency** | Lumens leaving the luminaire ÷ lumens from its lamps (%) | The legacy measure for fluorescent and HID fixtures (relative photometry) |
| **Luminaire efficacy** | Lumens leaving the luminaire ÷ watts drawn by the whole luminaire (lm/W) | **The metric to use now** (absolute photometry, IES LM-79 testing) |
| **CU** | Fraction of luminaire lumens reaching the work plane in a particular room | Depends on the optic **and** the room (lesson 05) |

With integrated LED products the LEDs cannot be separated from the housing, so manufacturers test
the complete luminaire and report delivered lumens and input watts. That makes **luminaire
efficacy** the fair comparison: it includes driver losses, heat and optical losses. A
high-efficacy LED board in a poor optic, or a lamp in a deep reflector that traps heat, can deliver
fewer useful lumens per watt than a lower-rated source in a well-designed housing. The same applies
to TLEDs and kits: judge them by tested luminaire output in the actual housing, not by the
lamp-only rating on the box.

## Environmental and Impact Ratings

| Rating | What it tells you |
|---|---|
| **Damp location** (UL listing) | Suitable for moderate moisture – covered porches, some cold rooms, bathrooms outside the shower |
| **Wet location** (UL listing) | Suitable where water can contact the fixture – exposed exterior, wash-down areas, shower zones where permitted |
| **IP rating** (IEC 60529) | First digit = protection against solids/dust (6 = dust-tight); second digit = water (5 = water jets, 6 = powerful jets, 7 = temporary immersion). Example: IP66 |
| **IK rating** (IEC 62262) | Resistance to impact, IK00–IK10; IK10 withstands the highest test energy – used in gyms, parking garages and vandal-prone areas |

The NEC defines damp and wet locations, and the luminaire's **listing** is what the inspector
checks. An IP number alone does not replace a UL wet-location listing. When you replace a fixture
or lens, keep the same or better location rating and gaskets intact.

> **Safety:** Removing lenses, louvers or optics to inspect or replace them means opening the
> luminaire. Apply LOTO and verify absence of voltage (live-dead-live) first, protect your eyes
> from glass or acrylic fragments, and never look directly into high-output LED arrays when
> testing.

## Key Takeaways
- Optics control light by reflection, refraction and diffusion; every surface costs some light.
- Describe distribution as direct to indirect indoors, and by IES Type and BUG outdoors.
- Luminaire efficacy (delivered lm ÷ input W) is the fair comparison today; source efficacy alone can mislead.
- A better source in a poor optic can lose – judge kits by tested output in the real housing.
- Match damp/wet listing, IP and IK ratings to the location, and rely on manufacturer photometric files.
