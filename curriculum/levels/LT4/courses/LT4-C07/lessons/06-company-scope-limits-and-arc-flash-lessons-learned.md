## Why This Lesson Exists

Lessons 1–5 covered NFPA 70E and our energized work permit. This lesson adds **where our company
draws the line** on electrical work and corrects several statements in older company safety
slides that no longer match NFPA 70E. When an old slide and this lesson disagree, **this lesson
applies**.

## Who Works on What

| Role | Scope under company policy |
|---|---|
| Lighting technician (LT1–LT3) | **Inside the lighting fixture.** Anything outside it needs supervisor or management approval. |
| Qualified technician / electrician (LT4–LT5) | Panels, switchgear and cabinets **480 V and below** with an incident energy of **4 cal/cm² or less**, unless management has deemed the person qualified for more |
| Nobody at our company | **Medium-voltage equipment** (over 1,000 V). We do not have the PPE, training or scope for it. |

A safety meeting once showed a video of a worker at medium-voltage switchgear. The company's
message was blunt: **we do not have the protection for this, so never put yourself in that
position.** If lighting you need is fed from equipment outside your scope, stop, photograph the
label and call your supervisor. Which breakers and disconnects lighting technicians may operate
is listed in LT2-C11 Lesson 5 (company policy, pending confirmation); qualified technicians
should know that list so they can back it up on site.

## Hot Work Rules From Our Manual

- A **written safety plan approved by a supervisor** (our Energized Electrical Work Permit, Lesson
  5) before any panel is worked hot.
- **Never work alone** on energized panels. The manual's threshold is above 2 cal/cm²; this
  program applies it to all energized panel work.
- **Insulated tools** on live circuits.
- **Bus rooms:** never enter or work alone, keep doors closed, no tools or materials carried above
  the waist, and nothing worked on unless de-energized and grounded (Lesson 5).

## Correcting the Old Slides

| Older company statement | Current requirement |
|---|---|
| "Coveralls are the last line of defense." | **True**, and it is why the first choice is always to eliminate the exposure: de-energize, or keep the door closed and the work outside the arc-flash boundary (NFPA 70E hierarchy of risk controls, Lesson 1). |
| "Even under 4.4 cal/cm², coveralls offer some protection," and "add up the calorie exposure." | Arc-rated clothing must be rated **at or above the incident energy** on the label or study, or meet the **PPE category** minimum (Category 1 = 4 cal/cm², Category 2 = 8 cal/cm²). Ratings of layered garments are **not added**; only a tested layered system has a combined rating (NFPA 70E 130.5, 130.7). |
| "FR clothing in panels that are 4 cal/cm²." | **Arc-rated (AR)** clothing, rated for the incident energy, whenever you are inside the arc-flash boundary (from 1.2 cal/cm²). |
| "Electricians carry a Category 1 suit without a face shield." | **PPE Category 1 includes an arc-rated face shield or arc-flash suit hood.** Category 2 requires an AR face shield **and** AR balaclava, or a hood. A kit without its face protection is not that category (NFPA 70E Table 130.7(C)(15)(c)). |
| "Use the Cat 2 suit with face shield where arc-flash risk is high." | Correct as far as it goes: wear the **whole** kit, with safety glasses, hearing protection, a Class E hard hat, and rubber insulating gloves with leather protectors. If the label is above 8 cal/cm², Category 2 is not enough. |
| "UL-approved insulated gloves over 300 V." | **Rubber insulating gloves made to ASTM D120**, in the voltage class for the job (Class 00 or 0 for building systems to 480 V), with leather protectors, **air-tested before each use and electrically retested at least every 6 months** (29 CFR 1910.137). Required whenever the risk assessment shows **shock exposure** (50 V and up inside the restricted approach boundary), not only above 300 V. |

> **Safety:** Before you open any cover, read the arc-flash label. If the incident energy is above
> 4 cal/cm², above what your issued PPE covers, or the equipment has no label and is large enough
> to need one, the job is outside your scope until management says otherwise.

## Lesson Learned: Arc Flash Next Door

On one of our job sites, a **utility lineworker** was hospitalized after an arc-flash explosion
in the utility's equipment while our crew was working nearby. Our crew was not involved, but they
were close enough to see it. Three lessons:

1. **Arc flash is not just a panel hazard.** Pad-mount transformers, meter sockets and service
   equipment carry far more fault energy than a lighting panel.
2. **Distance is protection.** Stay outside the arc-flash boundary of equipment you are not working
   on, and keep your crew and the public away when the utility is working.
3. **Experience does not prevent arc flash.** Professionals with the best training get hurt when
   equipment fails. The answer is a de-energized, verified, locked-out condition whenever possible.

## Key Takeaways
- Lighting technicians work inside the fixture; electricians to 480 V and 4 cal/cm² unless
  management qualifies them for more; nobody here works on medium voltage.
- Written, supervisor-approved plan for any hot panel work; never alone; insulated tools.
- AR clothing must be rated at or above the incident energy; ratings are not added up.
- PPE Category 1 and 2 kits include arc-rated face protection; no face shield, no category.
- Rubber insulating gloves are ASTM D120, class-rated, with protectors, tested per 1910.137,
  whenever there is shock exposure.
