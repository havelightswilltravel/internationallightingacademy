## Our Company's Starting Point

Lesson 3 covered the NFPA 70E rules. Our company's handbook says it in field language:

- **Whenever working on a lighting system, the power source is disconnected first.**
- **Only trained, qualified and authorized personnel** work in or near live circuits, and never
  on a task they feel is unsafe or have not been trained for.
- **If at all feasible, no work is done until the equipment is de-energized.**
- **All panels to be worked hot must have a written safety plan**, and a **supervisor must
  approve** that plan, before work starts. The company's written plan is the Energized
  Electrical Work Permit described below.
- **Never work hot panels alone.** Our handbook does not allow working alone on panels above
  2 cal/cm²; this program applies the rule to all energized panel work: a second qualified person
  or a trained attendant is present.
- **Arc-rated clothing.** Our older handbook required flame-resistant clothing at 4 cal/cm². The
  current rule (NFPA 70E 130.5 and 130.7) is stricter and is the one we follow: arc-rated
  clothing and face protection rated at or above the incident energy are required whenever work
  is inside the arc-flash boundary, which starts at 1.2 cal/cm².
- **Use insulated tools** and rated rubber insulating gloves with leather protectors, insulating
  mats or blankets, and barriers for the voltage involved.
  Our older handbook called for rubber gloves above 300 V; the current rule is rated gloves for
  any contact risk with exposed energized parts of 50 V or more inside the restricted approach
  boundary (NFPA 70E 130.7; 29 CFR 1910.137).

### Bus and Bus-Room Rules
Our handbook adds rules for bus rooms and switchgear: do not enter or work in a bus room
alone; do not leave bus room doors open; do not carry tools or materials above your waist
(they can fall into live bus); and do not work on any bus, bus structure, cable or disconnect
switch unless it is de-energized and grounded.

> **Scope varies by state and city.** In some areas a lighting technician may perform energized
> testing and troubleshooting under the company's license; in others it must be done by, or under
> the supervision of, a licensed electrician, and some jobs need a permit and inspection. Before you
> do this on a job, confirm with your supervisor that it is allowed in that location and that you
> are authorized for it. When in doubt, stop and ask.

### When Our Work Might Be Justified Energized
The handbook gives two examples: (1) an occupied building during business hours where the
lighting is needed for the safety of the public and employees, and de-energizing would create
additional hazards or is unfeasible; and (2) troubleshooting, when the system must be energized
to find the defective part (ballast, driver, capacitor, igniter, socket, wire or lamp).

OSHA does list removal of illumination from an area as an example of a greater hazard
(29 CFR 1910.333(a)(1)), but the honest first questions are: **Can we light the area with
temporary lighting? Can we kill only the fixtures we are working on? Can we do it after hours?**
If yes, the work is not justified energized. Troubleshooting with a meter is permit-exempt
(Lesson 3) but still requires the shock and arc-flash PPE.

> **Company stance; your company decides its own.** Our company allows qualified, authorized
> technicians to work on energized lighting during business hours when shutting the lighting off
> would itself create a hazard, for example darkening an occupied store or office. This rests on
> OSHA 29 CFR 1910.333(a)(1), which lists "removal of illumination for an area" as an example of an
> increased hazard, and NFPA 70E 130.2(A)(1), which allows energized work when de-energizing
> introduces additional hazards or increased risk. Other companies using this program must decide
> their own stance with their safety professional and document it. Either way, NFPA 70E still
> requires a risk assessment, the right PPE, and an energized electrical work permit
> (130.2(B)) unless an exemption applies.

> **Safety:** Our older handbook describes cutting the hot and neutral leads inside an
> energized fixture and fitting an in-line disconnect. That is energized work within the
> restricted approach boundary. It is done only by a qualified person, with justification, rated
> gloves and protectors, insulated tools, and this permit. It is never taught as a convenience
> method, and technicians who are not qualified and authorized for the task do not do it.

## The Energized Electrical Work Permit

Our permit has two parts: the **Energized Electrical Worksheet** (justification, analysis and
approvals) and the **Energized Work Checklist** (the day-of record). Both are completed before
any energized work starts.

### Part I: Completed by the Requester
After the header (job or work order number and date), the person asking for the work, often the
project supervisor, fills in:

1. **Description of the circuit, equipment and job location.** Panel name, circuit numbers,
   voltage, room or area.
2. **Description of the work to be done.**
3. **Justification** for why the circuit or equipment **cannot be de-energized or the work
   deferred until the next scheduled outage.** "The customer prefers it" or "it is faster" is
   not a justification.

The requester signs with title and date.

### Part II: Completed by the Qualified Persons Doing the Work
The qualified worker checks off each item as it is completed:

1. **Detailed job procedure**: the steps, in order.
2. **Safe work practices**: insulated tools, one-hand technique, covering adjacent live parts
   with blankets, attendant, escape path.
3. **Results of the shock risk assessment**: voltage and exposure. (Our form says "shock hazard
   analysis," the older NFPA 70E term.)
4. **Shock protection boundaries**: limited and restricted approach distances.
5. **Results of the arc-flash risk assessment**: incident energy at the working distance, or the
   PPE category. (Our form says "flash hazard analysis.")
6. **Arc-flash boundary**. (Our form says "flash protection boundary.")
7. **PPE** needed for the task, for both shock and arc flash.
8. **Means used to restrict access** of unqualified persons: barricades, tape, signs, attendant.
9. **Evidence of a job briefing**, including discussion of job-specific hazards.
10. **Do you agree the work can be done safely?** If the answer is **NO, the permit goes back to
    the requester.** The qualified worker always has the right to say no.

The qualified person signs and dates.

### Part III: Facility Approvals
Signatures from the **manager**, the **safety or general manager**, an **electrically
knowledgeable person**, and the **supervisor**. No signatures, no energized work.

### The Energized Work Checklist (Day-of Record)
Reviewed by the foreman before work starts. **It is valid only for the named workers and the day
issued.** It records: date and start time; project; building location; panel location;
description of the work; the specific reason the equipment cannot be de-energized; **maximum
voltage present**; **number of voltage sources present**; the PPE worn (hard hat, insulated
gloves, insulated sleeves, safety glasses, face shield or hood, arc-rated jacket and bibs,
insulating mats and blankets, hearing protection); who authorized the work and their title; the
owner or customer representative; and the time work was completed.

## Worked Example

*Example values only.* A qualified technician is asked to add a 20 A breaker to an energized
480Y/277 V lighting panel in a facility that runs 24 hours a day.

- **Part I:** The panel also feeds lighting over active work areas. Asked whether a planned
  shutdown is possible, the facility schedules a weekend outage. **The permit is returned and the
  work is done de-energized**, the most common outcome.
- Had no outage been possible and the justification been approved, **Part II** would record:
  480 V; limited approach 3 ft 6 in, restricted 1 ft 0 in; the label's incident energy and
  arc-flash boundary; rated gloves with protectors and insulated tools; arc-rated clothing and
  face protection at or above the label value; barricades and an attendant; a job briefing. The
  **checklist** is then completed that morning and closed out with the completion time.

> **Safety:** If conditions change during the work, such as a second source you did not expect
> or damaged bus, stop, back out and reassess. The permit covers only the work and conditions it
> describes.

## Key Takeaways
- Company policy: disconnect first; only trained, qualified, authorized people work live, never alone.
- Hot panel work requires a written safety plan (the permit) approved by a supervisor before starting.
- Part I justifies why it cannot be de-energized or deferred; Part II records the ten checks; Part
  III collects four approval signatures; the checklist is valid only for the named workers and day.
- Our form's "flash hazard analysis" means today's arc-flash risk assessment.
- A NO from the qualified worker sends the permit back to the requester.
