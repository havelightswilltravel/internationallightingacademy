## Why a Lighting Tech Needs to Know Energy Codes

Electrical codes (the NEC) keep installations safe. **Energy codes** limit how much energy a
building may use. For lighting, energy codes set two kinds of rules:

1. **How much lighting power** may be installed (lighting power density, LPD).
2. **What controls** must be installed and how they must work (mandatory controls).

When you replace fixtures, add controls or change circuits, the work may "trigger" energy-code
requirements. A senior technician should recognize those triggers in the field, because a retrofit
that skips required controls can fail inspection, lose utility incentives and leave the customer
out of compliance.

## The Main Codes

| Code | Publisher | Where it applies | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| **ANSI/ASHRAE/IES Standard 90.1** – Energy Standard for Buildings Except Low-Rise Residential | ASHRAE, co-sponsored by IES | Commercial buildings; referenced by the IECC and federal policy | Updated on a three-year cycle (2016, 2019, 2022 …). Section 9 covers lighting |
| **International Energy Conservation Code (IECC)** | International Code Council | Adopted by many states and cities | Commercial provisions allow either the IECC commercial chapter or ASHRAE 90.1 as the compliance path. Lighting is in Section C405 |
| **California Title 24, Part 6** – Building Energy Efficiency Standards | California Energy Commission | California only | Among the most stringent; requires field **acceptance testing** of lighting controls by certified acceptance test technicians |
| Other state codes | Various | e.g. Washington, Oregon, Massachusetts amendments | Often stricter than the model codes |

> **Note:** Which edition applies depends on what your state or city has **adopted**, and when. The
> AHJ-adopted edition, with any local amendments, governs – not the newest edition. Many
> jurisdictions lag several years behind the latest model code. Always confirm the adopted edition
> before quoting a requirement.

## Compliance Paths

Designers show compliance in one of two general ways:

- **Prescriptive path** – meet each listed requirement (LPD limits plus mandatory controls). This is
  what most retrofits use. Software such as the U.S. DOE's **COMcheck** is commonly used to document
  it.
- **Performance path** – energy modeling shows the whole building uses no more energy than a
  baseline. Mandatory provisions still apply even on the performance path.

Most lighting requirements fall into two buckets: **mandatory** (always required) and
**prescriptive** (required if using the prescriptive path). Lighting controls are largely mandatory.

## New Construction vs. Alterations

Energy codes apply fully to new buildings and additions. For **existing buildings**, they apply to
the parts being altered. Lighting alterations are where field techs most often get caught:

- Replacing a threshold share of luminaires in a space (the percentage varies by code and edition –
  check the adopted code) typically requires the new lighting to meet the LPD limit and certain
  controls requirements.
- **Lamp-only or ballast-only replacements** in existing luminaires are commonly exempt from
  the full alteration requirements, but check the specific code language.
- Rewiring or recircuiting may trigger additional control requirements.
- A change of occupancy or space use may trigger compliance for the whole space.

Because thresholds differ between 90.1, the IECC and Title 24 and between editions, the right answer
on a job is: **read the adopted code or ask the designer/plan reviewer**, and document what you were
told.

## Who Enforces It

The building department (AHJ) reviews energy-compliance documents at permit and may inspect
installed lighting and controls. Some jurisdictions and most utility programs require
**functional testing** or third-party verification of controls. ASHRAE 90.1 and the IECC both contain
functional testing requirements for lighting controls; Title 24 requires formal acceptance tests.

## Your Role in the Field

| Field task | Energy-code connection |
|---|---|
| Installing fixtures | Wattage and quantity must match the compliance documents (LPD) |
| Installing sensors | Location, mode and timeout must meet mandatory control rules |
| Daylight zones | Luminaires in daylight zones must be on separate daylight-responsive control |
| Substitutions | A "better" fixture with higher wattage can break LPD compliance – get approval |
| Commissioning | Functional testing and documentation may be required for final inspection |

## Key Takeaways
- Energy codes limit installed lighting power (LPD) and require specific lighting controls.
- ASHRAE 90.1 (Section 9), the IECC (Section C405) and California Title 24 Part 6 are the main codes you will meet.
- The AHJ-adopted edition and local amendments govern; confirm before quoting requirements.
- Mandatory provisions (mostly controls) apply on both the prescriptive and performance paths.
- Lighting alterations above a code-specified threshold trigger compliance; lamp/ballast-only swaps are often exempt – verify in the adopted code.
- Field substitutions and control settings can make or break compliance.
