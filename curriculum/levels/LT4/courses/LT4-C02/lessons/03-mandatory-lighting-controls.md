## The Big Picture

Modern energy codes treat most lighting controls as **mandatory**: they are required no matter how
efficient the luminaires are. Exact requirements, exceptions and numbers vary by code (ASHRAE 90.1,
IECC, Title 24) and edition, so this lesson teaches the **categories** you must recognize in the
field. Always confirm specifics in the adopted code and the project's compliance documents.

## Interior Control Categories

| Category | Purpose | Typical field implementation |
|---|---|---|
| **Local (manual) control** | Occupants can control lights in their space | Wallstation or switch in the space, readily accessible, where occupants can see the lights |
| **Restricted ON** | Prevent lights from turning on full automatically when not needed | Manual-ON, or automatic partial-ON (commonly to no more than about 50%) |
| **Automatic shutoff – occupancy/vacancy** | Turn lights off when a space is empty | Sensors that turn lights off within a code-maximum time after vacancy (20 minutes in several recent editions) |
| **Automatic shutoff – scheduled** | Turn lights off when the building is closed | Time-of-day schedule with limited override (often a maximum of about 2 hours per override) |
| **Light reduction / multi-level** | Let occupants or systems reduce light | Dimming or bi-level switching |
| **Daylight-responsive control** | Reduce electric light when daylight is available | Photosensors controlling primary and secondary sidelit and toplit zones separately where required |
| **Partial OFF / unoccupied setback** | Reduce light in spaces that cannot go fully dark | Corridors, stairwells and warehouses step down when unoccupied |
| **Special-application control** | Separate control of display, task, decorative, or plug-in lighting | Separate circuit or zone, sometimes with its own sensor |

Some spaces are exempt from specific requirements – for example, spaces where automatic shutoff
would endanger occupants. Spaces with 24-hour operation, security areas, emergency/egress lighting
and certain electrical rooms commonly have exceptions. Check the exact list in the adopted code.

> **Safety:** Required egress illumination (NFPA 101) must remain available when the space is
> occupied. Energy-code shutoff and setback controls must not leave egress paths below the
> life-safety requirement or disable emergency lighting. When a corridor or stairwell sensor steps
> lights down, the minimum level must still satisfy the egress design – confirm with the design
> documents.

## Exterior Control Categories

Exterior lighting rules commonly include:

1. **Daylight shutoff** – lights turn off when daylight is sufficient (photocell or astronomic time clock).
2. **Scheduled shutoff** – façade and landscape lighting off during late-night hours.
3. **Late-night reduction** – other exterior lighting reduced by a code-specified percentage during
   late-night hours or when no activity is detected (motion-sensing parking lot lighting is a common
   method).

Expect these rules whenever you replace parking lot or wall-pack fixtures. Many LED area lights now
include integral motion sensors and dimming to meet them.

## Functional Testing

ASHRAE 90.1 and the IECC require **functional testing** of installed lighting controls to confirm
they work as required, with documentation provided to the owner. California Title 24 goes further:
lighting control **acceptance tests** must be performed by a certified Acceptance Test Technician and
submitted on specific forms before final inspection.

A practical functional test checklist:

| Control | Test |
|---|---|
| Occupancy/vacancy sensor | Walk-out test: lights off within the programmed time (not longer than code maximum); restricted-ON behavior on entry |
| Wallstation | Local ON/OFF and dimming work from inside the space |
| Schedule | Sweep occurs at the programmed time; override duration is limited |
| Daylight control | Daylight zone dims in response to daylight; non-daylight zones do not |
| Exterior | Photocell/astronomic off in daylight; late-night reduction occurs |

These tests overlap heavily with NLC commissioning (LT4-C01). Do them once and document them in a
way that satisfies both the commissioning record and the energy-code requirement.

## Retrofit Field Scenarios

**Scenario 1:** A customer wants troffers in a 20-office suite replaced with LED troffers on the same
toggle switches. Depending on the adopted code and how many luminaires are replaced, this may be an
alteration that requires vacancy sensors, multi-level control and daylight control near windows.
Flag it before the bid is final.

**Scenario 2:** Replacing only the lamps in existing troffers with Type A TLEDs. This is often treated
as maintenance and exempt from controls upgrades – confirm with the adopted code.

**Scenario 3:** Replacing parking lot fixtures on the existing photocell. Likely triggers late-night
reduction requirements; specify fixtures with motion/dimming or add a control system.

## Key Takeaways
- Most lighting controls are mandatory regardless of luminaire efficiency.
- Recognize the categories: local control, restricted ON, automatic shutoff (occupancy and schedule), multi-level, daylight-responsive, partial OFF, and special-application control.
- Exterior rules typically require daylight shutoff, scheduled façade shutoff and late-night reduction.
- Functional testing is required under 90.1 and the IECC; Title 24 requires certified acceptance testing.
- Energy-code controls must never compromise egress or emergency lighting.
- Flag retrofit scopes that likely trigger control requirements before the job is bid or built.
