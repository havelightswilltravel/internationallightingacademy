## Why Senior Techs Need the Code Book

At LT1–LT3 you were told what the code requires for the tasks you performed. At LT4 you are expected
to **find and read the requirement yourself**, apply it to the situation in front of you, and know
when to escalate to the licensed electrician in charge, the engineer or the inspector. This course
focuses on the NEC articles that matter most for lighting work. Full code training continues in the
Electrical track (EA1-C02 and beyond).

> **Note:** This course references the **2023 NEC (NFPA 70)**. The NEC is revised every three years,
> and states and cities adopt editions on their own schedules, sometimes with amendments. Section
> numbers can change between editions. **The edition adopted by the local AHJ governs.** Always
> confirm which edition applies to the job.

## How the NEC Is Organized

| Part | Contents | Lighting relevance |
|---|---|---|
| Article 90 | Introduction – purpose, scope, arrangement, enforcement, mandatory vs. permissive rules | How to read everything else |
| Chapter 1 (Articles 100, 110) | Definitions; general requirements (approval, listing, terminations, working space) | 110.3(B) – install listed equipment per its instructions |
| Chapter 2 | Wiring and protection (branch circuits, feeders, overcurrent, grounding) | 210, 225, 240, 250 |
| Chapter 3 | Wiring methods and materials | 300, 310, 314 (boxes), raceway articles |
| Chapter 4 | Equipment for general use | **404 switches, 410 luminaires, 411 low-voltage lighting** |
| Chapters 5–7 | Special occupancies, special equipment, special conditions – these **supplement or modify** Chapters 1–4 | **600 signs, 680 pools, 700 emergency**, 725 control circuits |
| Chapter 8 | Communications systems (largely stand-alone) | Rarely for lighting |
| Chapter 9 | Tables | Conductor properties (Table 8), conduit fill |
| Informative annexes | Explanatory, **not enforceable** unless adopted | Examples and calculations |

The rule of thumb from Article 90: Chapters 1–4 apply generally; Chapters 5, 6 and 7 apply to special
situations and can **modify** the general rules. So a sign (Article 600) or an emergency lighting
circuit (Article 700) follows Chapters 1–4 **plus** its own article, and where they conflict, the
special article governs for that installation.

## Structure Inside an Article

Articles are divided into **Parts** (Roman numerals), **sections** (e.g., 410.16), and subdivisions
like 410.16(A)(1). Many sections include:

- **Exceptions** – follow the rule they modify. A "shall be permitted" exception is optional; a
  "shall" exception is mandatory.
- **Informational Notes** – explanatory only, **not enforceable** requirements (Article 90). Example: the
  3% / 5% voltage-drop recommendation is in informational notes.
- **Tables** – often have their own notes that are part of the requirement.

Key language:

| Wording | Meaning |
|---|---|
| "shall" / "shall not" | Mandatory |
| "shall be permitted" | Allowed, not required |
| "listed" | Included in a list published by a recognized testing organization (NRTL) |
| "identified (for a use)" | Recognized as suitable for a specific purpose, environment or application |
| "approved" | Acceptable to the AHJ |

In the 2023 NEC most definitions were consolidated into **Article 100**. When a term matters – such as
"luminaire," "wet location," "multiwire branch circuit" – look up the definition. Many code
disputes are really definition disputes.

## How to Find an Answer Fast

1. **Restate the question** in code terms: What equipment? What location? What action?
2. **Start with the index** in the back of the book – look up the equipment ("Luminaires") and then
   the sub-topic ("closets," "wet locations," "clearances").
3. **Or use the table of contents** if you know which article covers it.
4. **Read the scope** of the article to confirm it applies.
5. **Read the whole section**, including exceptions and referenced sections.
6. **Check for Chapter 5–7 modifications** (Is it a sign? Emergency system? Near a pool? Hazardous
   location?).
7. **Check local amendments** and the specifications – they may be stricter.
8. **Write the reference** on the work order or RFI (e.g., "NEC 2023 410.116(A)(1)").

## Tabbing Your Code Book

Permanent tabs for lighting work: 90, 100, 110, 210, 225, 240, 250, 300, 310, 314, 404, 410, 411, 600,
700, 725 and Chapter 9 Tables. (Check your exam's or company's rules on what tabs and notes are allowed
in a code book used for testing.)

## Know Your Limits

The NEC is a minimum safety standard, not a design manual. Lighting technicians must also follow
manufacturer instructions, project specifications, energy codes, NFPA 101, OSHA and state licensing
law. If a code question affects whether an installation is safe or legal and you are not certain,
**stop and ask** the licensed electrician responsible for the work. Do not guess, and do not let
schedule pressure decide a code question.

> **Safety:** The NEC governs how installations are built; **NFPA 70E and OSHA** govern how you work
> safely on them. A code-compliant installation can still hurt you if you work on it energized.
> De-energize, apply LOTO and verify absence of voltage before work, regardless of how well it was
> installed.

## Key Takeaways
- The AHJ-adopted NEC edition (with local amendments) governs; numbers can change between editions.
- Chapters 1–4 apply generally; Chapters 5–7 supplement or modify them for special situations.
- "Shall" is mandatory, "shall be permitted" is optional, informational notes are not enforceable.
- Definitions (mostly in Article 100 in the 2023 NEC) often decide the answer.
- Find answers with the index or table of contents, read the scope, full section and exceptions, then check special articles and local amendments.
- Escalate uncertain code questions to the licensed electrician, engineer or AHJ.
