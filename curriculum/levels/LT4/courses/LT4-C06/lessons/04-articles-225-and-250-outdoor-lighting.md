## Outdoor Lighting Pulls in Several Articles

Site and pole lighting (LT3-C05) involves more code articles than indoor work. This lesson covers the
NEC rules senior techs most often need for outdoor branch circuits, underground wiring, poles and
grounding. Always confirm with the AHJ-adopted edition and the project drawings.

## Article 225 – Outside Branch Circuits and Feeders

Article 225 covers branch circuits and feeders run outdoors on or between buildings, structures or
poles, and the equipment connected to them.

### Overhead clearances (225.18)
For overhead conductors up to 600 V (summary – read the section for conditions):

| Location | Minimum clearance |
|---|---|
| Above finished grade, sidewalks, or platforms accessible only to pedestrians (up to 150 V to ground) | 10 ft |
| Residential property and driveways, and commercial areas not subject to truck traffic (up to 300 V to ground) | 12 ft |
| Same areas as above, over 300 V to ground | 15 ft |
| Over public streets, alleys, roads, parking areas subject to truck traffic, and other land traversed by vehicles | 18 ft |

Trees and vegetation must not be used to support overhead conductor spans (225.26).

### Lighting equipment outdoors (225.7)
Article 225 includes provisions for supplying outdoor luminaires, including conditions under which
circuits over 120 V (such as 277 V) may supply luminaires on buildings or poles – for example,
locating luminaires a minimum distance from windows, platforms and fire escapes. Check 225.7 when
installing 277 V or 480 V luminaires on building exteriors.

### Disconnects for separate structures
Branch circuits or feeders supplying a separate **building or structure** generally require a
disconnecting means at that building or structure (Part II of Article 225). Whether this applies to
site equipment depends on definitions and the AHJ's interpretation – ask when in doubt.

## Article 300 – Underground Installations (300.5)

| Rule | Summary |
|---|---|
| **Burial depth (Table 300.5)** | Depends on wiring method and location – for example, direct-burial cable generally 24 in; rigid metal conduit 6 in; listed nonmetallic raceway 18 in in general locations. Under streets, roads, driveways and **parking lots**, the minimum is 24 in for all methods |
| **Wet location (300.5(B))** | The interior of underground enclosures and raceways is a **wet location** – conductors must be listed for wet locations (for example, THWN-2, XHHW-2) |
| **Protection where emerging (300.5(D))** | Direct-buried conductors emerging from grade must be protected by enclosures or raceways from the required depth up to at least 8 ft above grade |
| **Splices** | Direct-buried splices must use methods listed for the purpose |

## Poles Supporting Luminaires (410.30(B))

Metal and nonmetallic poles may be used to support luminaires and serve as a raceway for the supply
conductors when the pole meets the requirements of 410.30(B), including:

- An accessible **handhole** (with a cover suitable for use in wet locations) for access to supply
  terminations – with limited exceptions for short poles or poles with hinged bases.
- A **grounding terminal** accessible from the handhole, where the equipment grounding conductor is
  connected to the pole.
- Conductors in vertical poles supported as required.

## Article 250 – Grounding Basics for Lighting

### The equipment grounding conductor is the fault path
NEC 250.4 requires an **effective ground-fault current path** – a low-impedance path that carries fault
current back to the source so the overcurrent device trips quickly. **The earth is not an effective
ground-fault current path.** A ground rod alone has far too much resistance to trip a breaker.

| Practice | Code position |
|---|---|
| EGC run with the circuit conductors to every pole and luminaire | Required (wire type or a permitted raceway type, 250.118) |
| Ground rod at the pole base | **Permitted as an auxiliary electrode (250.54)**, but it does **not** replace the EGC and the earth cannot be used as the sole EGC |
| Using the neutral to ground the pole or luminaire | Generally **prohibited** on the load side of the service disconnect (250.142(B)) |
| Bonding neutral to ground in a pole base or lighting panel downstream of the service | Not permitted (the neutral-to-ground bond belongs at the service or separately derived system) |
| EGC size | From Table 250.122 based on the overcurrent device rating – e.g., 12 AWG copper for 20 A |
| EGC increased when ungrounded conductors are upsized for voltage drop | Required – increase proportionally (250.122(B)) |
| EGC identification | Bare, green, or green with yellow stripes (250.119) |

### Why this matters in the field
A pole with a ground rod but no EGC can become energized by a fault and **stay energized** because
the breaker never trips – the pole sits at a dangerous voltage until someone touches it. Every metal
pole and luminaire must be bonded to an EGC that runs back to the panel.

> **Safety:** Treat every metal pole and its handhole contents as energized until proven otherwise. A
> missing or broken EGC can leave a pole energized with no tripped breaker. Before opening the
> handhole, check for voltage between the pole and earth with a non-contact tester and a properly rated
> meter, then de-energize the circuit, apply LOTO and verify absence of voltage on all conductors before
> working on splices or fuses.

## Key Takeaways
- Article 225 governs outside branch circuits: overhead clearances (225.18), no vegetation support (225.26), conditions for outdoor luminaires over 120 V (225.7).
- 300.5 sets burial depths (24 in under parking lots for all methods), treats underground raceways as wet locations, and requires protection where conductors emerge.
- Poles used as raceways need an accessible handhole and grounding terminal, with limited exceptions (410.30(B)).
- The earth is not an effective fault path: every pole and luminaire needs an EGC back to the source; ground rods are only auxiliary (250.4, 250.54).
- Do not use the neutral for grounding or bond neutral to ground downstream of the service.
