## Article 404 – Switches

Article 404 covers all switches, switching devices and circuit breakers used as switches, operating
at 1000 V and below. Senior techs regularly install and troubleshoot switches, dimmers and sensors in
switch boxes, so these rules matter.

### Switch the ungrounded conductor (404.2)
- **404.2(A):** Three-way and four-way switches must be wired so all switching is done only in the
  **ungrounded** circuit conductor.
- **404.2(B):** Switches and circuit breakers must **not disconnect the grounded conductor** of a
  circuit, unless the device simultaneously disconnects all conductors of the circuit (or another
  specific exception applies).

A switched neutral leaves the luminaire energized when "off" – a classic shock hazard for the next
person who relamps it.

### Grounded conductor at switch locations (404.2(C))
Where switches control lighting loads, a **grounded (neutral) conductor** must generally be provided
at the switch location, with limited exceptions (for example, where the switch box is accessible for
adding one later through a raceway, or the switch does not serve a habitable room or bathroom – read
the exceptions). This exists so electronic switches – occupancy sensors, timers, dimmers – can be
powered without using the equipment grounding conductor. Related rule: **electronic lighting control
switches must be listed and must not introduce current on the equipment grounding conductor** during
normal operation (404.22, with limited exceptions for certain replacements). If you need to install
an occupancy sensor and there's no neutral, do not "borrow" the ground – choose a device listed for
the situation or have a neutral run.

### Grounding switches and faceplates (404.9)
Snap switches, including dimmers and similar control switches, must be connected to an equipment
grounding conductor, and metal faceplates must be grounded – with exceptions for certain replacements
where no grounding means exists.

### Voltage between adjacent devices (404.8(B))
Snap switches must not be ganged with other switches or receptacles in an enclosure **unless the
voltage between adjacent devices does not exceed 300 V**, or **identified barriers** are securely
installed between them.

**Field example:** two 277 V switches in one two-gang box, fed from different phases of a 480Y/277 V
system, have **480 V between them**. A barrier is required. Two 277 V switches on the same phase
(0 V between them) do not exceed 300 V. Two 120 V switches on a 120/208 V system: 208 V max – no
barrier needed for that reason.

### Accessibility (404.8(A))
Switches and circuit breakers used as switches must be located so they can be operated from a readily
accessible place, with the center of the operating handle in its highest position no more than
**6 ft 7 in (2.0 m)** above the floor or working platform (with exceptions).

### Ratings (404.14)
| Device | Permitted loads |
|---|---|
| AC general-use snap switch | Resistive and inductive loads (including electric-discharge lamps) up to its ampere rating at the voltage involved; tungsten-filament lamps up to its rating at 120 V; motor loads up to 80% of its rating |
| General-use dimmer switches (404.14(E)) | Only permanently installed incandescent luminaires, **unless listed** for the control of other loads and installed accordingly |

For LED loads, use dimmers and switches listed and rated for LED/electronic driver loads and check the
luminaire manufacturer's compatibility list (LT3-C02). Inrush (LT4-C04) can weld switch contacts that
are under-rated for electronic loads.

### Damp and wet locations (404.4)
Switches in damp or wet locations must be in weatherproof enclosures, and switches are generally not
permitted within wet locations of tub or shower spaces unless installed as part of a listed tub or
shower assembly.

## Article 411 – Low-Voltage Lighting

Article 411 covers lighting systems operating at **not more than 30 volts AC or 60 volts DC**, such as
landscape lighting, cable and rail lighting systems, and some under-cabinet systems.

Key points:
- The system must be **listed as a complete lighting system**, or **assembled from listed parts**.
- It is supplied by a **branch circuit rated not more than 20 A**.
- Low-voltage does not mean low hazard: **high currents** at 12 V can overheat conductors and start
  fires, which is why power supplies, conductors and connections must be listed and sized correctly.
- Bare-conductor (cable and rail) systems have restrictions on installation height and location.
- Installation near **pools, spas and fountains** is restricted unless permitted by Article 680.
- Secondary wiring must not be run through walls, floors or ceilings unless installed using a
  permitted wiring method.

Low-voltage lighting installed by "kit" often fails inspection because components from different
manufacturers were combined without a listing covering the combination.

> **Safety:** The primary side of a low-voltage lighting power supply is line voltage. De-energize the
> supply branch circuit, apply LOTO and verify absence of voltage before working on the power supply or
> its primary connections. On the secondary side, remove rings and metal jewelry – a 12 V system can
> deliver enough current through a ring to cause a severe burn.

## Key Takeaways
- Switch only the ungrounded conductor; do not switch the neutral (404.2(A)–(B)).
- Provide a neutral at switch locations for lighting (404.2(C)); electronic switches must not put current on the EGC (404.22).
- More than 300 V between adjacent devices requires identified barriers (404.8(B)) – common with 277 V switches on different phases.
- Switch handles no higher than 6 ft 7 in (404.8(A)); use devices rated and listed for the load (404.14).
- Article 411 low-voltage lighting: ≤30 V AC / 60 V DC, listed system or listed parts, branch circuit ≤20 A.
- Low voltage can still mean high current and fire risk; LOTO the primary before working on supplies.
