## De-Energize First

NFPA 70E (2024) requires energized electrical conductors and circuit parts to be put into an
**electrically safe work condition (ESWC)** before an employee works within the limited approach
boundary or interacts with equipment where an increased likelihood of injury from an arc flash
exists — unless energized work is justified. De-energizing is the **elimination** control at the
top of the hierarchy.

### Establishing an ESWC (Article 120 process, summarized)
1. Determine all possible sources of electrical supply to the equipment — check up-to-date
   drawings, diagrams and identification tags. Look for back-feeds: generators, UPS, PV, tie
   breakers, control power from other panels, capacitors.
2. After properly interrupting the load current, **open** the disconnecting device(s) for each
   source.
3. Wherever possible, **visually verify** that all blades of disconnecting devices are fully open
   or that draw-out breakers are withdrawn to the fully disconnected position.
4. Release **stored electrical energy** (capacitors, VFD DC buses) and block or relieve stored
   non-electrical energy as needed.
5. Apply **lockout/tagout** devices per the documented, established policy.
6. Use an **adequately rated test instrument** to test each phase conductor or circuit part to
   verify it is de-energized. Test **phase-to-phase and phase-to-ground**. Before and after each
   test, verify the instrument is operating satisfactorily on a known voltage source (the
   live-dead-live method). Permanently mounted absence-of-voltage testers may be used where
   they meet the standard's requirements.
7. Where the possibility of **induced voltages or stored energy** exists, ground the phase
   conductors before touching them. Where conductors could contact other exposed energized
   parts, apply temporary protective grounding equipment as required.

Until absence of voltage is verified, the equipment is treated as energized — the person testing
wears the PPE required for that equipment.

## When Energized Work Is Permitted

Energized work is permitted only when the employer can show one of these justifications:

| Justification | Examples |
|---|---|
| **Greater hazard** — de-energizing would introduce additional hazards or increased risk | Life-support equipment, emergency alarm systems, hazardous-location ventilation, deactivating would create a hazard elsewhere |
| **Infeasibility** — the task cannot be done de-energized due to equipment design or operational limitations | Diagnostics and testing (start-up, troubleshooting) that require the circuit to be energized; work on circuits forming an integral part of a continuous process that would require shutting down the whole process |
| **Less than 50 V** | Circuits below 50 V need not be de-energized if there is no increased exposure to electrical burns or explosion from arcs |

**Inconvenience, schedule pressure, or customer preference is not a justification.** "The
customer doesn't want the lights off" is not infeasibility — the work gets scheduled after hours.

## The Energized Electrical Work Permit (EEWP)

When energized work is justified and performed within the restricted approach boundary, or when
the employee interacts with equipment where an arc-flash hazard exists (with exceptions below),
work is done under a written permit (130.2(B)). The permit must include, at a minimum:

1. Description of the circuit and equipment and their location
2. Description of the work to be performed
3. Justification for why the work must be done energized
4. Description of the safe work practices to be used
5. Results of the **shock risk assessment**: voltage, limited and restricted approach
   boundaries, and the PPE and other protective measures for shock
6. Results of the **arc-flash risk assessment**: incident energy at the working distance or the
   PPE category, the arc-flash boundary, and the arc-flash PPE
7. Means used to **restrict access** by unqualified persons
8. Evidence of completion of a **job briefing**, including discussion of job-specific hazards
9. **Energized work approval** signatures (authorizing or responsible management, safety
   officer, or owner, as the employer's program specifies)

### Tasks that do not require a permit
Provided appropriate safe work practices and PPE are used, a permit is **not** required for
qualified persons performing:
- Testing, troubleshooting and voltage measuring
- Thermography and visual inspections where the restricted approach boundary is not crossed
- Access to and egress from an area with energized equipment if no electrical work is performed
  and the restricted approach boundary is not crossed
- General housekeeping and miscellaneous non-electrical tasks where the restricted approach
  boundary is not crossed

Note: these tasks still require the shock and arc-flash risk assessments, PPE and safe work
practices — they are exempt only from the written permit.

## Worked Scenario

**Task:** Replace a failed 20 A single-pole breaker in an occupied hospital's 208Y/120 V panel
feeding critical-branch receptacles in an ICU.

- Can it be de-energized? The panel can be fed from the alternate source during a planned
  shutdown, or the circuits temporarily moved. The facility's first answer should be a planned,
  coordinated outage. If engineering and clinical staff determine that de-energizing would
  create a greater hazard to patients, energized work may be justified.
- Shock: 208 V → limited 3 ft 6 in, restricted 1 ft 0 in; Class 00 or Class 0 gloves with
  protectors; insulated tools.
- Arc flash: label shows 1.8 cal/cm² at 18 in, AFB 22 in → arc-rated clothing and face/head
  protection rated at least 1.8 cal/cm² (most employers use a minimum of their category 1 or 2
  kit), plus hearing protection.
- Access: barricade tape at the arc-flash boundary and an attendant.
- Permit completed, job briefing held, approvals signed before work starts.

> **Safety:** If the scope changes during energized work — the breaker won't release, you find
> damaged bus, or conditions differ from the permit — stop, back out to a safe position, and
> reassess. The permit covers only the task and conditions it describes.

## Key Takeaways
- De-energize and establish an ESWC first; it eliminates the hazard.
- ESWC: identify sources, interrupt load, open, verify visually, release stored energy, LOTO,
  test with a verified meter phase-to-phase and phase-to-ground, ground if needed.
- Energized work requires a greater-hazard or infeasibility justification — not convenience.
- The EEWP documents the justification, risk assessments, PPE, access control, briefing and approvals.
- Testing, troubleshooting and voltage measuring are permit-exempt but not PPE-exempt.
