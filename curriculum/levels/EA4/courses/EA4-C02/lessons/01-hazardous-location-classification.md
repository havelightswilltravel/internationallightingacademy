## Why Classified Locations Are Different
Ordinary electrical equipment produces arcs, sparks, and hot surfaces every time a switch
operates or a motor starts. In most buildings that does not matter. Where flammable gases,
vapors, combustible dusts, or ignitible fibers may be present in ignitable concentrations,
that same arc can cause an explosion or fire. NEC Articles 500 through 516 (2023 NEC; the
AHJ-adopted edition governs) set the rules. The facility owner, often with an engineer and the
fire marshal, is responsible for **classifying** the areas. The electrician's job is to install
equipment and wiring that suits the classification shown on the drawings — never to guess it.

## The Class/Division System (Articles 500–503)

| Class | Hazard | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| Class I | Flammable gases, flammable liquid-produced vapors, combustible liquid-produced vapors | Fuel dispensing, refineries, spray booths, solvent rooms |
| Class II | Combustible dust | Grain elevators, flour mills, coal handling, metal powder plants |
| Class III | Ignitible fibers/flyings | Textile mills, woodworking producing fibers, cotton gins |

| Division | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Division 1 | The hazard is present (or likely) under **normal** operating conditions, during repair or maintenance, or where equipment failure could release the hazard and simultaneously cause electrical failure |
| Division 2 | The hazard is present only under **abnormal** conditions — container rupture, ventilation failure — or the area is adjacent to a Division 1 area and could receive the hazard |

## Material Groups
Groups describe how easily the material ignites and how violent the explosion is. Equipment
must be identified for the specific group.

| Group | Class | Typical materials |
|---|---|---|
| A | I | Acetylene |
| B | I | Hydrogen and similar gases |
| C | I | Ethylene and similar |
| D | I | Propane, gasoline, natural gas, and many solvents |
| E | II | Combustible metal dusts (aluminum, magnesium) |
| F | II | Carbonaceous dusts (coal, carbon black, coke) |
| G | II | Other combustible dusts (flour, grain, wood, plastics, chemicals) |

Equipment listed for Group B is not automatically suitable for Group D — the listing must
include each group present.

## The Zone System (Articles 505 and 506)
The NEC also permits the international zone system, based on IEC practice:

| Zone | Hazard present |
|---|---|
| Zone 0 | Flammable gas/vapor continuously or for long periods |
| Zone 1 | Likely under normal operation |
| Zone 2 | Not likely; only for a short time if it occurs |
| Zone 20 / 21 / 22 | Equivalent levels for combustible dust (Article 506) |

Zone gas groups are **IIC** (most severe — acetylene, hydrogen), **IIB** (ethylene), and **IIA**
(propane). Zone equipment uses protection-technique markings such as "Ex d" (flameproof),
"Ex e" (increased safety), and "Ex i" (intrinsic safety). Do not mix systems on the same
installation without engineering direction; follow the area classification drawing.

## Temperature Codes
Every gas or vapor has an **autoignition temperature** (AIT). Equipment is marked with a
temperature code (T-code) showing its maximum surface temperature. The equipment's T-code
temperature must be **below** the AIT of the material present.

| T-code | Max surface temperature |
|---|---|
| T1 | 450°C |
| T2 | 300°C |
| T3 | 200°C |
| T4 | 135°C |
| T5 | 100°C |
| T6 | 85°C |

(Subdivisions such as T2A–T2D, T3A–T3C, and T4A also exist.) Example: a vapor with an AIT of
160°C needs equipment marked T4, T5, or T6 — T3 (200°C) would be too hot.

## Protection Techniques
| Technique | How it works |
|---|---|
| Explosionproof (Class I) | Enclosure contains an internal explosion and cools escaping gases through flame paths so they cannot ignite the surrounding atmosphere |
| Dust-ignitionproof (Class II) | Excludes dust and prevents surface temperatures that ignite dust layers |
| Purged and pressurized | Positive pressure of clean air or inert gas keeps the hazard out |
| Intrinsically safe | Circuit energy is limited so it cannot ignite the material even under fault conditions; uses listed barriers and associated apparatus |
| Nonincendive | For Division 2 — normal-operation arcs lack the energy to ignite |
| Hermetically sealed / oil-immersed | Arcing parts sealed from the atmosphere |

## Common Classified Locations You Will See
- **Motor fuel dispensing** (Article 514): areas around dispensers and below grade (pits)
  classified by Table 514.3(B)(1).
- **Commercial repair garages** (Article 511): the area up to a specified height above the floor
  may be classified where flammable fuels are transferred; ventilation can reduce the
  classification.
- **Spray application** (Article 516): booths and their surroundings.
- **Aircraft hangars** (Article 513) and **bulk storage plants** (Article 515).

> **Safety:** Before opening any enclosure in a classified area, confirm with the facility that
> the area is gas-free (tested with a calibrated detector) or obtain a hot work permit. Opening
> an explosionproof enclosure with the circuit energized in a hazardous atmosphere defeats the
> protection completely. De-energize, apply lockout/tagout, verify absence of voltage with a
> meter approved for the area, and wait any cool-down time marked on the enclosure.

## Key Takeaways
- Classification is the owner's/engineer's responsibility; install to the drawing.
- Class I = gases/vapors, Class II = dust, Class III = fibers; Division 1 = normal conditions,
  Division 2 = abnormal conditions.
- Groups A–D are gases; E–G are dusts; equipment must be identified for each group present.
- Zones 0/1/2 (gas) and 20/21/22 (dust) are an alternative system under Articles 505/506.
- Equipment T-code temperature must be lower than the autoignition temperature of the material.
