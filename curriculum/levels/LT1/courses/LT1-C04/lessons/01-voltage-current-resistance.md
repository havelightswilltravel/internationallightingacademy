## Why Fundamentals Matter in the Field

You do not need to be an engineer to change a ballast, but you do need to understand what
electricity is doing. Knowing the basics helps you read labels, choose the right parts,
understand meter readings, and recognize when something is wrong. It is also the foundation
for every level that follows, all the way to the journeyman exam.

## The Electrical Circuit

A **circuit** is a complete path for current to flow from a source, through a load, and back
to the source.

| Part | Job | Lighting example |
|---|---|---|
| **Source** | Provides the push (voltage) | Utility transformer, panel, battery |
| **Conductors** | Carry current to and from the load | Copper branch-circuit wires |
| **Load** | Uses energy to do work | Lamp, ballast, LED driver |
| **Control** | Opens or closes the path | Switch, relay, occupancy sensor |
| **Protection** | Opens the circuit on overload or fault | Circuit breaker, fuse |

If the path is broken anywhere (an open switch, a broken wire, a burned-out lamp), current
stops. That is an **open circuit**. If current finds an unintended low-resistance path, such as
a hot wire touching a neutral or a grounded fixture housing, that is a **short circuit** or
**ground fault**, and very high current flows until the breaker trips.

## Voltage (V)

**Voltage** is electrical pressure: the difference in electrical potential between two points
that pushes current through a circuit.

- Unit: **volt (V)**. Symbol in formulas: **E** or **V**.
- Measured **between two points** (across a load, or from hot to neutral).
- Voltage can be present even when no current flows. A switched-off fixture still has voltage
  on its supply side, and an open switch has voltage across its terminals.

**Water analogy:** voltage is like water pressure in a pipe.

## Current (I)

**Current** is the flow of electric charge (electrons) through a conductor.

- Unit: **ampere (A)**, or "amp." Symbol: **I**.
- 1 milliamp (mA) = 1/1,000 of an amp.
- Current only flows when there is a complete circuit.
- Current is what heats wires, trips breakers, and injures people.

**Water analogy:** current is the rate of flow, like gallons per minute.

## Resistance (R)

**Resistance** is opposition to current flow.

- Unit: **ohm (Ω)**. Symbol: **R**.
- Higher resistance means less current for the same voltage.
- Resistance is measured with a meter **only on de-energized** components.

**Water analogy:** resistance is like the size of the pipe or a partly closed valve.

### What Affects Resistance

| Factor | Effect |
|---|---|
| Material | Copper and aluminum have low resistance; rubber, plastic, glass, and dry fiberglass have very high resistance |
| Length | Longer wire = more resistance |
| Cross-section (wire size) | Larger wire = less resistance. Note: **smaller AWG number = larger wire** (12 AWG is larger than 14 AWG) |
| Temperature | Most metals increase in resistance as they heat up |
| Condition | Corrosion, loose terminals, and nicked conductors add resistance and create heat |

## Conductors and Insulators

- **Conductors** let current flow easily: copper, aluminum, steel, water with dissolved
  minerals, and **the human body**.
- **Insulators** resist current: thermoplastic wire insulation (THHN), rubber, glass, porcelain,
  dry fiberglass.

No insulator is perfect. Damaged, wet, dirty, or overheated insulation can fail. That is why we
inspect cords, leads, and ladders, and why wet skin and wet locations increase shock risk.

> **Safety:** Your body's resistance drops sharply when your skin is wet or sweaty, or if the
> skin is broken. Lower resistance means more current through you at the same voltage. This is
> covered in the next lesson with Ohm's law.

## Loose Connections Cause Heat

A loose or corroded connection adds resistance at one small spot. Current flowing through
that resistance produces heat. Over time, the connection discolors, the insulation melts,
and it can start a fire. When you see browned sockets, melted connectors, or darkened wire
insulation in a fixture, you are seeing resistance heating. Report it; do not just replace the
lamp and walk away.

## Key Takeaways
- A circuit needs a source, conductors, a load, and a complete path.
- Voltage is pressure (volts), current is flow (amps), resistance is opposition (ohms).
- Voltage can be present without current; current needs a complete path.
- Smaller AWG numbers mean larger wires with less resistance.
- Loose or corroded connections create resistance and heat, a common cause of burned sockets and fires.
