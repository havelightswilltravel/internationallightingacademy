## Three Ways to Connect a Wire to a Device

| Method | How it works | Pros | Cons |
|--------|--------------|------|------|
| Backstab (push-in) | Stripped wire pushes into a hole and a small spring clip grips it | Fast | Small contact area; spring loosens with heat and time; most current devices accept 14 AWG only. A frequent cause of failures |
| Screw terminal (side-wire) | Wire is formed into a hook and wrapped clockwise under the screw head | Solid, inspectable, accepts 14 and 12 AWG | Slower; a hook wrapped the wrong way gets pushed out as the screw tightens |
| Back-wire clamp (screw-clamp) | Straight wire inserts behind a pressure plate, then the side screw clamps it | Fast and solid; often accepts two wires per terminal | Only on spec-grade and some residential devices; must still be torqued |

**Recommended practice for replacements:** use the screw terminal or the back-wire clamp. Do
not use backstab holes, even when the old device was backstabbed. (Follow any stricter rule
your supervisor gives you.)

### Making a good screw terminal connection

1. Strip only the length shown on the device's strip gauge (usually about 3/4 in for a hook).
2. Form a hook with long-nose pliers.
3. Place the hook under the screw so it wraps **clockwise**, the same direction the screw
   tightens, about three-quarters of the way around.
4. Tighten to the manufacturer's torque value. The NEC requires a calibrated torque tool
   where the manufacturer gives a torque value (2023 NEC 110.14(D)). Many devices list a value
   on the package or instruction sheet.
5. Make sure no bare copper extends past the screw head, and the insulation comes right up to
   the terminal.
6. Only one wire under a screw terminal unless the device is marked for more. If you need to
   connect more wires, use a pigtail with a listed wire connector.

## Why Devices Fail

- **Loose connections** heat up. Heat oxidizes the copper and loosens the connection more.
  This cycle can end with burned terminals, melted plastic and even fire.
- **Worn receptacle contacts** stop gripping plugs. Plugs fall out or arc when moved.
- **Overloads**: space heaters, coffee makers, or too many fixtures on one switch or dimmer.
- **Physical damage**: cracked faces, broken mounting ears, missing cover plates.
- **Age and heat**: brittle plastic and insulation, especially in older buildings.

## The Company Inspection Procedure

The company guide says: **look for damaged, burned or loose parts, check connections, and
replace if line voltage is good.** Here is how you look, in order. Steps 1 to 3 are done
before opening anything; step 4 and later are done only with the circuit locked out.

1. **Look and listen from the outside.** Discoloration or brown/black marks around slots or
   the switch toggle, a melted or cracked face, a burning smell, buzzing or crackling, a cover
   plate that is warm to the touch.
2. **Check plug grip.** Does a plug fall out or wiggle loosely? (Use a plug-in tester or a
   known good plug, never your fingers in the slots.)
3. **Wiggle the switch handle.** Flickering lights when you wiggle it, or a mushy feel, point
   to a worn switch or a loose terminal.
4. **De-energize, lock out and verify.** Before removing a cover plate on a device you
   suspect is damaged, lock out the circuit and verify absence of voltage at the device
   (live-dead-live).
5. **Remove the cover plate and pull the device out** gently. Look for:
   - Backstabbed wires, loose terminal screws, or wires pulled partly out of a clamp
   - Scorched terminals, melted plastic, darkened or brittle insulation
   - Nicked conductors, bare copper outside the terminal
   - Broken mounting ears or a broken box, missing box, or box set too deep
   - Aluminum wiring (dull silver color, marked "AL"), cloth-insulated wire, or no ground wire
   - More wires than you expect, such as two circuits (two hot wires) in the box
6. **Decide.** Replace a damaged device if the conditions in Lesson 3 are met. Write it up if
   the damage goes beyond the device.

> **Safety:** A burned device means a lot of heat has been there. The damage may continue
> into the wire, the box, or the next device on the circuit. If insulation behind the device
> is brittle or charred, do not just replace the device. Stop, leave the circuit locked out
> or the device made safe, and write it up for an electrician.

## Write It Up When You Find

| Finding | Why |
|---------|-----|
| Charred or brittle insulation on the wires | Wire damage extends beyond the device |
| Aluminum branch-circuit wiring | Requires special devices and methods |
| No equipment ground where a grounded receptacle is installed | Needs correction (or a GFCI with proper labeling, decided by an electrician) |
| Two circuits, a shared neutral, or a multiwire branch circuit | Hazard of energized conductors after one breaker is off |
| Damaged, missing or overfilled box | Box repair is branch-circuit work |
| Repeated failures at the same location | Overload or upstream problem |
| Breaker trips when the device is used | Upstream or load problem |

## Key Takeaways
- Backstabs are a common failure point; terminate replacements on screws or back-wire clamps.
- Hook wire clockwise under the screw, strip to the gauge, torque to the manufacturer's value.
- Inspect outside first, then lock out and verify before pulling a device.
- Look for burned, loose and damaged parts and for conditions that go beyond the device.
- Charred wire, aluminum wire, missing grounds, shared neutrals and damaged boxes are write-ups.
