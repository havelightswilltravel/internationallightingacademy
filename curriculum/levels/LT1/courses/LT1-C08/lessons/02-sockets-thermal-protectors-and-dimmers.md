## The Components of an Incandescent Fixture

The company's component list for incandescent systems is: **fixture, lamp, socket, wire,
switch and thermal protector**. Here is what each one does and how it fails.

| Component | What it does | How it fails |
|-----------|--------------|--------------|
| Fixture (housing, trim, junction box) | Holds the parts, contains heat, protects wiring | Overheated paint and wiring, loose trim, damaged box |
| Lamp | Makes the light | Open filament, broken glass, loose base |
| Socket | Connects the lamp to the circuit | Worn or flattened center contact, scorching, cracked body, loose terminal |
| Wire | Carries power from the box to the socket | Brittle or cracked insulation from heat, loose splices |
| Switch or dimmer | Turns the fixture on, off or dims it | Worn contacts, loose terminals, failed dimmer electronics |
| Thermal protector | Shuts the fixture off if the housing overheats | Cycles on and off, or fails open (fixture stays dark) |

## The Socket and the "Socket Eye"

A screw-base socket has two contacts. The **screw shell** is the threaded metal sleeve and
should connect to the neutral (white) conductor. The **center contact** at the bottom, often
called the **socket eye** or center tab, connects to the hot conductor.

The center contact is a springy brass tab. Over years of heat and lamp changes it can get
**flattened** or lose its spring. The lamp's base then barely touches it. Symptoms:

- The lamp works only when screwed in very tightly, or flickers when the fixture is bumped
- A new lamp does not light even though the old one tested good on the bench
- Scorch marks or pitting on the tab and the lamp base (arcing)

With the circuit **locked out and verified dead**, a lightly flattened tab can sometimes be
lifted slightly (about 1/8 in) with an insulated screwdriver. If the tab is discolored,
pitted, cracked, or does not spring back, replace the socket. Never lift a tab or reach into a
socket with the circuit live.

> **Safety:** A socket's center contact is energized whenever the switch is on, and the
> screw shell may be energized if the fixture was wired backwards. Lock out the circuit and
> verify absence of voltage (live-dead-live) before touching anything inside a socket.

## Thermal Protectors in Recessed Cans

Recessed incandescent fixtures sit inside the ceiling where heat can build up. The NEC
requires recessed incandescent luminaires to have **thermal protection** (with limited
exceptions; see NEC Article 410, the part on recessed luminaires). The thermal protector is a
small heat-sensing switch, usually mounted on the side or top of the housing, wired in series
with the socket. If the can gets too hot, it opens and the lamp goes out. When the can cools,
it closes and the lamp comes back on.

### IC vs. Non-IC

| Type | Meaning | Rule of thumb |
|------|---------|---------------|
| IC (Insulation Contact) | Rated to be buried in or touched by thermal insulation | Lower lamp-wattage limits, designed for contact |
| Non-IC | Must be kept away from insulation | Insulation must be kept back from the can (generally 3 in) |

The rating is on the label inside the housing. If you see insulation packed against a
non-IC can, write it up. Do not move or remove insulation yourself unless you are assigned to.

### Why Thermal Protectors Cycle

A fixture that goes off for several minutes, then comes back on, then goes off again is the
classic sign of a cycling thermal protector. Common causes:

1. **Lamp wattage over the label limit**, for example a 100 W lamp in a 60 W can
2. **Wrong lamp type**, such as an A19 in a can labeled for reflector lamps only
3. **Insulation against a non-IC can**, or attic heat in summer
4. **Wrong trim** that blocks airflow or reflects heat back into the can
5. **A failed protector** that trips at too low a temperature, or fails open entirely

Fix the cause, not the symptom. The protector is doing its job. **Never bypass, jumper or
remove a thermal protector.** If the correct lamp is installed and the can still cycles, or
the protector has failed open, write it up for fixture or protector replacement, or convert
to a listed LED retrofit (Lesson 4) if the work order allows it.

## Dimmers

Incandescent and halogen lamps dim easily on a standard **phase-cut (forward-phase)**
dimmer. Things to check on a dimmer:

- **Wattage rating.** A 600 W dimmer can carry 600 W of incandescent load. When dimmers are
  ganged in one box and the side fins are broken off, the rating drops (derated). The dimmer's
  instructions list the derated values.
- **Lamp compatibility.** When incandescent lamps are converted to LED, the dimmer must be
  rated for LED loads (often marked "LED/CFL", "CL", or "ELV/MLV"). An old incandescent
  dimmer with LED lamps can cause flicker, buzzing, or poor low-end dimming.
- **Heat and buzzing.** A dimmer that is very hot, discolored, or makes loud buzzing is
  overloaded or failing and should be replaced.

Switches and dimmers are covered in depth in LT1-C09 Outlets & Switches.

## Key Takeaways
- Components: fixture, lamp, socket, wire, switch and thermal protector; learn how each fails.
- The socket's center contact (socket eye) can flatten and lose contact; lift it only de-energized, and replace a scorched socket.
- Recessed incandescent cans have thermal protectors; cycling means too much heat, usually from wrong wattage, wrong lamp type or insulation.
- Never bypass a thermal protector.
- Check dimmer wattage, derating and LED compatibility.
