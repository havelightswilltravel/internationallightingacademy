## The Cold Stress Equation

Our company's cold-weather training uses OSHA's simple equation:

> **Low temperature + wind speed + wetness = injuries and illness**

Any one of these makes the others worse. That is why you can get hypothermia when it is **above
freezing**: a cold rain, a damp shirt and a breeze on a 40 °F day can pull heat out of your body
faster than you make it. Our training treats **40 °F and below** as cold, **any wetness** (rain,
snow, sweat, wet ground) as a hazard, and **wind of 5 mph and up** as a hazard.

Lighting crews get hit with all three: a bucket at 40 feet catches more wind than the ground, and
night work on parking-lot lights means the coldest hours of the day.

There is no specific federal OSHA standard for cold. As with heat, OSHA enforces cold hazards
under the **General Duty Clause**, Section 5(a)(1) of the OSH Act, and our company follows OSHA and
NIOSH cold stress guidance.

## Wind Chill

Wind chill is the temperature exposed skin "feels" when wind is added. Use the National Weather
Service wind chill chart or a weather app at the morning huddle.

| Example | Wind chill | Frostbite risk to exposed skin |
|---|---|---|
| 30 °F, 15 mph | about 19 °F | Low |
| 0 °F, 15 mph | about −19 °F | Frostbite possible in about **30 minutes** |
| −10 °F, 20 mph | about −35 °F | Frostbite possible in about **10 minutes** |
| −20 °F, 30 mph | about −53 °F | Frostbite possible in about **5 minutes** |

Remember that **any moving air counts as wind**, including riding in an open bucket or the back
of a truck.

What our company does as the numbers drop (adapted from the cold-risk guidance in our training):

- **Wind chill 30 °F or below:** the lead reminds everyone about cold injuries at the huddle.
- **25 °F or below:** the lead checks that everyone has proper cold-weather clothing, and warm-up
  breaks in the heated cab are scheduled.
- **0 °F or below:** buddy checks for frostbite at regular intervals, shorter work cycles aloft.
- **−10 °F or below:** postpone non-essential outdoor work; essential work in pairs only, with
  frequent warm-up breaks. Talk to your supervisor before going up.

## Dressing for the Cold: C-O-L-D

Our cold-weather poster uses an easy acronym:

- **C - Keep it Clean.** Dirt and oil fill the air spaces in fabric that hold heat.
- **O - Avoid Overheating.** Sweat soaks your layers, and wet clothing loses its insulation. Open
  up or remove a layer **before** you start sweating, such as before climbing or hard work.
- **L - Wear it Loose and in Layers.** Tight clothing and boots cut off circulation. Layers trap
  warm air and let you adjust.
- **D - Keep it Dry.** Change wet or damp clothes as soon as you can.

### The three layers
1. **Inner (base) layer** that wicks sweat away from the skin: wool, silk or synthetic such as
   polypropylene. **No cotton** next to the skin in cold weather. Cotton holds water and stays cold.
2. **Middle layer** for insulation: wool, fleece or down that still insulates when a little damp.
3. **Outer layer** that blocks wind and lets some moisture escape: a windproof, water-resistant
   shell, high-visibility where required.

Plus: a **hard-hat liner** or balaclava under your hard hat (never a regular hat that stops the
hard hat from fitting correctly), and a neck gaiter to cover the face in wind. If FR clothing is
required for the task, your layers must meet that requirement too.

### Feet
- Insulated, **waterproof boots** with non-skid soles; not laced so tight they cut off blood flow.
- Keep socks **clean and dry**. **Carry an extra pair** in a dry bag and change damp socks right
  away. Do not stand on cold, wet ground any longer than you have to.

### Hands
Hands are a lighting tech's tools, and they are the first thing to go numb.
- Wear **liner gloves under insulated work gloves** so you can pull off the outer glove for fine
  work without going bare-handed.
- **Never touch cold metal with bare skin.** Fixture housings, poles, ladders and tools in freezing
  weather can cause frostbite on contact. Wear proper gloves when handling fuel too; spilled
  gasoline evaporating on skin freezes it fast.
- Carry **spare dry gloves**. Our electrical rule from LT1-C01 still applies: **wet gloves stop
  electrical work.**
- If your hands go numb, warm them under your clothes (armpits) or in the heated cab before you
  continue.
- Leave **metal jewelry** at home in winter; rings and watch bands chill fast and conduct cold
  (and current) into your skin. Pack a full change of **spare dry clothing**, and schedule outdoor
  work for the **warmest part of the day** when the job allows.

### Keep the furnace running
Keep moving, **eat your meals**, and drink water or warm, sweet, non-alcoholic drinks (you still
dehydrate in dry cold air). **No alcohol**: it makes you feel warm while increasing heat loss and
blunting shivering. Tobacco and nicotine reduce blood flow to the skin.

## Cold Injuries: Recognize and Respond

Watch yourself and your buddy. Our cold poster lists warning signs: skin that is red, swollen or
darkened; pain, itching or tenderness; numbness or tingling; blisters; or skin that is gray, waxy
or **"wooden" to the touch**. Body-wide warning signs are dizziness, vigorous shivering, clumsiness
and poor judgment.

### Frostbite
Freezing of the skin and deeper tissue, usually fingers, toes, ears, nose and cheeks. Skin turns
pale, waxy-white or grayish, hard and numb.
1. Get the person to a **warm, dry** place. Do not leave them alone.
2. Remove wet or tight clothing and jewelry from the area.
3. **Do not rub** the area, and do not rub it with snow. Rubbing damages frozen tissue.
4. **Do not thaw it if it may refreeze.** Thawing and refreezing does more damage than staying frozen.
5. Rewarm gently in **warm, not hot, water** (about 99–104 °F; it should feel comfortable to an
   unaffected hand) or with body heat. **Never** use a heater, open flame, hot engine or heating
   pad: numb skin burns easily.
6. Get medical care.

### Trench foot (immersion foot) and chilblains
- **Trench foot** comes from feet staying wet and cold for hours, even well above freezing (up to
  about 60 °F). Feet go cold and numb, then red, swollen and painful. Dry the feet, warm them in
  warm air, keep weight off them, and get medical care. Prevent it with dry socks and boots.
- **Chilblains** are red, itchy, swollen patches on skin repeatedly exposed to cold, damp air. Warm
  gently; do not rub.

### Hypothermia: a medical emergency
Body temperature drops to **95 °F or lower**. Early signs are uncontrolled shivering, fatigue and
clumsiness. Later: slurred speech, confusion, drowsiness, and **shivering may stop**, which is a
bad sign, not a good one.
1. **Call 911.**
2. Move the person to a warm, dry place (the heated van cab). Remove wet clothing and replace with
   dry clothing or wrap them in blankets, including the head (leave the face uncovered).
3. If they are **alert**, give warm, sweet, non-alcoholic drinks.
4. Put warm packs on the **center of the body**: chest, armpits, neck and groin.
5. **Do not rub** them or put them in a hot bath; handle gently. Do not give anything by mouth if
   they are not alert.
6. If they are unresponsive, check for breathing and start CPR if trained and needed.

> **Safety:** The person with frostbite or early hypothermia often does not notice it. The buddy
> check is your best defense. Look at each other's faces, ears and noses on every warm-up break.

## Key Takeaways
- Cold + wind + wet = cold injury; hypothermia can happen above freezing.
- Read the wind chill at the huddle; any moving air, including an open bucket, counts as wind.
- C-O-L-D: clean, avoid overheating, loose layers, dry. No cotton base layer.
- Liner gloves, spare dry gloves and socks; never touch cold metal bare-handed.
- Frostbite: warm gently in warm (not hot) water, never rub, don't thaw if it may refreeze.
- Hypothermia: call 911, warm the core gently, warm sweet drinks only if alert.
