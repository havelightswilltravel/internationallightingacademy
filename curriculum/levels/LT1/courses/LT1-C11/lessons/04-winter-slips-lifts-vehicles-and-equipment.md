## Slips and Falls Are the Most Common Winter Injury

Our company's cold-weather training is paired with a slips-and-trips session for a reason: many
cold-weather injuries are not frostbite at all. They are **falls on ice**: in parking lots, on
sidewalks, stepping out of the van, and on wet floors inside the customer's building. No matter how
well a lot is plowed, you will walk on something slippery in winter.

## Walking on Ice and Snow

### Dress for traction
- Wear **boots with non-skid rubber soles**. Avoid plastic and leather soles.
- On solid ice, use **slip-on ice cleats** over your boots for outdoor walking, and take them off
  before walking onto smooth indoor floors or climbing ladders and lift platforms, where metal studs
  can slide.
- Keep warm, but make sure your hood or hat does not block your **vision or hearing**; you need to
  see and hear traffic in the lot.
- Wear your **high-visibility** vest or jacket. Drivers in a dark, snowy lot are not expecting a
  person on foot. In daytime, sunglasses or tinted safety glasses help you see hazards in snow glare.

### Walk like a penguin
Our training's technique for slippery surfaces:
1. Point your feet **slightly outward** and keep your center of balance over your feet.
2. Take **short, slow steps** and keep your feet under you.
3. Use your feet as **probes** to feel for slick spots before putting your weight down.
4. Keep your **hands free** and out of your pockets so you can balance and catch a rail.
5. Use rails, the van, or other solid objects for support.
6. Carry **less**. A heavy toolbag or a stack of fixtures on your shoulder throws off your balance
   and hides your feet. Make two trips, use a cart, or get help.

### Black ice
Dew, fog or melted snow can refreeze into a thin, nearly invisible layer that looks like a **wet
spot** on the pavement. It shows up most often **early in the morning, in shaded areas** (the north
side of buildings, under canopies, around pole bases), on bridges and ramps, and where snowmelt
drains across the lot. Treat every "wet" patch in freezing weather as ice.

### If you fall
Try not to break the fall with straight arms; that is how wrists and elbows break. Roll with it,
and if you fall backward, **tuck your chin** so your head does not hit the ground. Report every fall,
even if you feel fine; head injuries can show up later.

## Getting In and Out of Vehicles

A big share of winter falls happen right at the van door.
- Use **three points of contact** getting in and out: two hands and a foot, or two feet and a hand,
  using the grab handle and step. Face the vehicle when climbing into a truck cab or bed.
- Look at the ground before you step out; you may be stepping onto ice.
- Never jump down from a truck bed, a utility box or a bucket truck's deck.
- Clear snow and ice off steps, running boards and the truck deck before using them.
- Do not climb onto a snowy van roof or ladder rack to free a ladder. Clear what you can from the
  ground, and use a step or the rear ladder with care.

## Inside the Customer's Building

- **Knock the snow off and wipe your boots** at the entrance; a tech tracking slush onto polished
  tile is a slip hazard for everyone, including the customer's staff.
- Highly polished floors (marble, terrazzo, ceramic tile, sealed concrete) are slippery even when dry
  and very slippery when wet. When you go from carpet to tile, or from dry to wet floor, **shorten
  your stride**.
- Watch for entrance mats that are not lying flat, and for wet spots near doors.
- If you create a wet area (melted snow off the ladder, a dropped drink), clean it up and put out a
  **wet floor sign** until it is dry. A sign is a warning, not a fix.
- Report slick floors or spills in your work area to the site contact.

## Lifts and Ladders in Winter

The fall-protection and lift rules from your aerial lift and ladder training do not change in
winter, but the conditions do.

- **Clear ice and snow** from the platform floor, toe board, guardrails, steps and controls before
  use. Never stand on ice in a bucket or platform.
- Check the **ground**: frozen ground can thaw during the day and soften under outriggers or wheels.
  Ice under a scissor lift's wheels can let it slide. Do not drive a lift onto ice or a slope beyond
  the manufacturer's rating.
- **Check the wind** against the lift's rating in the operator's manual (many outdoor-rated lifts
  are limited to about 28 mph, and many slab scissor lifts are rated for **indoor use only**).
  Winter fronts bring gusts, and wind is stronger aloft.
- **Ladders:** check the feet and rungs for ice and snow and clear them. Set feet on solid, cleared
  ground. Keep **three points of contact**; bulky gloves reduce your grip, so use gloves with a
  grippy palm. Do not wear ice cleats on ladder rungs.
- **Harness and lanyard** go on over your winter layers, and must be adjusted to fit snugly over
  them. A harness fitted over a T-shirt in summer will not fit right over a parka.
- Hydraulic oil is thick when cold. Let the lift warm up as the manufacturer directs, and expect
  slower functions. Report any lift that drifts or acts differently.
- Plastics get **brittle** in the cold. Handle lenses, diffusers and fixture housings gently.
- Fluorescent lamps may be slow to start or dim in cold outdoor fixtures; that is a cold-start issue,
  not necessarily a bad lamp.

## Batteries and Equipment

- Batteries lose capacity in the cold. **Keep cordless tool and meter batteries in the warm cab**
  and swap them as they fade.
- Most **lithium-ion batteries should not be charged below freezing** (32 °F); let them warm up
  first. Follow the tool maker's instructions.
- Lift batteries also lose capacity. Plan for shorter run time and charge as directed.
- A **meter or tester** that has been in a freezing van can read erratically or have a weak battery.
  Do your live-dead-live check on a known source as always; never assume a cold meter is accurate.

## Heaters and Carbon Monoxide

Our cold training includes carbon monoxide (CO) for a reason. CO from engines and fuel-burning
heaters has no smell or color. Signs are headache, dizziness, confusion, excessive yawning,
drowsiness and loss of consciousness.
- **Never** run the van, a generator, or a propane or kerosene heater in an enclosed space without
  ventilation. Never sleep in a running vehicle.
- If you warm up in an idling van, make sure the **exhaust pipe is not blocked** by snow.
- Use only company-approved heaters, kept away from anything flammable.
- If someone shows CO symptoms, get them to **fresh air** and call 911.

## Key Takeaways
- Slips on ice are the top winter injury: non-skid boots, short penguin steps, hands free, carry less.
- Treat every "wet" spot in freezing weather as black ice, especially in shade early in the day.
- Three points of contact in and out of vehicles and on ladders.
- Clear ice from lift platforms and ladders, check wind against the lift rating, refit your harness over winter layers.
- Keep batteries warm; don't charge lithium-ion below freezing.
- Never run engines or fuel heaters in enclosed spaces; keep the van exhaust clear.
