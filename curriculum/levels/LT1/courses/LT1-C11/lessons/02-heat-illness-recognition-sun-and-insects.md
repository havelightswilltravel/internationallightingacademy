## Watch Yourself, Watch Your Buddy

Heat sneaks up on people. Our company's heat training warns that its effects can build so
slowly that the danger is not obvious until it is too late. The person getting sick is often the
**last** to notice, because heat illness affects judgment. That is why every crew member watches
the others, and why the buddy check is part of every hot-weather job.

## The Heat Illnesses, From Mild to Deadly

### Sunburn
More than a skin problem: sunburned skin cannot release heat as well, so a burn makes heat illness
more likely the next day too.

### Heat rash and heat cramps
- **Heat rash:** red bumps where sweat stays on the skin. Keep skin dry, rest in a cool place.
- **Heat cramps:** painful spasms in the legs, arms or stomach after heavy sweating. Stop, rest in
  the shade, drink water or a sports drink, and do not go back to hard work for a while. Cramps are
  an early warning.

### Heat exhaustion
The body is losing the fight but is still trying to cool itself. Blood rushes to the skin and
less goes to the brain and organs. Signs:

- Heavy sweating; cool, moist, clammy or pale skin
- Headache, dizziness, light-headedness or fainting
- Weakness, extreme tiredness, thirst
- Upset stomach or vomiting
- Mood changes such as irritability or mild confusion
- Fast, weak pulse

Left untreated, heat exhaustion can turn into heat stroke.

### Heat stroke: a medical emergency
The body's thermostat has **failed**. Body temperature can rise high enough to cause brain damage
and death quickly unless the person is cooled right away. Signs:

- **Confusion**, slurred speech, strange behavior, or loss of consciousness
- **Hot skin**; skin may be dry with **no sweating**, but a worker doing hard labor can still be
  sweating heavily with heat stroke, so do not wait for dry skin
- Seizures or convulsions
- Very high body temperature

| | Heat exhaustion | Heat stroke |
|---|---|---|
| Mental state | Alert, maybe irritable | **Confused, slurred speech, passes out** |
| Skin | Cool, moist, clammy | **Hot**, often dry (may still sweat) |
| Urgency | Treat now, watch closely | **Call 911 now, cool immediately** |

> **Safety:** Any change in mental state in the heat (confusion, odd behavior, slurred speech,
> passing out) means **heat stroke until proven otherwise.** Call 911.

## What to Do

### For heat cramps or heat exhaustion
1. Stop work. If the person is aloft, bring the lift or bucket down **under control**. Someone
   dizzy should not climb down a ladder alone.
2. Move them to a **cool, shaded or air-conditioned** place, such as the van cab with the AC on.
3. Loosen or remove heavy clothing, harness and hard hat.
4. Give **cool water** to sip if they are alert and not vomiting.
5. Cool them with wet cloths, misting and fanning.
6. Stay with them. If they get worse, do not improve within about an hour, or cannot keep water
   down, get medical care. They are done with hard work for the day.

### For heat stroke
1. **Call 911** right away (or have someone else call while you start cooling).
2. **Cool them fast.** Move to shade or AC, remove outer clothing, and soak the skin with cold water
   or ice water. Put ice or cold packs on the neck, armpits and groin. Fan them. Cold-water
   immersion, where available, is the fastest method. Keep cooling until help arrives.
3. **Do not** give anything to drink if they are confused, unconscious or vomiting; they may
   choke.
4. Stay with them, and tell the paramedics what happened and how long they have been cooled.
5. Notify your supervisor and report it as an incident.

Our company's old heat card said to give cool drinking water for any heat illness. That is still
right for an alert person, but **not** for someone who is confused or unconscious. Our older
safety-meeting slides also said to "sprinkle the skin lightly with water" and "elevate the feet"
for heat stroke. Current OSHA and CDC guidance is to **cool aggressively**: soak the skin and
clothing with cold water, use ice packs and fanning, or immerse in cold water. Light sprinkling is
not enough, and raising the feet does not replace cooling.

## Sun Protection

Our company's sun-protection recommendations are built for crews who spend all day outside:

- **Cover up** with clothing that blocks light. Long, light-colored, breathable sleeves are often
  cooler than bare skin in strong sun.
- Wear a **hat with a brim** that shades the ears and neck. A **full-brim hard hat** (360-degree
  brim) is effective; a hard hat sunshade or neck flap helps. Ball caps leave the ears and back of
  the neck exposed, where skin cancers often develop. Keep your hard hat on in the bucket.
- Use a **broad-spectrum sunscreen**. Our older handout said SPF 15 or higher; most
  dermatologists now recommend **SPF 30 or higher**. Apply it about 15 minutes before going out and
  reapply at least every **two hours**, sooner if you are sweating it off.
- Wear **safety glasses that also block 99–100% of UV**. Most Z87 tinted lenses do; check the
  marking. Long-term UV exposure contributes to cataracts.
- Limit direct sun between about **10 a.m. and 4 p.m.** when you can, and seek shade on breaks.

Facts to remember:
- Up to about **80% of UV** passes through clouds. A cloudy day is not a safe day.
- UV is **reflected** off concrete, water, sand and snow, so you get it from below in a parking lot.
- UV is stronger at **higher elevation**.
- Check the daily **UV Index** for your ZIP code. Take sun protection seriously at **3 or higher**
  (our older handout said 5).

## Insects: Mosquitoes, West Nile and Stinging Insects

Outdoor lighting work is often done at **dusk and after dark** so we can see the lights work,
which is exactly when mosquitoes are most active. **West Nile virus** is spread mainly by mosquito
bites. Most infections are mild or have no symptoms, but some people get seriously ill.

- **Mild (West Nile fever):** headache, fever, body aches, swollen lymph nodes, skin rash.
- **Severe:** high fever, stiff neck, disorientation, tremors, convulsions, muscle weakness. Get
  medical help right away.

Our company follows OSHA's West Nile guidance:
- Wear **long sleeves, long pants and socks** when you can.
- Use an EPA-registered repellent with **DEET or picaridin** on exposed skin; choose a strength that
  lasts as long as you will be out. Spray it on the outside of clothing too, since mosquitoes bite
  through thin cloth.
- Do **not** spray repellent on skin under clothing, on cuts or irritated skin, in enclosed spaces
  or directly on your face. Keep it out of eyes and mouth.
- Skip perfume and cologne on outdoor jobs. Wash treated skin and clothes after work.
- Report standing water around the job (old tires, clogged drains, pole bases holding water).

Outdoor fixtures, pole hand holes and sign cabinets are also favorite spots for **wasps and
bees**. Look before you open a door or reach into a fixture. If you are allergic to stings, tell
your lead and carry your prescribed epinephrine.

## Key Takeaways
- Heat exhaustion: sweaty, clammy, dizzy, weak; cool down, sip water, rest, watch closely.
- Heat stroke: confused or passed out, hot skin; call 911 and cool fast; nothing by mouth if not alert.
- Any mental change in the heat is heat stroke until proven otherwise.
- Full-brim hard hat, sleeves, SPF 30+ sunscreen reapplied every two hours, UV-blocking safety glasses.
- At dusk, cover up and use DEET or picaridin; check fixtures for wasps before reaching in.
