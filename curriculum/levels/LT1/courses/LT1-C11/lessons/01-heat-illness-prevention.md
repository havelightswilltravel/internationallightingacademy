## Heat Is a Job Hazard, Not Just Discomfort

Our company's heat training starts with one blunt point: extreme heat is more than an issue of
discomfort. It forces your body into overdrive as it tries to stay cool by sweating. Lighting
work puts us right in the middle of it: parking-lot and site lighting from a bucket truck in full
sun, wall packs over hot asphalt, sign and canopy work, rooftop units, and attics or high bays in
un-air-conditioned warehouses where the air near the ceiling can be far hotter than the floor.

Heat illness is **preventable**. Every heat death on a job site is a failure of planning, water,
rest or attention. That is why we treat it like any other hazard on the pre-task plan.

### Who is at risk
Our training lists the usual risk factors, and every one of them applies to field crews:

- **Strenuous work** in hot weather, even for young, healthy workers
- **Not being used to the heat** (new hires, people back from vacation or illness, the first hot week of the year)
- Being overweight, in poor condition, or having heart disease or poor circulation
- **Fever, dehydration, or a recent sunburn**
- Some **prescription drugs**, and **alcohol** the night before
- Heavy, dark or non-breathable clothing, including some FR clothing and harnesses

> **Safety:** If you take medication, ask your doctor or pharmacist whether it affects how your body
> handles heat. You do not have to tell us what it is, but tell your lead you need extra breaks.

## Know the Number: Heat Index

The **heat index** is what the body feels when heat and humidity are combined. Humid air slows
sweat from evaporating, so your built-in cooling system stops working well. Two points from our
company's heat chart are worth memorizing:

- The heat index chart assumes **shade and a light breeze**. Working in **direct sun can add up to
  15 °F** to the heat index.
- A dry climate is not safe either. In low humidity your sweat evaporates so fast you may not
  notice how much water you are losing. In direct sun above about 90 °F, a working body can lose
  a large amount of water quickly.

| Heat index (shade) | Risk with prolonged exposure or physical work |
|---|---|
| 80–90 °F | **Caution:** fatigue possible |
| 90–103 °F | **Extreme caution:** heat cramps and heat exhaustion possible |
| 103–124 °F | **Danger:** heat cramps and heat exhaustion likely; heat stroke possible |
| 125 °F and up | **Extreme danger:** heat stroke highly likely |

Check the heat index with a weather app or the OSHA-NIOSH Heat Safety Tool app before you leave
the shop, and again in the afternoon. Say the number at the huddle.

## Water, Rest, Shade

OSHA's heat campaign boils prevention down to three words: **water, rest, shade**. Our company's
version is the same, with field detail:

### Water
- Drink **about 1 cup (8 oz) of cool water every 15–20 minutes** while working in the heat, even
  if you are not thirsty. That works out to roughly two to four glasses an hour, which is what our
  heat handout recommends during heavy work. Thirst shows up late.
- Do not try to make up for a dry morning by chugging at lunch. Steady sipping is what keeps you
  ahead. Do not drink more than about 48 oz (1.5 quarts) an hour for hours on end; too much plain
  water without food can upset your body's salt balance.
- On long, sweaty days, a sports drink or a normal meal replaces the salt you lose. If your doctor
  has you on a low-salt diet, ask before using sports drinks.
- Avoid alcohol, and go easy on energy drinks, heavy caffeine and heavy meals during the workday.
- **Company rule:** the van leaves the shop with enough cool water for the whole crew for the whole
  day. If the cooler runs low, refilling it is a work task, not a personal errand.

A normal adult needs roughly 3.7 liters (men) or 2.7 liters (women) of total water a day just
living a normal life. A day of hot fieldwork adds a lot on top of that.

### Rest
- **Schedule** rest breaks; do not wait until someone looks bad. The hotter the heat index and the
  harder the work, the more often you break.
- Plan the hardest work, such as pole and bucket work in full sun, for the **morning** and the
  cooler evening hours when the job allows. Save shaded or indoor tasks for mid-afternoon.
- Rotate tasks so one person is not stuck aloft in the sun or in a hot attic all day.

### Shade
- Rest in shade or in the air-conditioned cab, not in the sun next to the truck. **Company rule:**
  idling the truck to cool off with the A/C is an approved reason to idle.
- For long unshaded jobs (digging and setting pole bases, parking-lot work), set up one of the
  company's **pop-up canopies** so the crew has shade where the work is.
- Block direct sun or other heat sources where you can. Use fans in hot attics and high bays.
- Wear **lightweight, light-colored, loose** clothing that breathes, within the PPE the task needs.

## Acclimatization: The First Week Is the Most Dangerous

Most heat deaths on job sites happen in a worker's **first few days** on the job or in the first
heat wave of the season. The body needs about one to two weeks of gradual exposure to start
sweating sooner, sweat more efficiently and keep its heart rate down.

Following NIOSH guidance, our company builds up exposure:

- **New workers:** no more than about **20%** of a normal full heat exposure on day one, adding no
  more than 20% each day.
- **Experienced workers** returning or facing the first heat wave: about **50%** on day one, 60% on
  day two, 80% on day three, and full duty on day four.
- Someone back from a week or more away (vacation, illness) has lost some of their tolerance and
  is treated as returning.

In practice, the lead gives the new tech the shaded tasks, more breaks and a buddy, and does not
send them up the bucket in the afternoon on day one.

## The Rules: OSHA and the States

There is not yet a final federal OSHA heat standard. Heat hazards are enforced under the **General
Duty Clause**, Section 5(a)(1) of the OSH Act, which requires employers to protect workers from
recognized serious hazards. OSHA has also run a **National Emphasis Program** on heat since 2022.
OSHA proposed a federal heat injury and illness prevention rule in 2024; at the time this course
was written it was **not final**, and its status may change. Check current OSHA guidance.

Several **states have their own heat rules** that are stricter and more specific, including
California, Washington, Oregon, Colorado (agriculture), Minnesota (indoor), Maryland and Nevada.
They set things like heat-index triggers, required water and shade, rest breaks and written heat
plans. Our company's written heat plan follows the strictest rule where we work. If you work in
another state, ask your lead which rules apply there.

> **Safety:** You have stop-work authority for heat, just like for any other hazard. If you or a
> crew member is getting sick from the heat, stop, cool down, and tell the lead. No job is worth a
> heat stroke.

## Key Takeaways
- Heat illness is preventable; plan for it like any other hazard.
- Check the heat index; direct sun can add up to 15 °F.
- Water, rest, shade: about a cup of water every 15–20 minutes, scheduled breaks, shade or AC.
- New and returning workers acclimatize gradually (NIOSH: about 20% more exposure per day).
- No final federal heat standard yet; OSHA uses the General Duty Clause, and several states have their own heat rules.
