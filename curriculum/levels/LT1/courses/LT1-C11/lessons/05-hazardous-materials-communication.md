## What Our Hazmat Training Covers

Our company's annual "Hazmat" class is a **Hazard Communication** class. Its goal is to make
everyone familiar with the **materials** hazards of our work: the chemicals we use, the lamps and
ballasts we remove, and the chemicals we find at customer sites. Physical hazards (electrical,
lifts, fall protection, scaffolds) are covered in their own training.

This lesson adds what a field tech needs to know about **hauling** those materials in a company
vehicle under the U.S. Department of Transportation (DOT) rules. LT1-C06 lesson 05 covers
**how to pack** spent lamps, clean up a broken lamp and identify PCB ballasts; review it with this
lesson. LT5-C03 covers running the waste stream on large projects.

## The Rule: OSHA Hazard Communication (29 CFR 1910.1200)

OSHA's HazCom standard (aligned with the Globally Harmonized System, GHS) gives you the **right to
know** about the chemicals you work with. It requires:

1. **Labels** on every container of hazardous chemical
2. **Safety Data Sheets (SDS)** for every hazardous chemical, available to you during your shift
3. **Training** so you can read the labels and SDS and know how to protect yourself
4. A **written HazCom program** listing the chemicals in the workplace

Our older training called these sheets **MSDS** (Material Safety Data Sheets). Since OSHA's 2012
update they are **SDS**, in a standard **16-section** format. If you find an old MSDS, ask for the
current SDS.

### Where to find an SDS
**Company rule:** our shop keeps SDS copies for every product we use in a **marked binder at a
fixed spot in the warehouse**, and you should know exactly where it is. Many SDS can also be pulled
up on your phone from the manufacturer.

The SDS sections you will use most:
| Section | Tells you |
|---|---|
| 2 - Hazard identification | The hazards, signal word and pictograms |
| 4 - First-aid measures | What to do if it gets on you, in you or in your eyes |
| 6 - Accidental release | How to clean up a spill |
| 8 - Exposure controls / PPE | The gloves, glasses and ventilation you need |

### Reading the label
A GHS label shows the **product name**, a **signal word** ("**Danger**" for more severe hazards,
"**Warning**" for less severe), **pictograms** (red-bordered diamonds such as flame, exclamation
mark, corrosion, health hazard), **hazard statements**, **precautionary statements**, and the
supplier's contact information.

### Chemicals we actually use
Our company uses **very few hazardous chemicals**, but you will handle some: electrical contact
cleaner and degreaser in aerosol cans (often flammable), lens and fixture cleaners, adhesives,
sealants and caulk, spray paint for poles, penetrating oil, wire-pulling lubricant, battery
electrolyte, and gasoline for generators. Read the label before you use any product for the first
time.

> **Safety:** Many aerosol cleaners are **extremely flammable**. Never spray them into a fixture or
> panel that is energized or still hot, and let the solvent evaporate fully before restoring power.

### Secondary containers: company rule
**If you pour or transfer a chemical into a smaller container** (a spray bottle, a jar, a small
can), **label it** with the product name and its hazards. An unlabeled bottle of clear liquid in a
van is how people get hurt. Never put chemicals in drink bottles.

## Lamps and Ballasts Are Materials Hazards Too

These points from our hazmat class build on LT1-C06 lesson 05:

- **Intact lamps are safe to handle.** Some lamps contain hazardous materials such as **mercury,
  sodium or lead**.
- Under EPA rules, when we pick up spent lamps from a customer, our company becomes a
  **co-generator of universal waste.** We ship them to a licensed recycler. **Company policy: every
  lamp is recycled**, including low-mercury lamps.
- Lamps are **never** thrown in our dumpster **or the customer's dumpster**.
- Broken lamps can give off harmful mercury vapor. They go into the **designated sealed container
  or drum**. **Always use gloves and safety glasses** when handling broken lamps.
- Ballasts are generally safe to handle, but older ones may contain **PCBs**, a probable
  cancer-causing chemical used until 1979. Treat any ballast without a "No PCBs" label as PCB.
  Every removed magnetic ballast goes to the recycler (see LT1-C06 lesson 05).
- **Write the count of suspect PCB ballasts on the service order** so the office knows what is
  coming back.

### Leaking ballast: company procedure
1. **Stop.** Do not handle it with bare hands. Keep people away.
2. **Notify both the office and the customer's site contact.** Get instructions before removal,
   or wait for help.
3. When told to remove it: wear **chemical-resistant gloves** (nitrile or rubber) and safety glasses,
   with the circuit locked out and verified dead. **Double-bag** the ballast, and put your used
   gloves in the **outer bag**.
4. Have the site contact inspect any fixture or surface the oil touched to decide whether it must be
   replaced or cleaned by a qualified contractor.
5. Back at the shop, tell the supervisor who handles PCB waste so it is packaged to the recycler's
   instructions.

Our older class said PCB oil is clear and the black tar in most ballasts is harmless. Do not try to
judge by color. **Treat any leaking ballast as a stop-work condition** and follow the procedure.

## Hazards at the Customer's Site

You will find chemicals at the sites we service: pool and cleaning chemicals, fuels, paints,
process chemicals in plants, and sometimes asbestos or lead paint in older buildings. Customers are
covered by the same HazCom rules. Under 1910.1200, on a **multi-employer** site, each employer must
tell the others about hazards their workers could be exposed to.

- The **customer is responsible** for telling us about hazards at their facility, and their SDS
  should be available to us.
- **You are responsible** for following the instructions they give you.
- If you have questions about any material, **ask the site contact** and call your supervisor. If
  you smell something unusual, see a leak or feel sick, leave the area and report it.

## Hauling Hazmat in a Company Vehicle: DOT Awareness

DOT's **Hazardous Materials Regulations (49 CFR Parts 171–180)** cover moving hazardous materials on
public roads. Anyone who prepares hazmat shipments or signs shipping papers must have **DOT hazmat
employee training** (49 CFR 172.704), repeated at least every three years. At LT1 you do not prepare
or sign shipments; the office and the recycler do that. What you do need to know:

- **Materials of trade (49 CFR 173.6).** Small amounts of hazmat carried to support our work, such
  as a gas can for the generator, aerosol cans and a few cleaners, are allowed under a simpler set of
  rules: original or approved containers, **closed tight, secured** so they cannot tip or slide, and
  within the quantity limits. The driver must know what is on board.
- **Batteries.** Exit-sign and emergency batteries, and lithium tool batteries, must be packed so the
  **terminals cannot short-circuit**: tape the terminals or bag each battery separately, and keep them
  away from metal tools. Never ship a damaged or swollen lithium battery; set it aside and tell your
  supervisor. Keep spent batteries in their own container (they are universal waste too).
- **Lamps and ballasts.** Carry spent lamps in **closed, sturdy boxes** secured so they cannot slide
  or be crushed. Carry ballasts in a closed container. Suspect PCB ballasts and broken lamps are kept
  separate, as LT1-C06 lesson 05 describes. Bring them straight back to the shop; do not leave them in
  the van overnight or drop them anywhere else.
- If something spills or breaks in the van, stop in a safe place, ventilate, and call your supervisor.

## Key Takeaways
- Our hazmat class is HazCom: labels, SDS, training, written program (OSHA 1910.1200).
- MSDS are now SDS (16 sections). Know where the shop's SDS binder is.
- Label every secondary container. Read the label before first use.
- We are a co-generator of universal waste lamps; never use any dumpster; recycle every lamp.
- Leaking ballast: stop, notify office and site contact, chemical gloves, double-bag, gloves in the outer bag.
- In the van: tape battery terminals, secure and close every container, and leave shipping papers to trained staff.
