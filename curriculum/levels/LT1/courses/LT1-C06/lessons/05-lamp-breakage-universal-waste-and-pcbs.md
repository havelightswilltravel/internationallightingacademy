## Why Waste Handling Is Part of Your Job

Fluorescent, CFL, and HID lamps contain **mercury**, a toxic metal that can damage the nervous system
and the environment. Older ballasts may contain **PCBs** (polychlorinated biphenyls), which are
linked to cancer and other health effects. Federal and state environmental rules control how these
materials are stored, shipped, and disposed of. Mishandling them can harm people and lead to fines
for the company and the customer.

## Universal Waste Lamps

The U.S. EPA's **Universal Waste Rule (40 CFR Part 273)** allows mercury-containing lamps to be
managed under simplified rules as long as they are sent for recycling or proper disposal. Many
states have **stricter** rules (for example, requiring all fluorescent lamps to be recycled
regardless of mercury content), so always follow the company's procedure, which is written to meet
state rules.

### Packaging Spent Lamps

1. Use the **original new-lamp boxes** or containers supplied by the recycler.
2. Place lamps **intact**. Do not tape lamps together, break them, or crush them to save space.
   Intentional crushing is not allowed under universal waste rules except in very limited,
   specially permitted situations.
3. Keep containers **closed** except when adding lamps, and make sure they are structurally sound
   (no crushed corners or tears).
4. **Label** each container with **"Universal Waste – Lamp(s)"** (or "Waste Lamp(s)" / "Used
   Lamp(s)") as your procedure requires.
5. Mark the **accumulation start date** (the date the first lamp went in). Universal waste generally
   may not be accumulated for more than one year.
6. Store containers in a protected, dry area where they will not be crushed or knocked over.
7. Record lamp counts for the recycler's shipping paperwork (bill of lading or manifest).

| Item | Typical handling |
|---|---|
| Linear fluorescent, CFL, HID lamps | Universal waste lamps, recycled |
| Incandescent and halogen lamps | Usually regular trash unless local rules say otherwise |
| LED lamps and fixtures | Often recyclable as electronic waste; follow company and state rules |
| Emergency and exit sign batteries | Universal waste batteries (separate container) |

## Broken Lamp Cleanup

When a mercury-containing lamp breaks, a small amount of mercury vapor and mercury-bearing powder is
released. Quick, careful cleanup minimizes exposure. These steps follow EPA's broken-lamp cleanup
guidance; follow your company's written procedure where it differs.

### Before Cleanup
1. **Have people leave the area**, and keep them out.
2. **Ventilate:** open a window or door to the outdoors if available, and air out the area for
   **5–10 minutes**.
3. **Shut off forced-air heating or air conditioning** to the area, if possible, so vapor is not
   spread through the building.
4. Get the **broken-lamp kit:** stiff paper or cardboard, sticky tape, damp paper towels or wipes,
   and a glass jar with a metal lid or a sealable plastic bag. Wear gloves.

### During Cleanup
1. **Scoop up** glass and powder with the stiff paper or cardboard.
2. Use **sticky tape** to pick up small pieces and powder.
3. **Wipe** the area with damp paper towels or wipes.
4. Place all debris and cleanup materials in the **sealed container.**
5. **Do not use a vacuum or broom** for the initial cleanup of a hard surface; they spread mercury
   vapor and dust. On carpet, vacuum only after all visible material is removed, as the procedure
   allows, and dispose of the vacuum bag or contents with the debris.

### After Cleanup
- Label the sealed container per your procedure and manage it as your company directs (broken lamps
  are handled more strictly than intact ones).
- Continue to ventilate and keep HVAC off for several hours if practical.
- Wash your hands.
- **Report** the breakage on the work order and, if it happened in an occupied area, to the site contact.

> **Safety:** If a large number of lamps break (for example, a dropped box), or breakage occurs in a
> sensitive area (childcare, healthcare), clear the area and call your supervisor before cleanup.

## PCB Ballasts

PCBs were used as insulating fluid in ballast capacitors until their manufacture was banned in the
U.S. in 1979 under the Toxic Substances Control Act (TSCA). Many older buildings still have
original ballasts.

### Identifying PCB Ballasts
| Situation | Treat as |
|---|---|
| Label states **"No PCBs"** | Non-PCB ballast (still follow company recycling rules) |
| **No "No PCBs" statement**, or label unreadable, or manufactured before 1979 | **Assume it contains PCBs** |
| Oily residue, wet spots, or black tar-like potting material leaking | **Leaking PCB ballast: stop** |

### Handling
- Wear nitrile gloves and eye protection when handling suspect ballasts.
- **Non-leaking** suspect ballasts: place in the designated container (often a lined drum) for the
  company's PCB disposal or recycling vendor. Do not throw them in the trash, even if federal rules
  might allow it in small quantities; many states and our company require proper disposal.
- **Leaking** ballasts: do not handle without your supervisor. The ballast, the leaked material, and
  anything it contaminated (fixture parts, ceiling tiles, gloves) must be managed as PCB waste under
  40 CFR Part 761. Avoid skin contact and do not breathe fumes from an overheated or burned ballast.
- Document the number of PCB and non-PCB ballasts removed on the work order.

## Key Takeaways
- Mercury-containing lamps are universal waste: pack intact, keep closed, label, date, and recycle.
- Do not tape, break, or crush lamps.
- For a broken lamp: clear and ventilate the area, shut off HVAC, scoop and tape (no vacuum or broom first), seal, label, and report.
- Ballasts without a "No PCBs" label are assumed to contain PCBs.
- Leaking ballasts are a stop-work condition; get your supervisor and follow the PCB procedure.
