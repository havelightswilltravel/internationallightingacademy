## When More Than One Person Is Working

On larger lighting jobs, several technicians may work on fixtures fed by the same circuits.
**Every person exposed to the hazard must be protected by their own lock.** OSHA requires group
lockout procedures that give each authorized employee the same level of protection as personal
lockout (29 CFR 1910.147(f)(3)).

### Method 1: Multi-Lock Hasp
1. The breaker lockout device is installed on the breaker.
2. A **hasp** (a scissor-style device with holes for several locks) is placed through the lockout
   device.
3. Each worker attaches their own lock and tag to the hasp.
4. The breaker cannot be turned on until **every lock** is removed.

### Method 2: Group Lockbox
Used when there are many isolation points (for example, 12 lighting breakers in three panels).

1. The **primary authorized employee** (usually the lead technician or supervisor) locks and tags
   each isolation point with company locks.
2. The keys for those locks go into a **group lockbox**.
3. The primary authorized employee verifies isolation and absence of voltage, and documents it.
4. **Each worker attaches their personal lock** to the lockbox before starting work.
5. Each worker should be able to verify, or have explained to them, that the isolation is effective.
   Many companies let each worker verify absence of voltage at their own work location.
6. The lockbox cannot be opened until every worker has removed their lock.

> **Safety:** Adding your lock to the hasp or lockbox is not optional. If your lock is not on it,
> you are not protected. Never work on a circuit under someone else's lock alone.

## Shift and Personnel Changes

When a job continues across shifts or crews, protection must be continuous (29 CFR 1910.147(f)(4)).
The incoming worker applies their lock **before** the outgoing worker removes theirs, so there
is never a moment without a lock in place. The outgoing and incoming workers should also hand off
information: which circuits are locked out, what work is complete, and any hazards found.

## Removing Someone Else's Lock (Supervised Removal)

Sometimes a worker leaves the site with their lock still on. OSHA allows removal by someone else
**only** under a specific procedure (29 CFR 1910.147(e)(3)) that typically requires:

1. The **supervisor** (not a coworker) directs the removal.
2. Verification that the worker who applied the lock is **not at the facility.**
3. All reasonable efforts to **contact the worker** to tell them the lock is being removed.
4. A check that the equipment is safe to energize (all people clear, fixture assembled).
5. Making sure the worker **is informed before they return to work** that their lock was removed.
6. Documentation of the removal.

Never cut a lock yourself, even if you are certain the work is done.

## Special Situations in Lighting Work

### Fixtures Fed by More Than One Circuit
Some fixtures have separate circuits for normal and emergency operation, or for two levels of
switching (inboard/outboard lamps). Identify and lock out **all** feeds, and test every conductor
in the fixture.

### Emergency Lighting
Fixtures with battery packs, central inverter circuits, or generator-fed emergency circuits may stay
energized when the normal breaker is off. Emergency circuits are often in a **separate panel**.
Before taking emergency lighting out of service, notify the building owner. This affects life
safety, and the work may require alternate measures (LT3-C04).

### Lighting Contactors and Control Panels
A contactor or relay panel may turn circuits on automatically by time clock, photocell, or building
automation. Turning off the contactor is **not** isolation. Lock out the breaker that feeds the
circuit, not the control.

### Multiwire Branch Circuits
Several circuits may share one neutral. Opening only one breaker can leave the shared neutral
carrying current from the others. The NEC requires a means to simultaneously disconnect all
ungrounded conductors of a multiwire branch circuit (handle ties or multi-pole breakers), but older
installations may not comply. If you find a shared neutral, stop and get your supervisor (LT4-C04).

### Tag-Only Situations
If a lock truly cannot be applied, the written procedure will require additional protection, such
as removing a fuse or disconnecting conductors, done by a qualified person. LT1 technicians do not
perform tag-only lockouts.

## When Things Do Not Add Up

Stop work and call your supervisor if:

- The breaker is not labeled or the label does not match what you see.
- Fixtures stay on after the breaker is off, or other things go off unexpectedly.
- You measure voltage after lockout.
- A lockout device does not fit the breaker securely.
- Someone asks you to work without your lock in place "just for a minute."

## Key Takeaways
- Every exposed worker attaches their own lock, either to a hasp or a group lockbox.
- During shift changes, the incoming lock goes on before the outgoing lock comes off.
- Only a supervisor can direct removal of another worker's lock, following the written procedure.
- Lock out all feeds, including emergency circuits; controls and contactors are not isolation.
- Stop and get help whenever the circuit does not behave as expected.
