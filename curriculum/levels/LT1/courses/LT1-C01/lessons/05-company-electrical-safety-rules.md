## Treat It as Live

Our handbook and safety training start with the same rule: **treat all electrical circuits and
equipment as live.** Do not adjust or repair anything electrical unless it is part of your job
and your assignment. Report hazards and maintenance needs to your supervisor or the site's
maintenance staff as soon as possible, and call the safety director if a severe hazard exists.

Two more rules come straight from our handbook:

- **Never work on a fixture or other electrical part if you feel you cannot do it safely.**
  When in doubt, stop and call your supervisor or an electrician.
- **Never attempt a task you have not been trained on.** Watching someone else do it once is
  not training. Our LOTO training lists "feeling they can do the task after watching someone
  else" as a common cause of accidents.

Remember that **even low voltage can kill**: it is the current that hurts you, and wet or sweaty
skin lets far more of it through (Lessons 3 and 4).

## De-Energize First

Company policy is to **de-energize every circuit before working on it** and follow the company
lockout/tagout procedure (LT1-C06). Keep covers on all fixtures and wiring unless they are
removed for service by an authorized person, and only after all energy sources are isolated.
When the lights are off for your work, bring **portable work lights** so you are not working in
the dark.

Working live is the exception, and it is not LT1 work. Only trained, qualified and authorized
technicians work on energized systems, and only with a written safety plan approved by a
supervisor and the company's **Energized Electrical Work Permit** (LT4-C07). If a job cannot be
locked out, you stop and call your supervisor.

## Hot Gloves: Our Company's Rule

Lesson 3 introduced the rubber insulating "hot" gloves our company provides. Here is the
company rule for using them:

> **Our rule:** If you have **not personally locked and tagged out** the circuit you are
> servicing, wear your hot gloves.

Examples from our training of when hot gloves are worn:

- working in panels, switchgear or ground boxes,
- working on neon, or climbing inside a sign,
- whenever you could become grounded, such as reaching through a T-bar ceiling, working against
  water pipes or reaching into a hand hole,
- **testing for absence of voltage**, since a circuit is live until proven dead.

How to wear and care for them:

- Three layers: a **cotton liner**, the **rubber insulating glove**, and the **leather
  protector** over it. Wear the leather protectors **at all times** with hot gloves; the rubber
  is what protects you, and the leather protects the rubber.
- **Inspect and air-test before and after each use.** Look for cuts, punctures, swelling,
  cracking or embedded debris.
- Report damage or replacement needs to the office **immediately**. Never use a glove that is
  past its test date (29 CFR 1910.137).

Hot gloves are a backup, not a plan. For an LT1, the answer is still to work only on circuits
**you** have locked out and verified. When unsure, ask your supervisor.

> **Safety:** Cloth-insulated wiring (found in older buildings) is brittle and can crumble when
> moved, exposing bare copper. Company policy: **always lock out** around cloth wiring, and wear
> hot gloves along with your normal PPE. Report it to your supervisor.

## Ladders and Being Grounded

- Use **non-conductive (fiberglass) ladders** for electrical work. Do not use metal ladders near
  electrical installations (29 CFR 1926.1053(b)(12); 1910.333(c)(7)).
- Rubber feet on an articulating, multi-position ladder **do not guarantee** you are isolated
  from shock. Many have aluminum rails. Talk to your supervisor before using one for electrical
  work, and never let wires or other conductive material bridge between the ladder and ground.
- Where your body could touch grounded metal (ceiling grid, ducts, conduit, a lift platform),
  de-energize, or use barriers such as insulating mats and gloves.

## Tools and Extension Cords

Our company uses only **grounded or double-insulated** power tools. Metal-housed tools must have
an intact three-prong ground pin, and **ground pins are never removed.** Before using an
extension cord, follow our handbook's checklist:

1. Inspect for breaks, burns, cuts, cracks and the condition of the plug. **A cord that fails
   gets red-tagged and its ends cut off** so it can never be used again.
2. Make sure the tool's rating does not exceed the cord's rating.
3. Do not run cords through doorways or holes in ceilings, walls or floors, and do not attach
   them to walls, ceilings or doors.
4. Never bend, remove or modify the prongs.
5. Do not use cords in wet conditions unless they and the equipment are rated for it.
6. Do not plug one extension cord into another.
7. Do not drive over, drag, step on or set things on a cord.
8. Unplug the cord when you are finished. Cords are **temporary** power only, never permanent wiring.

On construction sites, use GFCI protection or the assured equipment grounding conductor program
(29 CFR 1926.404(b)(1)). Never use spark-producing tools in hazardous atmospheres.

## Around the Panel

- Keep at least **3 feet of clear working space** in front of electrical panels, more at higher
  voltages (29 CFR 1910.303(g)). Never stack materials or trash there.
- Breakers and switches should be labeled for their purpose. Report unlabeled or wrong labels.
- Never use water on an electrical fire. Use a **Class C**-rated extinguisher and shut off the
  power if you can. **Know where the extinguisher is on your truck** before the job starts.

Our safety inspection form also asks: Are switch and junction box covers on? Any exposed wires,
frayed cords or overheating switches and fixtures? Are GFCIs near water working, and are breaker,
fuse and cord sizes right for the load? Check these on every job.

## If Someone Is Shocked

1. Do not touch them. **Shut off the power** if you can do it quickly.
2. If you cannot, use a **dry non-conductive item** (dry rope, a dry wooden stick, dry clothing)
   to separate them from the source.
3. Call 911 or send for help.
4. Check breathing and pulse; give CPR/AED and first aid if trained until help arrives.
5. Report it. Anyone who is shocked gets medical evaluation (Lesson 2).

## Key Takeaways
- Treat all circuits as live; never do a task you are not trained for or feel is unsafe.
- De-energize and lock out first; working live is for qualified people with a permit, never LT1.
- Company rule: if you did not personally lock and tag it, wear hot gloves, with leather
  protectors, inspected before and after each use.
- Fiberglass ladders, grounded or double-insulated tools, and inspected cords; red-tag and cut bad cords.
- Keep 3 feet clear in front of panels, know where your truck's extinguisher is, and never touch
  a shock victim who is still in contact.
