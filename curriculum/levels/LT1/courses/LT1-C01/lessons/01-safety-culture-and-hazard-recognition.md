## Why Safety Comes First in Lighting Work

Lighting work looks simple from the floor: change a lamp, swap a ballast, move on. But every
lighting job combines three of the most serious hazards in the trades: **electricity, heights,
and moving equipment or people below you.** Electrocutions and falls are consistently among
the leading causes of death in construction and maintenance work in the United States. Most
of those incidents were not caused by rare, unpredictable events. They were caused by
shortcuts that "always worked before."

A **safety culture** is what people do when nobody is watching. In our company it means:

- Every person, on their first day, has the authority and the duty to **stop work** if
  something is unsafe. No one will be disciplined for a good-faith stop.
- We plan the job before we start it.
- We report near misses so the next person does not get hurt.
- We never assume a circuit is dead. We test it.
- Production never outranks safety. If the schedule and safety conflict, safety wins and
  you call your supervisor.

> **Safety:** You are an entry-level technician. You are **not** qualified to work on or near
> exposed energized parts. If a task would require it, stop and get your supervisor.

## The Hazard Hunt: Look Before You Work

Before you set up a ladder or open a fixture, take two minutes to scan the area. Use this
top-to-bottom routine:

1. **Overhead:** What is above the fixture? Sprinkler pipes, ductwork, cable trays, open
   junction boxes, other fixtures, overhead lines outdoors.
2. **At the fixture:** Is the lens cracked? Are there burn marks, a burning smell, melted
   sockets, or dangling wires? Is the fixture securely supported?
3. **Working height:** What will you be standing on? Is the ladder right for the height?
4. **Floor:** Is it level, dry, and solid? Is there traffic: people, carts, forklifts, doors
   that swing into your ladder?
5. **Surroundings:** Is the electrical panel accessible? Are there chemicals, hot surfaces,
   noise, or customer equipment you could damage?
6. **People:** Who else is working nearby? Who needs to know the lights or a circuit will be off?

## Common Hazards on Lighting Jobs

| Hazard | Where you find it | Basic control |
|---|---|---|
| Electric shock | Sockets, ballast wiring, damaged fixtures, panels | De-energize, lockout/tagout, test before touch |
| Arc flash | Electrical panels, disconnects | Stay out of open panels; only qualified persons work there |
| Falls | Ladders, lifts, mezzanines, roof edges | Correct ladder, 3 points of contact, fall protection |
| Cuts | Broken lamps, sheet-metal fixture edges | Cut-resistant gloves, eye protection |
| Mercury exposure | Broken fluorescent, CFL, and HID lamps | Broken-lamp cleanup procedure, ventilation |
| Falling objects | Tools, lenses, lamps dropped from height | Hard hat, barricade the area, tool lanyards |
| Struck-by vehicles | Warehouses, parking lots | High-visibility vest, cones, a spotter |
| Heat / burns | Lamps and ballasts that just turned off, HID fixtures | Let fixtures cool; gloves |
| Asbestos / lead | Older ceilings and paint | Do not disturb suspect material; ask first |

## The Pre-Task Plan (Job Hazard Analysis)

Most jobs start with a written **pre-task plan** or **job hazard analysis (JHA)**. It does not
need to be long. It answers four questions:

1. What are the steps of this job?
2. What can go wrong at each step?
3. What will we do to prevent it (controls and PPE)?
4. Who do we call, and where do we go, if someone gets hurt?

Fill it out honestly, review it with your crew, and update it when conditions change: a new
area, a new task, a different ladder, or a surprise like an energized circuit you did not
expect.

## The Hierarchy of Controls

When we fix a hazard, we try the most effective option first:

1. **Elimination** – remove the hazard entirely (de-energize the circuit).
2. **Substitution** – use something less hazardous (a lift instead of a tall ladder, an LED
   lamp instead of a mercury lamp).
3. **Engineering controls** – physical barriers, guards, insulated covers.
4. **Administrative controls** – procedures, training, signs, scheduling work after hours.
5. **PPE** – the last line of defense, not the first.

Notice that de-energizing is at the top. That is why lockout/tagout (covered in LT1-C06) is
the foundation of everything we do.

## Stop-Work Authority

Use stop-work authority whenever you see:

- An unexpected voltage reading or a circuit you cannot identify.
- A damaged ladder, tool, or meter.
- Someone working without required PPE or fall protection.
- A change in conditions the plan did not cover.
- Anything that makes you uneasy and that you cannot explain.

How to do it: say clearly and calmly, "Stop, I have a safety concern." Make the area safe if
you can do so without risk, explain what you saw, and contact your supervisor. Then the team
fixes the issue before restarting.

## Key Takeaways
- Electricity, falls, and people or vehicles below you are the big three lighting hazards.
- Every technician has the authority and the duty to stop unsafe work.
- Scan the work area top to bottom before every task and complete the pre-task plan.
- Use the hierarchy of controls: de-energizing beats PPE every time.
- An LT1 does not work on or near exposed energized parts. Stop and call your supervisor.
