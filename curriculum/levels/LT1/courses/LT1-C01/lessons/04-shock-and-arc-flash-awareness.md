## Why Electricity Hurts

Electric current flows when your body becomes part of a circuit, for example when one hand
touches an energized conductor and your other hand or your feet touch something grounded
(a metal ceiling grid, a conduit, a damp floor, a ladder with metal rails). The **current**
passing through the body causes the injury. Voltage is what pushes that current, and
building voltages of 120 V and 277 V are more than enough to kill.

### Effects of Current on the Body (approximate, 60 Hz AC, hand-to-foot)

| Current | Likely effect |
|---|---|
| About 1 mA | Faint tingle; threshold of feeling |
| About 5 mA | Slight shock; not painful, but startling. Startle reactions cause falls from ladders. |
| Roughly 6–30 mA | Painful shock; muscle control lost. The "let-go" threshold is in this range; above it, you may be unable to release the conductor. |
| About 50–150 mA | Extreme pain, breathing may stop, severe muscle contractions; death is possible |
| About 1,000–4,300 mA | Ventricular fibrillation (heart stops pumping effectively); likely fatal without immediate CPR/AED |

For scale: a 20-amp lighting circuit breaker is designed to trip at **20,000 mA**. A breaker
protects the wiring, **not you.** A GFCI trips at about 4–6 mA and gives much better shock
protection, but most lighting circuits are not GFCI-protected.

Other shock injuries include deep internal burns along the current path, heart rhythm problems
that appear hours later, and secondary injuries from falls. That is why anyone who is shocked
must get medical evaluation.

> **Safety:** If someone is being shocked, **do not touch them.** De-energize the circuit if you
> can do it quickly and safely, or use a dry nonconductive object to separate them only if
> there is no other option and you can do it safely. Call 911. Start CPR/AED if trained and
> the person is not breathing.

## Arc Flash and Arc Blast

An **arc flash** happens when current jumps through the air between conductors or to ground,
for example when a tool slips inside a panel, a breaker fails, or a meter on the wrong
setting is applied to a live bus. The arc can reach temperatures commonly cited around
35,000 °F, hotter than the surface of the sun. It produces:

- Intense heat that causes severe burns several feet away and ignites clothing.
- A pressure wave (**arc blast**) that can throw a worker, rupture eardrums, and launch
  molten metal and parts.
- Blinding light and toxic gases.

Arc flash incidents happen most often during tasks inside **panels, switchboards,
disconnects, and motor control equipment**, especially when covers are removed, during
testing, or when equipment is operated in poor condition.

## NFPA 70E Approach Boundaries

NFPA 70E defines distances around exposed energized parts:

| Boundary | What it means for an LT1 |
|---|---|
| **Arc flash boundary** | Distance at which a person could receive a second-degree burn from an arc flash (incident energy of 1.2 cal/cm²). Inside it, arc-rated PPE is required. Shown on the equipment's arc-flash label. |
| **Limited approach boundary** | Shock-protection boundary. **Unqualified persons stay outside** unless continuously escorted and advised by a qualified person. For common building voltages (about 50–750 V), this is 3 ft 6 in from fixed exposed parts and 10 ft from exposed movable conductors such as overhead lines. |
| **Restricted approach boundary** | Closer shock boundary that only qualified persons, with proper PPE and planning, may cross. Unqualified persons **never** cross it. |

A **qualified person** has the training and demonstrated skill to recognize and avoid the
electrical hazards of specific equipment and tasks. At LT1 you are **unqualified** for
energized work. Your work is done only after circuits are placed in an electrically safe work
condition (LOTO, LT1-C06).

## Reading an Arc-Flash Label

Equipment such as panelboards often carries a label that may show: nominal voltage, arc
flash boundary, incident energy (cal/cm²) at a working distance **or** the required PPE
category, limited and restricted approach boundaries, and the date of the study. If you see a
label, it is telling you how dangerous the equipment is with the cover off. If you see an
**open panel** with exposed live parts, stay back and tell your supervisor.

## What You Can Do Right Now

1. Treat every conductor and fixture as energized until it has been locked out and tested.
2. Never remove a panel cover (dead front) or reach into an open panel.
3. Do not operate a breaker that shows signs of damage, heat, or burning smell; report it.
4. Stand to the side when a qualified person operates a disconnect.
5. Watch for warning signs: buzzing, burning smell, discoloration, melted insulation.
6. Keep metal tools, tape measures, and ladders away from exposed energized parts.

## Key Takeaways
- Current through the body causes injury; even 120 V and 277 V circuits can kill.
- Breakers protect wiring, not people. A few milliamps can freeze you on a conductor.
- Arc flash produces extreme heat, blast pressure, molten metal, and light.
- Unqualified persons stay outside the limited approach boundary and never cross the restricted approach boundary.
- Anyone who receives a shock must be medically evaluated.
