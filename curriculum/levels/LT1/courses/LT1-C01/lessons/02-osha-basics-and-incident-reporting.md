## What OSHA Is and Why It Matters to You

The **Occupational Safety and Health Administration (OSHA)** is the federal agency that sets
and enforces workplace safety rules in the U.S. Some states run their own OSHA-approved
"state plans" that must be at least as effective as federal OSHA, and some have stricter rules.

Two sets of OSHA rules apply to our work:

| Standard | Covers | Examples for lighting work |
|---|---|---|
| **29 CFR 1910** – General Industry | Maintenance and service in existing buildings | Relamping, fixture repair, LOTO (1910.147), electrical safety-related work practices (1910.331–.335), PPE (1910.132–.138) |
| **29 CFR 1926** – Construction | Construction, alteration, renovation | New installations, major retrofits, ladders (1926.1053), fall protection (Subpart M) |

Which standard applies depends on the work: routine maintenance generally falls under 1910,
while construction and major alteration falls under 1926. Many lighting retrofits are treated
as construction. Your supervisor will tell you which rules govern a job; when in doubt, follow
the stricter requirement.

OSHA also recognizes consensus standards. The one you will hear most often for electrical
safety is **NFPA 70E, Standard for Electrical Safety in the Workplace**. OSHA rules say *what*
must be achieved (for example, protecting workers from electrical hazards); NFPA 70E gives
detailed methods for *how* to do it.

## Your Rights and Responsibilities

**You have the right to:**

- A workplace free from recognized serious hazards.
- Training in a language and vocabulary you understand.
- Required PPE provided by the employer (with limited exceptions such as ordinary safety-toe
  footwear and prescription safety eyewear, depending on company policy).
- See the Safety Data Sheets (SDS) for chemicals you work with.
- Report injuries and hazards, and file a complaint with OSHA, **without retaliation.**

**You are responsible for:**

- Following company safety rules and the training you receive.
- Wearing and caring for your PPE.
- Reporting hazards, injuries, and near misses promptly.
- Not removing guards, bypassing safety devices, or using damaged equipment.

## Hazard Communication (HazCom)

Lighting work involves some chemicals: cleaners, lubricants, adhesives, and the mercury in
lamps. OSHA's Hazard Communication standard (1910.1200) requires that containers be labeled
and that **Safety Data Sheets** be available. Before using an unfamiliar product, read the
label and SDS for hazards, PPE, and first aid.

## What Counts as an Incident or a Near Miss

| Term | Meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Injury / illness** | Someone is hurt or becomes ill from work | Cut from a broken lamp, shock, sprained ankle off a ladder |
| **Property damage** | Equipment or customer property is damaged | Ladder gouges a customer's wall, fixture drops onto a desk |
| **Near miss** | Something that could have caused harm but did not | Lens slips and falls in a barricaded area; a "dead" circuit tests live |
| **Unsafe condition** | A hazard that exists right now | Missing panel cover, damaged extension cord |

**Near misses are free lessons.** For every serious injury there are typically many near
misses that came before it. Reporting them lets us fix the cause before someone gets hurt.

## How to Report

1. **Take care of people first.** Call 911 for any serious injury, shock with symptoms, burn,
   fall from height, or loss of consciousness. Give first aid only within your training.
2. **Make the scene safe.** De-energize if you can do it safely, barricade, and keep others out.
3. **Notify your supervisor immediately**, by phone, not just text, for any injury, any
   electrical shock (even a "small" one), or any significant damage.
4. **Report in writing the same day** using the company incident or near-miss form/app.
5. **Preserve the scene** and take photos if it is safe. Do not throw away the damaged tool
   or lamp.
6. **Cooperate with the investigation.** The goal is to find causes, not to blame people.

> **Safety:** Anyone who receives an electrical shock should be evaluated by a medical
> professional, even if they feel fine. Electrical current can cause internal injuries and heart
> rhythm problems that are not obvious right away.

### Writing a Good Report

Write what happened, not opinions. Compare:

- Weak: "Ladder was bad, almost fell."
- Strong: "At 10:15 in Room 204, the right rear foot of the 6-ft fiberglass stepladder
  (asset #L-12) slipped on a wet tile floor while I was on the second step. I stepped down
  without falling. No injury. Ladder tagged out; floor was wet from mopping with no sign posted."

Include: date, time, exact location, people involved, equipment (with asset numbers), what
you were doing, what happened, and what you did right afterward.

## Employer Reporting to OSHA

Employers must report work-related **fatalities to OSHA within 8 hours**, and **in-patient
hospitalizations, amputations, or loss of an eye within 24 hours** (29 CFR 1904.39). This is
one reason your supervisor needs to hear about serious incidents immediately.

## Key Takeaways
- OSHA 1910 covers general industry/maintenance; 1926 covers construction. NFPA 70E explains how to meet electrical safety requirements.
- You have the right to training, PPE, hazard information, and to report without retaliation.
- Report every injury, shock, damage event, and near miss to your supervisor the same day.
- Anyone who is shocked gets medical evaluation.
- Good reports are specific, factual, and written promptly.
