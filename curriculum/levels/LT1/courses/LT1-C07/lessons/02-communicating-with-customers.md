## Why Communication Matters

Customers rarely judge the technical quality of a ballast replacement. They judge whether you
**listened, explained clearly, kept them informed, and did what you said you would.** Most
complaints come from poor communication rather than poor work.

## The Basics

- **Introduce yourself** with your name and the company: "Hi, I'm Jordan with [Company]. I'm here
  about the lighting service request."
- **Listen first.** Let the customer describe the problem fully before you respond. Ask clarifying
  questions: Which fixtures? Since when? Does it happen all the time or only sometimes? Anything else
  changed recently?
- **Repeat back** what you heard: "So the four fixtures in the back hallway flicker, mostly in the
  morning, starting last week. Got it."
- **Set expectations**: what you will do, how long it will take, whether lights or power will be off,
  and when you will check back in.

## Plain Language, Not Jargon

Customers do not need to know what a programmed-start ballast is. They need to know what is wrong,
what you will do, and what it means for them.

| Jargon | Plain language |
|---|---|
| "The ballast is shot." | "The part inside the fixture that powers the lamps has failed, so I'll replace it." |
| "I need to LOTO the circuit." | "I'll need to turn off and lock the power to these lights for about 30 minutes while I work." |
| "Those are T12s on a mag ballast." | "These are an older type of fixture. Parts are getting hard to find, so upgrading them may be worth considering. I'll note it for my supervisor." |
| "There's a ground fault." | "There's an electrical problem in this circuit that needs to be checked by our electrician." |

## What You Can and Cannot Promise

At LT1, you represent the company but you do not set prices or change scope.

- **Do:** explain what you found and what you did, and note recommendations on the work order.
- **Do not:** quote prices, promise discounts, or agree to extra work that is not on the work order.
  Say: "That's a great question. I'll make a note and have the office follow up with you today."
- **Do not** diagnose beyond your training or guess. "I'm not sure; I'll have a senior technician
  look at it" is a professional answer.
- **Do not** criticize previous contractors or the building's electrical system. State facts:
  "This fixture has a damaged socket that needs repair."

## Keeping the Customer Informed

- **Before shutting off lights or power**, tell the site contact and the people affected.
- **If you find a problem** that changes the job (wrong parts, damaged wiring, unsafe condition),
  stop and tell your supervisor first, then inform the customer as your supervisor directs.
- **If the job will take longer** than expected, say so as soon as you know.
- **Before you leave**, review what was done, what still needs attention, and what happens next.

## Handling Upset Customers

Customers get frustrated when lights have been out for days, when work disrupts their business,
or when a problem comes back. Use the **LAST** approach:

1. **Listen** without interrupting. Let them finish.
2. **Acknowledge** their frustration: "I understand. Having half the warehouse dark makes it hard
   to work safely."
3. **Solve** what you can, or explain the next step: "I've replaced these lamps. The two fixtures on
   the end need a part I don't have; I've requested it and our office will call you to schedule."
4. **Thank** them for their patience, and follow through.

Never argue, raise your voice, or take it personally. If a customer becomes abusive or threatening,
calmly leave the area and call your supervisor.

## Phone, Text, and Email

- Use **company-approved** channels for customer communication.
- Keep messages short, professional, and factual. Avoid slang and emojis.
- Do not share customer information, building access codes, or photos of the site on personal
  social media. Many facilities have strict security and privacy requirements.

## Communicating With Your Team

Good communication inside the company matters too:

- Confirm assignments and ask questions when instructions are unclear.
- Tell your supervisor right away about parts shortages, delays, injuries, and safety concerns.
- Hand off clearly at shift changes or when another technician takes over.
- Speak up during pre-task briefings; your observations count.

## Key Takeaways
- Introduce yourself, listen fully, repeat back, and set expectations.
- Explain problems in plain language; avoid jargon.
- Do not quote prices or agree to extra work; refer it to the office.
- Keep the customer informed before shutdowns, when scope changes, and before you leave.
- Handle frustration with Listen, Acknowledge, Solve, Thank, and get help if things escalate.
