## Why Our Company Has Its Own Rules

OSHA sets the minimum. Our company's fall protection policy, from the safety handbook and
our fall protection training, adds rules based on the work we actually do: service calls,
retrofits, and fixture replacements in occupied buildings, parking lots, and on rooftops.

## Equipment We Provide

- Our company **provides safety harnesses and lanyards** and trains you on how to use them
  (OSHA 29 CFR 1910.30 and 1926.503 require that training).
- Our company **provides relief steps (trauma straps) installed on every harness** and
  trains you to deploy them (Lesson 6).
- **Every arrest lanyard has a deceleration device** (shock absorber).
- **Double-leg (100% tie-off) lanyards** for moving between anchors.
- **Rope or cable grabs** for fixed ladders that have a vertical lifeline.

## Inspection

- **You visually inspect your harness and lanyard before every use** and report any defect
  to your supervisor immediately. Defective equipment is tagged and removed from service.
- A **competent person inspects harnesses periodically** to confirm they are in safe working
  condition (ANSI Z359 calls for this at least yearly; the manufacturer may require more).
- What you look for, item by item, is in skill **LT1-S21**: webbing, stitching, hardware,
  labels, impact indicators, relief steps, and the lanyard and its hooks.

> **Safety:** Never modify a harness or lanyard to make it more comfortable or "fit the
> job": no knots, no cutting, no tying a lanyard back on itself unless the manufacturer
> allows it. Equipment that is too uncomfortable to wear correctly is a fit problem; tell
> your supervisor so you get the right size.

## Anchorage and Tie-Off Rules

1. **5,000 lb per worker.** Every tie-off point must be capable of withstanding 5,000 lb,
   or be designed by a qualified person with a safety factor of at least two (29 CFR
   1926.502(d)(15); 1910.140(c)(13)).
2. **How do you know?** An engineered anchor is **listed or tagged** on the device. If you
   cannot confirm the rating, it is not an anchor. Ask your supervisor.
3. **Never** tie off to conduit, sprinkler pipe, light fixtures, cable tray, ductwork,
   roof vents, or guardrails not designed as anchors.
4. **High and tight.** Tie-off points should be as high as the work permits, and **at
   shoulder height or above whenever possible.** A low anchor lengthens the free fall and the
   clearance you need.
5. **Adjust the lanyard so a fall is never more than 6 ft.**
6. **100% tie-off at all times** when you are using a harness and lanyard. When moving from
   one tie-off point to another, **use a double-leg lanyard**: clip the second leg to the
   new anchor before unclipping the first, unless a cage or guardrail protects you during the
   transition.
7. **Fixed ladders with a vertical lifeline:** use the cable or rope grab, and **each worker
   is tied off to their own lifeline.** Current OSHA rules require a ladder safety system or
   personal fall arrest system on fixed ladders that extend more than 24 ft
   (1910.28(b)(9)).

## Guardrails, Holes, and Working Surfaces

- **Guardrails:** a complete system has a **top rail 39 to 45 in.** above the walking surface
  and a **midrail** (or screen or mesh) about halfway, and the top rail must hold **200 lb**
  applied in any outward or downward direction (1926.502(b); 1910.29(b)). A railing missing
  its midrail is not complete. Report broken or missing guardrails before you use the area.
- **Stairs:** four or more risers or more than 30 in. high need a handrail or stair rail.
- **Holes and floor openings you create** (a removed ceiling tile over an open atrium, a
  roof hatch left open) must be **covered or barricaded immediately.**
- **Surfaces must be strong enough** to hold you. Do not walk on skylights, ceiling grid,
  or suspect roof decking.
- **Slips and trips** cause more injuries than falls from height. Watch for spills, uneven or
  damaged surfaces, poor housekeeping, improper footwear, unplanned routes (especially on
  team lifts), stairs without handrails, and tight or zigzag paths.

## Scaffolding

Our company policy: **do not use scaffolding unless you have been trained** by a competent
person on that type (29 CFR 1926.454). Our company does not have LT1 technicians erect
scaffolding, and scaffold work is allowed only when the supervisor has determined there is
no other way to do the job. If a customer or another contractor asks you to work from their
scaffold, tell them you have not been trained on it and call your supervisor. Rolling
scaffolds are covered in LT3-C06; nobody rides or moves a rolling scaffold with anyone on it.

## Protecting People Below: Falling Objects

Fall protection includes keeping the people **below** you safe. Our company requirements:

- **Hard hats** whenever objects could fall from above, or you could strike your head while
  being lifted in a bucket or lift.
- **Toe boards** are built into the lifts we use. They mark the edge of your platform and
  stop tools and parts from rolling off.
- **Housekeeping at height:** clean up as you go, **take only the tools and equipment
  needed** for the task, and remove all scrap, old material, and loose objects from any
  elevated platform. Store material so it cannot fall.
- **Control the area below.** Our training calls this a **controlled access zone**: the area
  under and around your work that **you control the whole time you are elevated.** Mark it
  with cones, caution tape, barricades, and signs, and if anyone walks in, **stop work** until
  they are clear. Cones and tape alone do not keep people out; you have to watch the zone.
- **Get the site manager involved.** Discuss the work and agree on a safety plan. Blocking an
  entrance may keep shoppers safe but cause problems for the business that you do not know
  about. The manager can help pick the time and route.
- **Use a spotter** for lift work on most interior jobs, especially during business hours.

> **Safety:** OSHA does not accept a controlled access zone as fall protection for the
> person who is elevated. Our zone protects the **people below** from falling objects; you
> still need a guardrail, restraint, or arrest system for yourself.

## Key Takeaways
- Our company provides harnesses with relief steps, shock-absorbing lanyards, and double-leg lanyards; inspect them before every use.
- Anchors: 5,000 lb or engineered and tagged; tie off high and tight, at shoulder height or above when possible.
- 100% tie-off: use a double-leg lanyard when moving between anchors; own lifeline on fixed-ladder systems.
- Cover or barricade holes you create; report missing guardrails; do not use scaffolding unless trained.
- Control the zone below you, keep the platform clean, and involve the site manager.
