## A Fall Does Not End When the Lanyard Catches You

Many people think that once a harness stops a fall, the job is done. It is not. Our
company's training breaks a fall into **four phases**, and you have to plan for all four
**before** you clip in:

1. **Before the fall**
2. **Fall arrest**
3. **Suspension**
4. **Rescue**

Improving one phase can make another worse. For example, a front attachment makes
self-rescue easier but puts more stress on your spine at arrest. That is why our company
picks the equipment and trains you to use it a specific way.

## Phase 1: Before the Fall

**Compliance is key.** The single most important thing you can do is follow your training:
**always have fall protection** when you are exposed to a fall. A harness that is
uncomfortable or "in the way" is a reason to get the right size, never a reason to leave it
off or modify it.

Before you clip in, run through this checklist in your head:

- **Is my harness and lanyard inspected,** with every part there and working?
- **Are my relief steps installed?**
- **Is my anchor rated** (5,000 lb or engineered and tagged), **high and tight?**
- **How far would I fall?** With a 6-ft lanyard, do I have **18½ ft** below the anchor? If
  not, what else should I use?
- **If I do fall, how will I be rescued?** Is someone else on site who knows I am up here?
  **Do I have my whistle on me? Do I have a phone?** (Even an old phone with no service plan
  will usually still dial 911.)

OSHA requires the employer to provide for **prompt rescue** of workers after a fall
(29 CFR 1910.140(c)(21); 1926.502(d)(20)). On our jobs that means a rescue plan you know
**before** work starts, not just calling 911 afterward.

## Phase 2: Fall Arrest

The longer the lanyard, the longer you accelerate and the harder the stop. The
**deceleration device** on every company lanyard spreads that stop out so the arrest
force on your body stays at a safe level. The **back D-ring**, which our company recommends
in almost all cases, keeps your body upright during arrest so the force is spread from your
shoulders to your thighs and best protects your spine.

## Phase 3: Suspension and Suspension Trauma

After the fall is stopped, you are hanging upright with your legs dangling. That is
dangerous by itself.

**What happens (orthostatic intolerance):** when you stand, your leg muscles squeeze the
veins and push blood back up to your heart. Hanging still in a harness, your legs are
relaxed and the **leg straps press on the veins.** Blood **pools in your legs.** Your heart
speeds up to keep blood going to your brain, but it can only pump the blood that comes back
to it. Eventually you faint.

On the ground, fainting fixes the problem: you collapse flat, and your heart, legs, and
brain are on the same level again. **In a harness, you cannot fall flat.** You stay upright,
unconscious, while your brain loses blood supply. This is what kills workers who survived
the fall itself.

Our company training uses a simple rule: **plan on about five minutes** of hanging still
before you could lose consciousness. Longer suspensions need footholds or another way to
put weight on your legs. That is why every company harness has relief steps.

> **Safety:** If you fall and are hanging in your harness: **deploy your relief steps first,
> then call for help.** Stand in the steps and keep your legs moving: push down, pump your
> legs, and raise your knees if you can. Use your whistle and phone.

The same physics applies to anyone who must stand still for long periods, especially in
heat or when dehydrated. Keep your knees slightly bent and stay hydrated.

## Phase 4: Rescue

Rescue must happen **fast**, because a suspended worker may lose consciousness within
minutes.

- **Self-rescue:** if you are uninjured and can reach the surface, a ladder, or the lift
  platform safely, you may be able to get your weight back on your feet. Do not unclip until
  you are standing on something safe.
- **Assisted rescue:** the crew follows the rescue plan. Often that means a lift brought up
  to the worker or a ladder placed for the worker to stand on. A coworker who is not trained
  and equipped for rescue **does not** climb out to the victim and become a second victim.
- **Call 911** and tell the dispatcher that the person **fell and was suspended in a harness,
  and for how long.**

### After the Worker Is Down

Our older training taught a slow "kneel, then sit, then lie down over 30 to 40 minutes"
procedure. **That advice has been revised.** More recent medical reviews found no benefit to
holding a rescued worker upright. Treat a rescued worker like any other medical emergency:
follow your first aid and CPR training and the 911 dispatcher's instructions, and make sure
the responders know the person was suspended. Never leave a rescued worker alone.

## After Any Fall

- The harness, lanyard, and any other component that arrested the fall are **removed from
  service** and turned in. Do not reuse them.
- Report the incident to your supervisor right away, even if no one is hurt.

## Key Takeaways
- Plan all four phases before you clip in: before the fall, arrest, suspension, and rescue.
- Hanging still in a harness can cause unconsciousness within minutes; relief steps buy time.
- If you fall: deploy your relief steps first, then call for help with your whistle or phone.
- Rescue must be prompt and planned before work starts; untrained coworkers do not climb out to the victim.
- After rescue, treat it as a medical emergency, tell 911 about the suspension, and retire the equipment.
