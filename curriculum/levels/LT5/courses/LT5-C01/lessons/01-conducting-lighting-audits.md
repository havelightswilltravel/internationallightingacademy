## Why the Audit Matters
Every retrofit proposal, estimate and savings calculation is only as good as the audit behind
it. A missed room, a wrong fixture count, or a guess about operating hours can turn a
profitable job into a loss, or promise a customer savings that never show up on the utility
bill. As a CALT you are expected to produce an audit that another estimator could price
without returning to the site.

## Before You Go
1. **Get the basics from the customer:** building drawings or a reflected ceiling plan if
   available, 12 months of utility bills, the hours each area is occupied, and any known
   problems (dark areas, glare, failed fixtures, nuisance tripping).
2. **Ask about access:** escort requirements, secure areas, ceiling heights, lift access, and
   whether occupied spaces can be entered during business hours.
3. **Plan your route:** go floor by floor and room by room in a fixed order so nothing is
   skipped. Number spaces on the plan to match your audit form.
4. **Pack the kit:** audit form or app, illuminance meter (in calibration), laser distance
   meter, camera, flashlight, ladder, clamp meter, and lamp/ballast reference sheets.

> **Safety:** An audit is still field work. Use ladders correctly (three points of contact,
> proper angle), never open panelboards, junction boxes or fixture wiring compartments that may
> be energized unless you are qualified and the equipment is locked out or you are working
> under an approved energized-work procedure. Reading a panel schedule through a closed,
> intact dead-front is fine; removing covers is not part of a walk-through.

## What to Record in Every Space
Use the same fields for every space so the data can be totaled later.

| Field | Example | Why it matters |
|---|---|---|
| Space ID / name / type | 2-114, Open Office | Sets IES target and code control requirements |
| Fixture type | 2x4 recessed troffer, 3-lamp | Drives product selection |
| Lamp / ballast or driver | F32T8, instant-start NEO ballast | Determines input watts and retrofit options |
| Quantity | 18 | Takeoff and savings |
| Mounting height | 9 ft ceiling | Labor, lift needs, optics |
| Existing controls | Single-pole switch, no sensor | Control upgrade scope |
| Operating hours | 12 h/day, 5 days/wk | Savings calculation |
| Measured illuminance | 38 fc avg at desk | Over- or under-lit |
| Condition / notes | 3 fixtures with yellowed lenses | Scope extras |

**Input watts, not lamp watts:** A 3-lamp F32T8 fixture is not 96 W. Ballast losses and the
ballast factor change the real input power. Use manufacturer input-watt tables or a
standard fixture wattage table approved by your company or the utility program. Measuring a
sample circuit with a clamp meter (in a closed panel with an accessible feeder or at a
plug-in fixture) can confirm the table values.

## Identifying Existing Equipment
- **Look at the label first.** Ballast and driver labels give type, input voltage, lamp
  configuration and often input watts. Use a flashlight from the ladder; do not disassemble
  energized fixtures.
- **Lamp markings** (e.g., F32T8/841, MH400/U) tell you technology, wattage and color.
- **HID fixtures** usually have a ballast in the fixture housing or a remote box. Note the
  wattage and whether it is probe-start or pulse-start metal halide, as this affects input
  watts.
- **Older magnetic ballasts** may be marked "No PCBs." If a pre-1979 ballast is not marked
  "No PCBs," treat it as potentially PCB-containing and flag it for proper disposal pricing.
- **Fluorescent and HID lamps contain mercury.** Count them so universal waste recycling can
  be priced.

## Measuring Light Levels
1. Let lamps warm up (HID and some fluorescent need several minutes).
2. Hold the meter sensor level at the task height (about 30 in for desks, floor level for
   corridors and warehouses unless the task is elevated).
3. Keep your body and shadow out of the reading.
4. Take readings at several points: under fixtures, between fixtures, and at task locations.
5. Note daylight conditions. For accurate electric-light readings, take a second reading
   with blinds closed or at night, or record that daylight was present.

Compare the averages to IES recommended illuminance for the space type. Over-lit spaces are
an opportunity to reduce fixture count or wattage; under-lit spaces may need more output, and
the customer should know the retrofit will change the look of the space.

## Operating Hours and Controls
Operating hours are the single biggest swing factor in savings. Ask the facility manager,
then verify: are lights on in empty rooms? Are corridors on 24/7? A simple plug-in or
self-contained light logger left for one to two weeks is the best evidence in uncertain
areas. Record existing controls and note where current energy code would require automatic
shutoff, occupancy sensing or daylight responsive controls in a full retrofit. Requirements
depend on the energy code edition adopted locally (ASHRAE 90.1, IECC or a state code such as
Title 24), so flag these for confirmation rather than assuming.

## Closing Out the Audit
- Walk back through the plan and confirm every space has an entry.
- Photograph representative fixtures, labels and problem areas, and tie photo numbers to
  space IDs.
- Note access issues (high ceilings over equipment, hard-lid ceilings, asbestos warnings,
  occupied hours only) because these drive labor.
- Debrief the customer: thank them, confirm hours, and set expectations for when the
  proposal will arrive.

## Key Takeaways
- A complete, consistent audit is the foundation for accurate scopes, estimates and savings.
- Record input watts, quantity, hours, controls and measured light levels for every space.
- Do not open energized equipment during a walk-through; read labels with a flashlight.
- Flag PCB-suspect ballasts and mercury lamps so disposal is priced.
- Verify operating hours with loggers where uncertain; hours drive savings more than anything else.
- Confirm energy code control requirements against the locally adopted code.
