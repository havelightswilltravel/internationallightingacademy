## What a JHA Is
A **job hazard analysis (JHA)**, also called a job safety analysis (JSA), breaks a job into
steps, identifies the hazards in each step, and specifies how each hazard will be controlled.
OSHA publishes guidance on JHAs (OSHA Publication 3071, *Job Hazard Analysis*). For electrical
work, NFPA 70E also requires a job briefing before starting each job that covers hazards,
procedures, energy source controls, PPE and special precautions.

A JHA is only useful if it is **specific to today's job and site** and the crew actually
discusses it. A generic form signed without reading is paperwork, not safety.

## Building a JHA: Step by Step
1. **List the job steps** in order (usually 5-12 steps). Keep each step short: "Set up lift,"
   "Lock out circuit," "Remove old fixture."
2. **Identify hazards for each step.** Ask: What can hurt someone? What energy is present
   (electrical, gravity, mechanical, stored)? What can go wrong?
3. **Choose controls** using the hierarchy of controls.
4. **Assign responsibility** for each control.
5. **Review with the crew,** adjust based on their input, and have everyone sign.
6. **Revisit when conditions change.**

## The Hierarchy of Controls
Use the most effective control that is feasible. PPE is the last line of defense, not the
first.

| Level | Meaning | Lighting example |
|---|---|---|
| Elimination | Remove the hazard | De-energize and lock out instead of working live |
| Substitution | Replace with something less hazardous | Use a lift instead of a tall extension ladder; LED instead of mercury lamps |
| Engineering controls | Isolate people from the hazard | Guardrails on a scissor lift; barricades around the work zone; insulated tools |
| Administrative controls | Change how people work | LOTO procedure, JHA, scheduling work when the floor is empty, spotters |
| PPE | Protect the worker | Safety glasses, gloves, hard hat, arc-rated clothing where required, fall arrest in boom lifts |

> **Safety:** For electrical hazards, establishing an electrically safe work condition (LOTO
> plus verification of absence of voltage) is the primary control. Energized work is permitted
> only when de-energizing creates a greater hazard or is infeasible, and it requires a
> qualified person, a risk assessment, appropriate PPE and, in most cases, an energized
> electrical work permit under NFPA 70E. "It's faster" or "the customer doesn't want an
> outage" is not a justification.

## Sample JHA (Excerpt): High-Bay Replacement with Scissor Lift

| Step | Hazards | Controls | Who |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1. Set up work zone | Forklift traffic; pedestrians | Coordinate with warehouse manager; barricade; spotter during moves | Lead |
| 2. Inspect and position lift | Tip-over on uneven or damaged floor; overhead obstructions; contact with racking | Pre-use inspection; check floor; workplace inspection; trained operator only | Operator |
| 3. Lock out circuit | Wrong circuit; re-energization | Identify circuit from panel schedule and verify; personal locks and tags on every worker; live-dead-live at fixture | Each worker |
| 4. Remove old fixture | Falling objects; heavy lift; sharp edges; broken lamp (mercury) | Tool lanyards; two-person lift; gloves; lamp breakage kit | Crew |
| 5. Install new fixture | Overloading platform; pinch points | Check platform capacity; stay within guardrails | Operator |
| 6. Restore power and test | Unexpected energization while others still working | Headcount; each worker removes own lock; clear area before energizing | Lead |

## Stop-Work Authority
Every crew member has the right and responsibility to stop work when they believe a situation
is unsafe. As lead, you must make that real.

**When to stop work:**
- A new hazard appears (a circuit tests live that was supposed to be dead; someone enters the
  barricaded area; weather changes for exterior work).
- The plan no longer fits the job (different ceiling, different fixture, scope change).
- Someone is not trained or authorized for the task.
- Equipment fails inspection.
- Anyone feels unsure.

**How to handle a stop:**
1. Stop the task and make the area safe.
2. Thank the person who called the stop.
3. Gather the crew; discuss what changed.
4. Update the JHA and controls; get supervisor input if needed.
5. Resume only when everyone agrees the hazard is controlled.

## Near Misses and Incidents
A near miss is a free lesson. Report every near miss and incident per company policy, even
when no one is hurt. Common lighting near misses: a "dead" circuit that tested live because of
a shared neutral or a second feed, a dropped tool from a lift, a ladder that slipped. Use the
report to update JHAs and toolbox talks. Never discourage reporting; crews that hide near
misses eventually have injuries.

## Key Takeaways
- A JHA breaks the job into steps, identifies hazards and assigns controls, and must be site-specific.
- Use the hierarchy of controls: elimination first, PPE last.
- An electrically safe work condition is the primary electrical control; energized work requires justification under NFPA 70E.
- Everyone has stop-work authority; thank people who use it and update the plan.
- Revisit the JHA whenever conditions change.
- Report near misses and use them to improve.
