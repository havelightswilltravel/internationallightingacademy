## The Lead's Job
As an LT5 technician, you are often the person in charge on site. The crew looks to you for direction,
the customer sees you as the face of the company, and your supervisor relies on you to report
honestly about progress and problems. Leading well is a skill you can learn, just like
installing a driver.

A lead technician is responsible for four things, in this order:
1. **Everyone goes home uninjured.**
2. **The work is done correctly and to code.**
3. **The customer is respected and informed.**
4. **The job finishes on schedule and budget.**

When these conflict, the order above decides. A job that finishes early because someone
skipped LOTO is a failure.

## Before the Job: Plan
- **Review the job packet:** scope, drawings, fixture schedule, sequences, customer contact,
  access rules, hours allowed.
- **Visit or review the site** if you did not audit it. Look for changes since the estimate.
- **Confirm materials:** delivered, correct, counted. Open sample boxes; verify voltage, CCT,
  lumen package and mounting.
- **Confirm equipment:** lifts booked and inspected, ladders, LOTO kits (enough locks and tags
  for every worker), meters with current calibration, PPE.
- **Check authorizations:** who is trained on the MEWP? Who is qualified (per NFPA 70E) for the
  tasks planned? Who needs direct supervision?
- **Know the emergency plan:** nearest hospital or urgent care, site emergency contacts,
  location of the AED and first aid kit, assembly area.

## Assigning Work
Match tasks to each crew member's level, training and authorization, and pair people
deliberately.

| Crew member | Good assignments | Should not be assigned |
|---|---|---|
| LT1 | Material staging, ground support, relamping under supervision, cleanup, documentation photos | Unsupervised electrical work; operating a MEWP before authorized training |
| LT2 | TLED conversions, fixture whips, de-energized retrofits after LOTO verification | Controls programming without guidance |
| LT3/LT4 | Driver replacements, sensor install and adjustment, emergency drivers, troubleshooting | Tasks outside their documented qualifications |

Tell each person **what** to do, **why** it matters, **how** you want it done, and **when** you
will check back. Then check back.

## Running the Day
- **Morning huddle (10-15 minutes):** goals for the day, JHA review, hazards specific to today's
  areas, assignments, questions. Everyone signs the JHA.
- **Walk the work:** circulate. Spot problems early. Look at LOTO points: is every worker's
  lock on?
- **Midday check:** progress vs plan; adjust assignments; check on breaks and hydration in hot
  conditions.
- **Customer touchpoint:** a short update builds trust and catches issues ("we need access to
  the server room tomorrow").
- **End of day:** clean the work area, secure materials and lifts, remove locks only after
  each person verifies their own work is clear, update the schedule, log hours, note problems.

## Communication Basics
- **Be specific.** "Finish rooms 110-118 by lunch, kits only, I'll check at 11:30" beats "keep
  going."
- **Close the loop.** Ask people to repeat back critical instructions, especially about which
  circuits are locked out.
- **Report problems early.** Your supervisor would rather hear "we're two hours behind because
  the ceiling grid is damaged" at 10 a.m. than at 5 p.m.
- **Praise in public, correct in private,** except for an immediate safety correction, which
  happens on the spot.

> **Safety:** You have the authority and the duty to stop work when conditions are unsafe.
> Make sure every crew member knows they have stop-work authority too, and that they will
> never be punished for using it in good faith.

## Handling Scope Changes
Customers will ask for extras: "While you're here, can you move that light?" Do not agree on
the spot to work that is not in the scope. Write it down, explain that you will get it priced
or approved, and contact your supervisor. Unapproved extras cause unpaid labor, schedule
slips, and sometimes code problems (for example, relocating a fixture may require a new box or
circuit work that needs a licensed electrician or a permit).

## Leading by Example
Your crew will copy what you do, not what you say. If you wear safety glasses only when the
customer is looking, so will they. If you verify absence of voltage every time, they will too.
The fastest way to build a safe crew is to be the most consistent person on it.

## Key Takeaways
- Priorities: safety, quality and code, customer, then schedule and budget.
- Plan materials, equipment, authorizations and the emergency plan before mobilizing.
- Assign work based on level, training and authorizations, and check back.
- Run a daily huddle with JHA review; walk the work; update the customer and supervisor.
- Everyone has stop-work authority; make sure the crew knows it.
- Route scope changes through your supervisor; never agree to extras on the spot.
