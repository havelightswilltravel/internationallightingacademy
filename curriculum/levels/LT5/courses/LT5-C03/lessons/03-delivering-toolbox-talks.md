## What a Toolbox Talk Is For
A **toolbox talk** is a short (typically 10-15 minute) safety discussion held at the job site,
usually at the start of a shift or week. Its purpose is to focus the crew's attention on a
specific hazard they will face, refresh safe practices, and invite questions. It is not a
lecture, a formality, or a substitute for formal training.

## Choosing the Topic
The best topics are tied to **today's work** or a **recent event**.
- Upcoming tasks: lift work, working in occupied space, emergency lighting tests, exterior
  pole work.
- Recent near misses or incidents (in your company or the industry).
- Seasonal hazards: heat stress in summer, slips and cold in winter, early darkness for
  exterior work.
- Observed problems: crew members not wearing glasses, ladders set up wrong, locks missing.

**Lighting-specific topic ideas:**
- Live-dead-live: why we test the tester
- Shared neutrals and multiwire branch circuits: why "breaker off" may not mean "dead"
- Ladder setup and three points of contact
- MEWP pre-use and workplace inspection
- Lamp breakage and mercury handling
- PCB ballast identification and handling
- Working above others: dropped-object prevention
- Emergency lighting: never leave egress paths dark during testing
- Heat stress in attics and high bays
- Arc flash: what it is and why we de-energize

## Structure of a Good Talk (the 5-part outline)
1. **Hook (1 minute):** A short real story, a question, or a prop. "Who's seen a ladder like
   this?"
2. **The hazard (2-3 minutes):** What can happen and how. Keep it concrete.
3. **The controls (3-4 minutes):** What we do to prevent it, demonstrated where possible.
4. **Connect to today (2 minutes):** Where on this job does this apply? What specifically will
   we do?
5. **Check and record (2-3 minutes):** Ask open questions, take questions, record attendance.

## Delivery Tips
- **Talk, don't read.** Use a one-page outline, but speak in your own words.
- **Use props.** A damaged ladder, a meter with a cracked lead, a LOTO kit, a broken lamp kit.
  People remember what they see and handle.
- **Ask open questions.** "What would you check before getting on this lift?" works better
  than "Everyone understand?" (which always gets nods).
- **Let the crew talk.** Experienced techs have stories; invite them. New techs often have
  questions they are afraid to ask; make it safe.
- **Keep it short and focused.** One topic per talk. If it runs over 15 minutes, you are
  probably covering too much.
- **Be honest.** If you do not know an answer, say so and follow up.
- **Respect language needs.** If crew members are more comfortable in another language, use
  bilingual materials or a translator.

> **Safety:** A toolbox talk does not qualify anyone to do a task. If a talk reveals that a
> crew member lacks required training (for example, MEWP operator training or qualified-person
> training for electrical work), do not assign them the task until the training is completed
> and documented.

## Sample Outline: Shared Neutrals (MWBCs)
- **Hook:** "Last year a tech on a retrofit turned off the breaker, opened a fixture, and got
  shocked on the white wire. How?"
- **Hazard:** On a multiwire branch circuit, two or three ungrounded conductors share one
  neutral. If only one breaker is off, the shared neutral can still carry current from the
  other circuits. Opening the neutral can expose you to voltage.
- **Controls:** Identify MWBCs (handle-tied or common-trip breakers, shared neutrals in the
  box). Lock out all ungrounded conductors of the MWBC. Test every conductor, including the
  neutral to ground, live-dead-live. Never open a shared neutral under load.
- **Today:** "Rooms 200-210 are on a 3-phase MWBC. All three breakers get locked out together."
- **Check:** "What breakers do we lock for room 205? How do we verify?"

## Recording and Follow-Up
Record the date, topic, presenter and attendees on a sign-in sheet, and file it according to
company policy. Records show the crew was informed and help track which topics have been
covered. If a question comes up that you could not answer, follow up the next day. If the talk
uncovered a hazard or equipment problem, fix it and tell the crew it was fixed.

## Key Takeaways
- Toolbox talks are short, focused discussions tied to today's work or a recent event.
- Use the 5-part outline: hook, hazard, controls, connect to today, check and record.
- Talk instead of reading, use props, and ask open questions.
- One topic per talk; keep it to 10-15 minutes.
- A toolbox talk is not a substitute for required training.
- Record attendance and follow up on open questions or hazards.
