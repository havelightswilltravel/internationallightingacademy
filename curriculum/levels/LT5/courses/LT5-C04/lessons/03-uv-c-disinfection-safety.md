> **Note:** UV-C technology, standards and regulations are evolving. This lesson covers
> well-established safety facts and must be reviewed at least annually by an SME.

## What UV-C Is
Ultraviolet light is divided into bands. **UV-C** (roughly 200-280 nm) can inactivate
microorganisms by damaging their DNA and RNA. This is called **ultraviolet germicidal
irradiation (UVGI)**.

| Source | Typical wavelength | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Low-pressure mercury lamp | 254 nm | Most established germicidal source; contains mercury |
| UV-C LED | Roughly 260-280 nm | Compact; output and efficiency improving |
| Krypton-chloride (KrCl) excimer ("far-UVC") | 222 nm | Filtered versions marketed for occupied spaces; research and exposure guidance evolving |

## Applications
- **Upper-room UVGI:** Fixtures mounted high on walls irradiate air above people's heads with
  louvers to limit downward exposure. Air movement carries room air through the UV zone.
- **In-duct / HVAC coil systems:** Lamps inside air handlers disinfect air or keep coils clean.
- **Unoccupied-room disinfection:** Portable or fixed systems irradiate surfaces when the space
  is empty, with interlocks and occupancy sensing.
- **Filtered far-UVC in occupied spaces:** Marketed for continuous use within exposure limits.

Effectiveness depends on dose (intensity x time), distance, shadowing, air mixing and lamp
condition. A lighting tech installs and services these systems; effectiveness claims belong to
the engineer and manufacturer.

## The Hazards
**UV-C is invisible and does its damage without immediate pain.** Symptoms often appear hours
later.
- **Eyes:** Photokeratitis (like "welder's flash" or snow blindness): severe pain, tearing and
  a gritty feeling, typically appearing hours after exposure.
- **Skin:** Erythema (sunburn-like redness).
- **Ozone:** Some lamps, particularly those emitting below about 240 nm, can generate ozone,
  a respiratory irritant. Ozone-free lamps are available; ventilation matters.
- **Mercury:** Low-pressure lamps contain mercury. Broken lamps require cleanup procedures and
  disposal as universal waste or per local rules.
- **Material degradation:** UV-C degrades plastics, fabrics and some finishes over time.

Occupational exposure limits for UV are published by ACGIH (threshold limit values), and
photobiological safety of lamps is evaluated under standards such as IEC 62471. Systems for
occupied spaces must be designed and installed so exposure stays within limits.

> **Safety:** Never look at an operating UV-C source, and never rely on "it looks dim" to
> judge safety; UV-C is invisible, and the visible glow of a mercury lamp says nothing about its
> UV output. Before servicing, de-energize the fixture with LOTO, verify absence of voltage at
> the fixture, and confirm the UV source is off (indicator, interlock, or manufacturer-specified
> check). If any exposure is possible, wear UV-rated face protection and cover all skin.

## Working Safely Around UV-C Systems
### Before work
1. Read the manufacturer's installation and service manual. Note interlocks, warning labels,
   and required PPE.
2. Identify every UV-C device in the work area, including in-duct units in air handlers.
3. Coordinate with the facility; post signs during service.

### Servicing upper-room fixtures
1. Turn off the fixture and apply LOTO at the circuit disconnect; do not rely only on a
   local switch or the fixture's own switch.
2. Verify absence of voltage (live-dead-live) before touching wiring.
3. Wear UV-rated eye/face protection and cover skin if the lamp could be energized during any
   step (for example, testing).
4. Allow lamps to cool before handling.
5. Handle lamps with clean gloves; skin oils on the quartz glass can reduce output and cause
   hot spots.
6. After relamping, verify louvers are positioned correctly; misaligned louvers can direct UV
   into the occupied zone.

### In-duct and air-handler systems
- Many air handlers have door interlock switches that turn off the UV lamps when the access
  door opens. **Do not defeat interlocks.** Lock out both the UV system and the air handler
  per the facility's procedure before entry.
- Fan, belt and electrical hazards also apply; follow the mechanical contractor's or
  facility's LOTO procedures.

### Verifying operation
Use a UV-C meter or indicator cards designed for the wavelength, held so you are not exposed.
Never "check" with your eyes or hands.

## Customer Conversations
- UV-C is a supplement to ventilation, filtration and cleaning, not a replacement.
- Products should be backed by third-party test data and listed for safety; be cautious of
  consumer devices with unverified claims.
- Lamps lose UV output over time while still glowing; follow the manufacturer's replacement
  interval.

## Key Takeaways
- UV-C (about 200-280 nm) inactivates microorganisms; common sources are 254 nm mercury lamps, UV-C LEDs and 222 nm excimer lamps.
- UV-C is invisible and causes delayed eye (photokeratitis) and skin injury.
- Always LOTO, verify absence of voltage, and confirm UV is off before service; never defeat interlocks.
- Wear UV-rated face protection and cover skin whenever exposure is possible.
- Mercury lamps require breakage procedures and universal waste disposal.
- This topic changes quickly and needs periodic SME review.
