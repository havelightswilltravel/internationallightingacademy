## Two Ways to Take Care of Lighting
Every customer's lighting gets maintained one way or another. The question is whether it is
maintained **reactively** (someone calls when something is dark) or **by plan** (the work is
scheduled, batched and tracked against an agreement).

| | Reactive ("break-fix") | Planned service |
|---|---|---|
| Trigger | A complaint or a dark fixture | A calendar, an inventory and agreed service levels |
| Cost per repair | High: a trip, setup and often a lift for one fixture | Lower: one trip and one lift day cover many repairs |
| Budget for the owner | Unpredictable | Known monthly or annual figure |
| Records | Scattered tickets, if any | Inventory, failure log, reports |
| Light levels | Drift down unnoticed | Measured and reported |
| Retrofit/replace timing | Decided in a crisis | Planned over budget years |

Reactive work never goes away completely, because some failures cannot wait. A planned program
decides **in advance** which failures get an immediate response and which are batched.

## Why the Model Changed
Older facilities often relamped whole areas on a schedule because fluorescent and HID lamps
burned out at predictable ages; that practice has been replaced by LED retrofitting or luminaire
replacement, so we do not sell scheduled relamping. LED systems still need care, but the work is
different. Light fades slowly instead of failing all at once, drivers and controls fail
individually, dirt still steals light, and warranties have value only if someone tracks them. A
modern program manages those things.

## What a Modern Planned Program Includes
A planned service agreement usually bundles some or all of the following. Each one is covered
in a later lesson or in the course referenced.

1. **Asset inventory:** every luminaire, driver, control device and emergency unit, by location
   (Lesson 03).
2. **Scheduled inspection:** a walk-through for dark, dim, flickering or damaged fixtures,
   loose or missing lenses, and damaged supports.
3. **Failure response:** repairs under agreed response tiers (below).
4. **Controls health check:** sensors responding, time delays and schedules correct, daylight
   zones not stuck dim, photocells not causing day-burning (see LT5-C02 for test methods).
5. **Emergency lighting testing and records** on the schedule the code requires (see LT3-C04).
6. **Cleaning** at an interval set by the environment and measured light loss (Lesson 05).
7. **Warranty administration:** claims, RMAs and credit tracking (Lesson 03).
8. **Matched attic stock** so replacements match in color and output.
9. **A retrofit/replace/maintain roadmap** for the areas still on legacy or failing equipment
   (Lesson 04).
10. **Reporting:** what was found, fixed, measured and recommended (Lesson 06).

## Response Tiers
A service agreement should state, in writing, how fast each kind of failure gets a response.
These are example tiers; the actual times are negotiated and priced in each agreement.

| Tier | Examples | Example response |
|---|---|---|
| 1 – Life safety / security | Exit signs or emergency lights dark; egress path dark; parking lot or entrance dark at night; exposed wiring or a hanging fixture | Same day (for example, within 4 hours) |
| 2 – Operational | A work area, aisle or classroom noticeably dark; a control fault leaving a zone on 24/7 or off | Next business day |
| 3 – Routine | Single fixture out in a well-lit area; cosmetic damage; slightly mismatched color | Batched to the next scheduled visit |

> **Safety:** A fixture hanging by its wiring, a broken lens over a work area or exposed
> conductors are Tier 1 regardless of how small the area is. Make the area safe (barricade, de-
> energize under LOTO with live-dead-live verification) before anything else, and write it up for
> an electrician if branch-circuit repair is needed.

## The Owner's Cost Picture
Customers often compare only the price of a part. Show them the full cost of a repair, which is
mostly labor and access.

> **Example figures only:** Every rate, price and cost in the examples below is made up for
> teaching. None of them are any company's actual labor rates, prices, costs or customer pricing.
> For real estimates and proposals, always use your company's current figures.

Example for one building with high ceilings, using made-up example rates (not real company
rates):

| | Reactive | Planned (quarterly) |
|---|---|---|
| Repairs per year | 12 | 12 |
| Trips | 12 separate calls | 4 visits |
| Labor and access per trip | 2 h × $95 + $350 lift = $540 | 6 h × $95 + $350 lift = $920 |
| Labor and access per year | 12 × $540 = **$6,480** | 4 × $920 = **$3,680** |

Parts cost the same either way. The planned approach saves money mainly because trips and lift
days are shared, and it also produces the records needed for warranty claims and replacement
planning. When you present this, use the customer's real call history and your current rates,
not these example numbers.

## What Planned Service Is Not
- It is **not** a promise that nothing will fail between visits. Tier 1 and Tier 2 calls still
  happen.
- It is **not** a way to keep failing equipment alive forever. When the failure log shows rising
  repair costs, the program should produce a retrofit or replacement recommendation.
- It is **not** permission to work outside your qualifications. Panel interiors, breaker or
  contactor replacement and branch-circuit work are still written up for an electrician unless
  the company has qualified you.

## Where Planned Service Pays Best
Screen each customer site and area. Planned service pays off most where:

- **Access is expensive:** high bays, atriums, stairwells, parking structures, poles. Batching lift
  days matters most here.
- **The environment is dirty:** manufacturing, kitchens, warehouses with forklift traffic,
  parking garages. Light loss from dirt is large and recoverable.
- **Light is indirect or uplit:** dirt on top of an indirect fixture or a coved ceiling costs much
  more light than dirt on a downlight.
- **Legacy equipment remains:** fluorescent or HID areas need a retrofit or replace plan, and
  until then they fail often.
- **Failure has consequences:** retail, healthcare, schools, 24-hour operations and security
  lighting.

Small, clean, low-ceiling offices with new LED fixtures may need little more than an annual
walk-through and emergency testing.

## Key Takeaways
- Reactive service costs the owner more per repair and leaves no records; planned service batches
  work and tracks it against an agreement.
- Scheduled relamping is history. Today's program covers inventory, inspection, response tiers,
  controls checks, emergency testing, cleaning, warranty work, attic stock, a retrofit/replace
  roadmap and reporting.
- Put response tiers in writing; life-safety and security failures always get the fastest response.
- Labor and access, not parts, drive the owner's cost; show it with real numbers.
- Planned service pays best where access is costly, the environment is dirty, light is indirect,
  legacy equipment remains or failure has consequences.
