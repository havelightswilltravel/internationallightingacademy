## The Proposal
A service proposal must make clear what the customer gets, what it costs and how they will know it
worked. Build it from the inventory, failure log and condition data (Lessons 03–04).

| Section | Content |
|---|---|
| Site summary | Areas covered, fixture counts by type, current condition, open problems |
| Scope of service | Inspections and frequency, cleaning areas and interval, controls checks, emergency testing, warranty administration, attic stock management |
| Response tiers | Tier definitions and response times (Lesson 01) |
| Exclusions | Branch-circuit repairs, panel work and anything requiring an electrician or other trade, damage by others, items needing AHJ approval |
| Pricing | Fixed periodic fee, what is included (labor, access, parts?) and rates for extra work |
| Verification | How light levels will be measured and reported before and after service |
| Reporting | What the customer receives and how often |
| Roadmap | Areas recommended for retrofit or replacement, with timing and budget ranges |

Speak the customer's language: fewer complaints, predictable cost, safer spaces, documented
compliance and a plan for aging equipment. Use their own call history and our cost model, not
generic claims.

## Scheduling Around Occupants
- **Plan with the customer:** get occupancy hours, shift changes, deliveries, events, exam weeks
  or seasonal peaks.
- **Work unoccupied hours** where possible, especially for lift work in aisles, cleaning and
  measurements (night readings avoid daylight).
- **Coordinate site logistics:** badging, escorts, keys, freight elevator times and loads, lift
  delivery, staging and secure storage, charging for lifts and tools.
- **Phase by area** so each area is returned to the occupants clean and fully working at the end of
  each shift.
- **Batch Tier 3 work** into the scheduled visit; plan the lift days around the highest work.
- **Confirm the day before:** scope, areas, access and contacts.

## Verifying Results: Light Levels
Verification turns a service visit into evidence. Use the same method before and after (see
LT4-C03/02 for meter use and survey basics).

1. **Plan the grid.** Mark measurement points on the floor plan for each sample area (for example,
   a regular grid at the work surface height, plus key task points). Record exact locations so the
   after readings use the same points.
2. **Use a calibrated meter** with a current calibration sticker; record its model and serial.
3. **Control daylight.** Measure at night or with shades closed. If neither is possible, take a
   reading with the electric lights off at each point and subtract it from the lights-on reading.
4. **Stabilize.** Override any dimming so the fixtures run at full output, let them warm up long
   enough to stabilize (time per the company procedure) and record the control state.
5. **Take readings** at each point, holding the meter level at work height, keeping your body and
   shadow out of the way. Take vertical readings where vertical light matters (shelving, white
   boards).
6. **Record** fixture outages and anything unusual at the time of measurement.
7. **Service** the area (clean, repair, re-set controls).
8. **Re-measure** at the same points, under the same conditions.
9. **Calculate** the average and the maximum-to-minimum ratio before and after, and the percentage
   change.

> **Safety:** Measurement often happens in aisles and around equipment after hours. Wear high-
> visibility clothing where vehicles operate, keep clear of moving equipment, and do not open any
> fixture or panel during the survey without LOTO and live-dead-live verification.

## Verifying Results: Reflectance Check
Room surfaces return light to the workplane. A quick field check shows whether walls and ceilings
are part of the problem.

**Meter method (matte surfaces):**
1. Hold the meter sensor against the surface, facing out into the room, and record the reading
   (light arriving at the surface).
2. Turn the sensor to face the surface, about 2–4 inches away, without shadowing the spot, and
   record the reading (light leaving the surface).
3. Reflectance ≈ reflected reading ÷ incident reading.

Example: 40 fc arriving, 28 fc reflected → about 70% reflectance.

**Reference card method:** hold a card of known reflectance (such as a white or gray card from a
photography kit) flat against the surface. Measure the light reflected from the card, then from the
surface, from the same position. Surface reflectance ≈ card reflectance × (surface reading ÷ card
reading).

These are field estimates, good enough to show change. Repeat at the same spots on later visits. A
falling reflectance means the customer's walls or ceiling need cleaning or painting, which you can
recommend but which is usually outside our scope.

## Reporting
Send a short report after every visit and a fuller one each year.

**Every visit:**
- Work completed, by asset ID.
- Failures found, causes and parts used, with warranty claims filed.
- Open items and Tier 1 or 2 issues.
- Before/after light levels for sampled areas.

**Annually:**
- Failure trends and what they mean.
- Warranty credits recovered.
- Light-level and reflectance trends.
- Updated retrofit/replace/maintain roadmap with next year's budget recommendation.

Write plainly. One table and three sentences often do more than ten pages.

## Key Takeaways
- A proposal states scope, response tiers, exclusions, pricing, verification, reporting and a
  roadmap.
- Schedule around occupants and plan site logistics before mobilizing.
- Verify with a repeatable grid: calibrated meter, daylight controlled, fixtures stabilized at
  full output, same points before and after.
- A meter or reference-card reflectance check shows whether room surfaces are stealing light.
- Regular, plain reports with trends and recommendations are how the customer sees the value.
