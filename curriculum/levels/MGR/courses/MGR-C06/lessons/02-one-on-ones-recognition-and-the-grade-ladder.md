## Why People Stay

Technicians rarely leave for pay alone. Common reasons people stay — or leave — include:

- Their relationship with their direct supervisor.
- Whether they see a future: skills, grade, and pay growth.
- Whether they feel respected and recognized.
- Whether the schedule and on-call load are fair and predictable.
- Whether they feel safe and supported on the job.

Most of those are in the manager's hands.

## Regular One-on-Ones

A short, regular one-on-one is the simplest retention tool there is. Aim for **15–30 minutes every
two weeks** (weekly for new hires during their first 90 days). It belongs to the technician, not to
dispatch problems.

A simple agenda:

1. **How are things going?** Let them lead.
2. **What's getting in the way?** Tools, parts, scheduling, training, a difficult customer.
3. **Progress on the ladder.** Open the app together: courses passed, OJT hours logged, skill
   sign-offs remaining for the next grade.
4. **One piece of feedback** each way: something they did well, something to work on, and "What
   could I do better to support you?"
5. **Commitments.** What each of you will do before the next one.

> **Tip:** Never cancel one-on-ones as the first thing to go when it gets busy. Rescheduling is
> fine; repeated cancelling tells the technician they don't matter.

## Recognition That Means Something

Recognition is cheap and powerful when it is **specific, timely, and sincere**.

| Generic | Specific |
|---|---|
| "Good job this week." | "You noticed the damaged cord on that lift and tagged it out before anyone used it. That's exactly the habit we want." |
| "Thanks for helping out." | "You stayed to finish the parking lot retrofit so the store opened on time. The manager called to thank us." |

Ways to recognize:
- In the moment, privately or in front of the crew (ask what the person prefers).
- At crew meetings — especially for safety catches and customer compliments.
- In the app: celebrate grade promotions and certificates publicly.
- Pass along customer compliments word for word.
- Small, policy-approved rewards: lunch, gear, a preferred assignment.

Recognize **safe behavior and good process**, not only speed or volume. Rewarding only speed
teaches people to cut corners.

## The Grade Ladder and Pay by Grade

Our program gives managers a built-in retention tool: a visible path with clear requirements.

- **Technicians can see where they stand.** Their current grade, what's left for the next one,
  and (where the company uses the pay scale) their current rate and the rate at the next grade.
- **Advancement is in their control.** It depends on demonstrated competence — courses, final
  exam, verified OJT hours, observed skills — not on waiting for seniority.
- **Long-term path.** From LT1 through the Certified Advanced Lighting Technician level, and for
  those who want it, into the electrical track toward journeyman exam preparation.

How managers make the ladder work:

1. **Talk about it in every one-on-one.** "You have two skills left for LT3. Let's schedule your
   evaluator."
2. **Remove roadblocks.** Make sure techs get the job assignments they need to practice and log
   skills; approve OJT hours promptly.
3. **Schedule evaluations quickly.** A technician who's ready and waits months for a sign-off feels
   cheated.
4. **Celebrate promotions** and make pay changes effective on time.
5. **Be honest** when someone isn't ready, with specific steps to get there.

> **Legal:** Apply promotion and pay rules consistently. Make sure every technician has fair access
> to the assignments, training time, and evaluations needed to advance. Unequal access can become
> a discrimination issue as well as a retention problem.

## Fair Schedules and On Call

Few things drive technicians out faster than an unfair on-call rotation or constant last-minute
overtime. Publish the rotation in advance, spread overtime fairly, honor swaps within policy, and
give recovery time after brutal callouts. Revisit the realistic job preview: if the job has
changed, say so.

## Key Takeaways
- The direct supervisor is the biggest factor in whether technicians stay.
- Hold short, regular one-on-ones and protect them from cancellation.
- Make recognition specific, timely, sincere, and focused on safe behavior and good process.
- Use the grade ladder and pay by grade in every conversation; remove roadblocks and schedule evaluations promptly.
- Keep schedules and on call fair and predictable, and give everyone fair access to advancement.
