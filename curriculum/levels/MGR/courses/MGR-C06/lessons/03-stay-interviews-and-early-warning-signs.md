## Ask Before They Leave

Most managers learn why people leave in the exit interview — too late. A **stay interview** asks
current employees what keeps them and what might make them leave, while you can still do something
about it.

## When and With Whom

- With every technician at least **once a year**, separate from performance reviews.
- Sooner for high performers, people approaching a big milestone (such as finishing LT5), people
  who just had a hard stretch (heavy on call, a rough project), and new hires at about 6 months.
- One-on-one, private, unhurried (20–30 minutes), somewhere comfortable — a coffee shop or the
  truck works fine.

## Stay Interview Questions

Pick five or six and ask them open-ended:

1. "What do you look forward to most when you come to work?"
2. "What are you learning here? What would you like to learn?"
3. "Where do you want to be in a year? In five years?" (Then connect to the grade ladder.)
4. "What frustrates you about the job? What gets in your way?"
5. "If you could change one thing about your job or our team, what would it be?"
6. "What would make you think about leaving?"
7. "Have you ever thought about leaving? What prompted it?"
8. "How do you like to be recognized?"
9. "What can I do more of, or less of, to support you?"

## How to Run It

1. **Set it up honestly.** "This isn't a review. I want to understand what keeps you here and what
   I can do better."
2. **Listen more than you talk.** Aim for the technician talking 80% of the time.
3. **Don't defend or explain.** If they say the on-call rotation is unfair, ask "What would make it
   fair?" — not "Well, everyone has to do it."
4. **Summarize** what you heard: "So the big things are getting your LT4 evaluation scheduled and
   more notice on overtime. Did I get that right?"
5. **Commit only to what you can deliver.** "I'll get your evaluation scheduled within two weeks"
   beats "I'll get you a raise" that you can't approve.
6. **Follow up** by the date you promised, even if the answer is "not yet, here's why."
7. **Document themes** and actions. Look for patterns across the team.

> **Legal:** Keep the conversation job-focused. If a technician shares health, family, or other
> personal information, listen respectfully, don't probe, and don't record protected details. If
> they raise a possible accommodation need, harassment, discrimination, or a safety concern, tell
> them you'll involve HR (or safety) and do so promptly.

## Early Warning Signs of Turnover

Watch for changes in behavior compared with that person's normal pattern:

| Sign | What it might mean | What to do |
|---|---|---|
| Stops talking about the future or the next grade | Mentally checked out | Have a direct, caring conversation |
| Progress in the app stalls | Lost motivation or blocked by roadblocks | Ask what's in the way; remove it |
| More absences, late arrivals, or schedule requests | Interviewing, burnout, or personal issues | Check in privately without assuming; refer to HR for leave questions |
| Less effort or volunteering; minimum work | Disengagement | Stay interview; recognition; reassess workload |
| Complaints about pay compared with others | Feels underpaid or unfairly treated | Review grade, pay, and path; explain honestly |
| A close coworker or mentor just left | Loss of connection | Check in; assign a new mentor or peer |
| Conflict with a supervisor or crew member | Relationship breakdown | Address it early; mediate |
| Grade ceiling reached with no next step | No future here | Discuss the electrical track, lead roles, mentoring |
| Updating certifications or asking for copies of records | May be preparing to leave | Don't punish — ask what they want next |

> **Tip:** Never retaliate or treat someone worse because you suspect they are looking. It
> accelerates the departure and creates legal risk. Ask, listen, and try to fix what you can.

## Respond Early

When you see warning signs:

1. Have a private conversation soon: "I've noticed you seem less into things lately. How are you
   doing? Anything going on at work I can help with?"
2. Listen for the real issue — usually the supervisor relationship, growth, schedule, pay
   fairness, or a specific frustration.
3. Act on what you control; escalate what you don't.
4. Follow up within two weeks.

## Key Takeaways
- Stay interviews ask what keeps people and what might make them leave, while you can still act.
- Hold them at least yearly and sooner for high performers and people at milestones.
- Listen, summarize, commit only to what you can deliver, and follow up on time.
- Watch for changes from a person's normal pattern: stalled progress, disengagement, more absences, talk about pay.
- Respond early with a caring, private conversation; never retaliate.
