> **Legal:** General training, not legal advice. Pay-history, pay-transparency, immigration, and
> record-keeping rules vary and change. HR and counsel set the final policy.

## Pay History vs. Desired Pay

A growing number of states and cities **ban asking about a candidate's pay history** (current or
past salary or wages), and some bar using it to set pay even if the candidate volunteers it. The
goal of these laws is to stop past pay gaps from following people from job to job.

| Don't ask | Ask instead |
|---|---|
| "What do you make now?" | "What pay range are you looking for?" |
| "What were you paid at your last job?" | "Our range for this role is $__ to $__ depending on placement grade. Does that work for you?" |
| "Can I see a pay stub?" | (Don't request it) |

The kit's question "How much do you want to make?" is the right approach. Base the actual starting
pay on our **pay-by-grade scale** and the candidate's **placement grade** (MGR-C05), not on what they
earned before. Many jurisdictions also now require employers to **post a pay range** in job ads or
provide it on request. Follow HR's guidance.

## Form I-9 and Work Authorization

Every new employee in the U.S. must complete Form I-9 to verify identity and work authorization.

1. **Before the offer:** ask every candidate only, "Are you legally authorized to work in the United
   States?" and, if relevant, "Will you now or in the future require sponsorship?" Don't ask about
   citizenship, birthplace, or immigration status beyond that.
2. **Section 1:** completed by the employee **no later than the first day of work**.
3. **Section 2:** completed by the employer **within three business days** of the first day,
   examining original documents the employee chooses from the official Lists of Acceptable Documents.
4. **Don't specify which documents** the employee must show, and don't ask for more than the form
   requires. Doing so can be an unfair documentary practice.
5. **E-Verify:** used where required by law or contract, or where our company has enrolled; apply
   it to all new hires at the site, never selectively.
6. **Retention:** keep each I-9 for three years after the date of hire or one year after employment
   ends, whichever is later, stored separately from personnel files.

## Consistency and Documentation

Your hiring file should show a consistent, job-related process from start to finish:

- Job profile and must-haves (dated, before posting).
- Job ad and where it was posted.
- Screening grid results for every applicant.
- Phone-screen forms.
- Interview scorecards and notes for every interviewer.
- Reference check forms and license verification.
- Calibration notes and the documented reason for the decision.
- Offer letter, and FCRA documents (kept by HR).

### Record Retention

Federal rules generally require employers to keep applications and other hiring records for **at
least one year** from the date the record was made or the hiring decision, whichever is later.
Federal contractors and some states require longer, and records must be kept until any charge or
lawsuit is resolved. Our company's retention schedule governs. Keep medical information and I-9s
separate from the general file. Using the app's Hiring records keeps everything in one place.

## Reviewing Our Interview Kits

Our company's kits are a strong foundation, but several items were written before current
practice and should be used in the reworded form below. HR and counsel should approve final
wording.

| Kit item | Risk | Compliant version |
|---|---|---|
| Candidate quality: "Healthy" | Disability/health judgment; not an essential function | Remove; list specific functions (lifting, climbing, working from lifts, outdoor work up to 8 hrs) with "with or without reasonable accommodation" |
| "How much weight can you lift on a consistent basis?" and "Can lift a steady 50 lbs or more" | Invites disability disclosure | "This job requires frequently lifting and carrying materials up to 50 lbs. Can you perform this function, with or without reasonable accommodation?" (Confirm 50 lbs is supported by the job analysis) |
| "Do you have any issues with heights?" (electrician kit) | Invites disclosure of medical/psychological conditions | "This job requires regular work from ladders and lifts. Can you perform that, with or without reasonable accommodation?" |
| "What will your supervisor say about the number of times you were... Absent?" | Absences may include protected leave (FMLA, disability, military, workers' comp, state sick leave) | "Will your former supervisor say you met attendance and punctuality expectations?" Ask references about meeting expectations, excluding protected leave |
| "Can you work around live electricity?" | Safety framing: technicians work de-energized by default; also a possible medical-condition prompt | Describe work near electrical equipment, our LOTO and verification procedures, and ask whether they can follow them with or without accommodation |
| "Can you work on call? Odd hours? Odd shifts? Overtime?" / "Can you travel?" | Generally fine if asked of all; risk if followed by family or religion questions | Keep as written; ask everyone; route religious or disability scheduling needs to HR |
| "What are your personal goals?" / "What matters to you?" / "Describe your likes and dislikes" | Can invite family, religion, or health information | Frame as "professional goals" or "what matters to you in a job"; don't follow up on personal disclosures |
| "When can you purchase tools?" / "Capable of purchasing electrical tools" / "What electrical tools do you have?" | Wage-and-hour rules: required tool costs cannot reduce pay below minimum wage or overtime due, and some states require employers to provide or reimburse tools; can screen out lower-income candidates | State the company's tool policy (what we provide, what techs acquire, any allowance or payroll plan) and ask "Can you meet this tool policy?" |
| "Do you own a smartphone... with data?" / "Do you own a computer?" | Some states require reimbursement of necessary business expenses; possible disparate impact | "This job requires using a smartphone for work orders and dispatch; here is our device or reimbursement policy. Can you meet this requirement?" Only ask about a computer if the job needs one |
| "Is your driving record clean?" / "Valid driver's license with only a few minor tickets" | Vague standard; must be applied only to driving roles | "This role drives a company vehicle. Can you meet our written driving-record standard of ___?" Check the record through the FCRA process after offer |
| "Have you ever been fired?" | Generally lawful; low risk if asked of all and job-focused | Keep, or use "Have you ever been let go from a job? What happened and what did you learn?" |
| "Long term" (quality) | Can become a stand-in for age bias | Assess interest in a career path and fit with our ladder, never age |
| "Professional in appearance" / Observe: "Dressed appropriately?" | Can penalize religious attire or race-associated hairstyles | Judge against the written dress code, with religious accommodation |

## Key Takeaways
- Ask desired pay, not pay history; set starting pay from the grade scale and placement.
- Ask only whether the candidate is authorized to work; complete the I-9 on time without specifying documents.
- Keep a complete, consistent hiring file and follow the retention schedule (at least one year federally, often longer).
- Use the reworded versions of the flagged kit items, and have HR and counsel approve final wording.
