> **Legal:** This course is general training for managers, not legal advice. Federal, state, and
> local laws differ and change. Our company's HR team and legal counsel set the final policies and
> should review any interview kit or job description. When in doubt, ask HR before you ask the
> candidate.

## The Core Principle

Employment decisions must be based on **job-related qualifications**, applied **consistently**.
It is illegal to make hiring decisions based on a protected characteristic, and it is risky even
to ask about one, because the question itself can be evidence that the answer influenced the
decision.

## Who Is Protected

Federal law, enforced by the U.S. Equal Employment Opportunity Commission (EEOC), prohibits
discrimination based on:

| Protected characteristic | Main federal law |
|---|---|
| Race, color | Title VII of the Civil Rights Act |
| Religion | Title VII |
| Sex, including pregnancy, sexual orientation, and gender identity | Title VII; Pregnancy Discrimination Act; Pregnant Workers Fairness Act |
| National origin | Title VII; immigration law also bars citizenship-status discrimination |
| Age 40 and over | Age Discrimination in Employment Act (ADEA) |
| Disability | Americans with Disabilities Act (ADA) |
| Genetic information, including family medical history | Genetic Information Nondiscrimination Act (GINA) |
| Military service and veteran status | USERRA |

Coverage thresholds vary (for example, Title VII and the ADA generally apply to employers with 15 or
more employees; the ADEA to 20 or more). Many **states and cities add protections** — marital
status, familial status, lawful off-duty conduct, hairstyle, criminal history, and others — and
often apply to smaller employers. Treat all of them as off limits for hiring decisions.

## Discrimination Can Be Unintentional

- **Disparate treatment:** treating someone differently because of a protected characteristic.
  ("We need someone who'll be around a long time" when passing over a 58-year-old.)
- **Disparate impact:** a neutral-looking rule that screens out a protected group more than others
  and is not job-related and consistent with business necessity. (A 75-lb lifting test when the job
  rarely exceeds 40 lbs; a car-ownership requirement for a job that doesn't drive.)

That is why every requirement should trace back to the job analysis in MGR-C01.

## Questions to Avoid and Better Alternatives

| Topic | Don't ask | Ask instead (job-related) |
|---|---|---|
| Age | "How old are you?" "When did you graduate?" "How long until you retire?" | "Are you at least 18?" (only where legally required for the job, such as operating certain equipment or driving) |
| National origin / citizenship | "Where are you from?" "Is English your first language?" "Are you a citizen?" | "Are you legally authorized to work in the U.S.?" "This job requires communicating with customers in English; can you do that?" (only if job-related) |
| Religion | "What church do you go to?" "Do you work on Sundays?" | "This job requires an on-call rotation including some weekends. Can you meet that schedule?" (accommodation handled by HR) |
| Family / marital status / pregnancy | "Are you married?" "Do you have kids?" "Planning a family?" "Who watches your kids when you're on call?" | "This job includes on call, overtime, and travel a few times a year. Can you meet those requirements?" |
| Sex / gender | "Can a woman handle this work?" "Would you be comfortable on an all-male crew?" | Describe the physical and schedule requirements; ask everyone the same question |
| Disability / health | "Any health problems?" "Ever filed workers' comp?" "How many sick days did you take?" | "Can you perform the essential functions of this job, with or without reasonable accommodation?" (See Lesson 2) |
| Military | "What type of discharge did you get?" | "What training or experience from your military service relates to this job?" |
| Arrests | "Have you ever been arrested?" | See Lesson 3 — conviction questions only where and when allowed |
| Pay history | "What do you make now?" | "What pay range are you looking for?" (See Lesson 4) |
| Personal life | "What are your personal goals?" (can invite family plans) | "What are your professional goals for the next few years?" |

> **Tip:** Small talk is where most problems start. "Nice ring — how long have you been married?"
> or "Where'd you get that accent?" feel friendly but put protected information on the table. Keep
> small talk to the drive in, the weather, and the job.

## When the Candidate Volunteers Information

Candidates sometimes volunteer protected information ("I'm a single mom," "I have a bad back").
Don't follow up, don't write it down, and don't let it influence you. Redirect: "Thanks for
sharing. Let's stay focused on the job." If the candidate raises a need for accommodation, tell
them you'll connect them with HR and let HR manage the interactive process.

## Consistency Is Protection

The structured interview from MGR-C03 is also your best legal protection:

1. The **same questions** for every candidate for the opening.
2. **Scores against anchors**, with **evidence notes**.
3. **Documented, job-related reasons** for selection and non-selection.
4. **No notes** about protected characteristics.

If a decision is ever challenged, a consistent, documented process is far easier to defend than
"I just had a feeling."

## Key Takeaways
- Hire on job-related qualifications applied consistently; don't ask about protected characteristics.
- Federal protections cover race, color, religion, sex (including pregnancy, sexual orientation, gender identity), national origin, age 40+, disability, genetic information, and military service; state and local laws add more.
- Neutral rules that aren't job-related can be discriminatory through disparate impact.
- Replace risky questions with job-related ones, usually "Can you meet this requirement?"
- Don't follow up on volunteered protected information; refer accommodation requests to HR.
- This is training, not legal advice; HR and counsel set final policy.
