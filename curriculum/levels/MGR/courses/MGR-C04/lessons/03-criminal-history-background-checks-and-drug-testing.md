> **Legal:** General training, not legal advice. Fair-chance, background-check, and drug-testing
> laws vary widely by state and city. Follow our company's written policies and HR's direction.

## Arrests vs. Convictions

- An **arrest** shows only that someone was suspected of something. It is not proof of conduct.
  EEOC guidance says arrest records alone should not be used to deny employment; many states bar
  employers from asking about arrests that didn't lead to conviction. **Don't ask "Have you ever been
  arrested?"**
- A **conviction** is evidence that the conduct occurred. It may be considered where it is
  job-related, but blanket bans ("no one with any record") risk disparate-impact discrimination and
  may violate state or local law.

## Fair-Chance ("Ban the Box") Laws

Many states, counties, and cities restrict **when** employers can ask about criminal history —
often not on the application and not until after an interview or a conditional offer — and **how**
they must evaluate it. Some also require specific notices before rejecting someone because of a
record. Federal contractors and agencies have their own rules.

Our practice, unless HR directs otherwise:

1. Remove criminal-history questions from applications and interviews.
2. Run background checks only after a **conditional offer**, if the role requires one.
3. Use an **individualized assessment** of any conviction, considering:
   - The **nature and gravity** of the offense.
   - The **time elapsed** since the offense or completion of the sentence.
   - The **nature of the job** (for example, a technician working unsupervised in schools or homes
     versus a warehouse role).
   - Evidence of rehabilitation, work history since, and the candidate's explanation.
4. Let HR make or approve any decision based on criminal history.

> **Tip:** Fair-chance hiring has a business case too. Many people with old records become loyal,
> long-term technicians. The individualized assessment protects customers and gives qualified
> people a fair shot.

## The Fair Credit Reporting Act (FCRA)

When our company uses an outside vendor (a consumer reporting agency) for criminal background
checks, **motor vehicle records (driving records)**, employment verification, or similar reports,
the federal FCRA applies. The steps:

1. **Stand-alone disclosure:** a clear, written notice, in a document consisting solely of the
   disclosure, that a report may be obtained.
2. **Written authorization** from the candidate before ordering.
3. **Pre-adverse action notice:** if you may reject based on the report, first give the candidate a
   copy of the report and the "Summary of Your Rights Under the FCRA," and allow a reasonable time
   (commonly about five business days) to dispute or explain.
4. **Adverse action notice:** if you then reject, send the final notice with the required
   information (vendor contact details, that the vendor did not make the decision, the right to a
   free copy and to dispute).

Some states add their own disclosures and waiting periods. HR runs this process; managers must not
order reports on their own or make a final decision before step 3 is complete.

## Driving Records for Driving Roles

For technicians who drive company vehicles, an acceptable driving record is a legitimate
requirement, usually set by our company policy and insurer. Good practice:

- State the standard in writing (for example, number of moving violations in a set period; no
  major violations such as DUI within a set period). The kit's "valid driver's license with only a
  few minor tickets" should be translated into the written standard.
- Apply the standard only to roles that actually drive, and apply it the same way to everyone.
- In interviews, ask "Can you meet our driving-record standard of ___?" rather than "Is your
  record clean?"
- Run the motor vehicle record through the FCRA process after a conditional offer.

## Drug and Alcohol Testing

| Point | Guidance |
|---|---|
| Written policy | Testing must follow our company's written policy: who is tested, when (pre-employment, post-accident, reasonable suspicion, random), which substances, and consequences |
| Consistency | Test everyone in the covered job category the same way; don't single out individuals |
| State law | Many states regulate testing procedures and notice; several protect off-duty cannabis use or restrict pre-employment cannabis testing for some roles, often with exceptions for safety-sensitive jobs |
| DOT-regulated drivers | Employees who need a CDL for their job are subject to federal DOT testing rules |
| ADA | Testing for illegal drugs is not a medical exam; but questions about prescription medications are medical questions and are off limits pre-offer |
| Confidentiality | Results go to HR, not into interview notes |

Our work at heights and around electrical equipment is safety-sensitive, which is often relevant
under state law. HR will tell you exactly how our policy applies in your state.

## Key Takeaways
- Never ask about arrests; consider convictions only when and how the law allows.
- Remove criminal-history questions from applications and interviews unless HR directs otherwise; check after a conditional offer.
- Use an individualized assessment: offense, time elapsed, nature of the job, rehabilitation.
- Vendor background and driving-record checks follow the FCRA: stand-alone disclosure, authorization, pre-adverse notice with report and rights summary, waiting period, adverse action notice.
- Drug testing follows the written policy and state law, applied consistently; medication questions are off limits pre-offer.
