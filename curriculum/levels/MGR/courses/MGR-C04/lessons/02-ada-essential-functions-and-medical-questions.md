> **Legal:** General training, not legal advice. State disability laws may be broader than the ADA.
> HR and counsel make final determinations, and HR handles all accommodation requests.

## What the ADA Requires of Hiring Managers

The Americans with Disabilities Act prohibits discrimination against qualified individuals with
disabilities. For hiring, three ideas matter most:

1. **Qualified individual:** someone who meets the job's legitimate requirements and can perform
   the **essential functions** with or without **reasonable accommodation**.
2. **Reasonable accommodation:** a change in how the job is done that lets a qualified person do
   it, unless it would cause undue hardship. Examples: a lifting aid, a modified schedule, a
   different way to receive dispatch messages.
3. **Strict limits on medical questions**, which change at each stage of hiring.

## The Three Stages

| Stage | Disability-related questions and medical exams | What you can do |
|---|---|---|
| **Pre-offer** (application, screens, interviews) | **Not allowed.** No questions about disabilities, health conditions, medications, prior injuries, workers' compensation history, or sick leave used | Describe essential functions and ask whether the candidate can perform them, with or without reasonable accommodation; ask everyone to describe or demonstrate a function only if you ask everyone |
| **Post-offer, before start** | A medical exam or health questions are allowed **only if** required of **all** entering employees in the same job category, kept confidential and separate from the personnel file | Make the offer conditional on the exam (if the company uses one); results can be used only in job-related, business-necessity ways |
| **Employment** | Must be job-related and consistent with business necessity | HR manages the interactive process |

A test for **illegal drug use** is not a "medical exam" under the ADA and may be given pre- or
post-offer under company policy and state law (see Lesson 3).

## Rewording the Physical Questions

Our trade has real physical demands, and it's fair to discuss them. The key is to ask about the
**job function**, not the person's body or health.

| Risky | Compliant |
|---|---|
| "How much weight can you lift on a consistent basis?" | "This job requires frequently lifting, carrying, and positioning fixtures and materials up to 50 lbs. Can you perform this function, with or without reasonable accommodation?" |
| "Do you have any issues with heights?" | "This job requires regular work from ladders, scissor lifts, and boom lifts, often 20+ feet up. Can you perform this function, with or without reasonable accommodation?" |
| "Are you healthy?" / "Healthy" on the checklist | Remove. List the specific functions (lifting, climbing, outdoor work up to 8 hours) instead |
| "Any back problems?" "Ever been hurt on the job?" | Don't ask pre-offer |
| "How many sick days did you take last year?" | "Our attendance expectations are ___. Can you meet them?" (Ask former supervisors whether attendance met expectations, excluding protected leave — see MGR-C05) |
| "Can you work around live electricity?" | "This job involves working near electrical equipment. We train technicians to de-energize, lock out, and verify before work, and energized work is done only by qualified persons. Are you able and willing to follow those procedures and work in that environment, with or without reasonable accommodation?" |

> **Tip:** Avoid asking only some candidates to demonstrate a physical task because of how they
> look. If you use a job-related physical demonstration (for example, setting up an extension
> ladder), ask **every** candidate for the role to do it, and run it safely.

## If the Answer Is "I'd Need an Accommodation"

Pre-offer, if a candidate with an obvious or disclosed disability says they'd need an accommodation,
you may ask how they would perform the function. In practice, the safest step for a manager is to
thank them and **refer the matter to HR**, which will run the interactive process. Do not:

- Assume an accommodation is impossible.
- Decide on your own that the person is "a safety risk." A direct-threat determination must be an
  individualized assessment based on objective evidence, made with HR.
- Reject the candidate because of the accommodation request.

## Confidentiality

Any medical information you learn, from any source, must be kept confidential and separate from
the regular personnel file. Don't write it in interview notes or the app scorecard, and don't share
it with the crew.

## Pregnancy

The Pregnant Workers Fairness Act requires covered employers to provide reasonable accommodations
for known limitations related to pregnancy, childbirth, or related conditions. In hiring, never
ask about pregnancy or plans to have children. If a candidate discloses a pregnancy, don't factor
it in; route any accommodation question to HR.

## The Smoking Policy Question

The kit asks: "Our policy prohibits smoking in certain areas (company and customer property). Can you
comply with this policy?" Asking about compliance with an on-the-job policy is generally fine
because it is about conduct at work. Some states protect lawful off-duty tobacco or cannabis use,
so don't ask whether a candidate smokes or uses tobacco away from work.

## Key Takeaways
- Pre-offer: no disability, health, medication, injury, workers' comp, or sick-leave questions.
- Describe essential functions and ask everyone whether they can perform them, with or without reasonable accommodation.
- Post-offer medical exams are allowed only if required of all entering employees in that job category, and kept confidential.
- Replace "Healthy" and "How much can you lift?" with specific essential-function wording.
- Refer accommodation requests to HR; never decide "safety risk" on your own.
