## Use a Screening Grid

Before opening the first resume, turn your must-haves into a short grid. Every application gets the
same grid. This keeps the process fast and fair, and gives you a record of why each person did or
did not advance.

| Criterion | Source | Result |
|---|---|---|
| Valid driver's license (driving roles) | Application | Yes / No / Unknown |
| Hands-on mechanical or trade experience | Resume | Strong / Some / None |
| Customer-facing experience | Resume | Strong / Some / None |
| Required license (electrician roles) | Application | Number listed? Verify later |
| Availability for on call and travel | Application or phone screen | Confirm on phone |

Advance candidates who meet the must-haves. Do not reject for missing preferences.

## What Resumes Can and Cannot Tell You

Resumes show history and claims. They do **not** reliably show safety attitude, honesty, or
work ethic. Their real value is to tell you **what to ask about** in the phone screen and interview.

> **Tip:** Many excellent technicians write poor resumes. Spelling, layout, and fancy formatting
> are not job requirements for a field technician (written work orders are, and you can test that
> directly). Don't screen out a mechanic because the resume is one plain page.

## Job-Hopping: Ask, Don't Assume

Several short jobs can mean an unreliable worker, but they can also mean:

- Seasonal or project-based work, normal in construction and installation.
- Staffing-agency assignments listed as separate jobs.
- Layoffs, company closures, or a family relocation.
- A young worker figuring out what they want, now looking for a career.

Treat a pattern as a **question to ask**, not a reason to reject. The company kit already has good
questions for this: "Why did you/are you leaving your last position?" and "Walk me through the last
three positions you held... How long did you hold each?" Listen for whether the reasons are
consistent, whether the candidate takes some responsibility, and whether our job fixes the
problem they describe (for example, "I wanted training and a path up").

## Employment Gaps: Handle With Care

Gaps happen for many reasons, some of which are legally protected or sensitive: military service,
pregnancy and caregiving, illness or disability, education, incarceration, or simply a slow job
market.

Do:
- Ask a neutral, open question: "I see a gap between these two jobs. Is there anything you'd like
  to tell me about what you were doing?"
- Accept a brief answer and move on to job-related topics.

Don't:
- Ask "Were you sick?", "Were you having a baby?", or "Were you in jail?"
- Reject automatically for a gap.

> **Legal:** Gaps caused by disability, pregnancy, or military service touch protected categories,
> and some jurisdictions restrict asking about criminal history until later in the process (see
> MGR-C04). A neutral question lets the candidate share what they choose. This is general guidance,
> not legal advice.

## Other Fairness Traps

| Trap | Better practice |
|---|---|
| Rejecting "overqualified" candidates | Ask what they want from the role; experienced people may want stability or a new path. Be cautious: "overqualified" can be a stand-in for age bias |
| Favoring schools or employers you know | Score against the grid |
| Screening by name, address, or graduation year | Ignore them; some managers hide them when screening |
| Using unverified "gut" impressions from photos or social media | Don't search candidates' personal social media for screening; it exposes protected information you can't unsee |

## Record Your Decisions

For each applicant, record the grid result and the reason for advancing or not. Keep records for
the period our company's retention policy requires (federal rules generally require at least one
year for applications and hiring records; some laws require longer). See MGR-C04.

## Key Takeaways
- Screen every application against the same grid built from must-haves.
- Use resumes to decide what to ask, not to judge character.
- Treat job-hopping and gaps as questions, asked neutrally, not automatic rejections.
- Don't screen on name, address, graduation year, photos, or personal social media.
- Document why each applicant advanced or didn't, and keep records per policy.
