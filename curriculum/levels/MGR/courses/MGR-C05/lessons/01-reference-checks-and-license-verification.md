## Why Bother With References

Many managers skip references because "they only give you the good ones" or "companies only
confirm dates." Done well, a reference call is still one of the best ways to confirm what you heard
in the interview, especially about reliability, safety, and how the candidate treats customers and
coworkers. And for licensed roles, verifying the license isn't optional.

## Before You Call

1. **Get written authorization.** The candidate signs a release allowing us to contact references
   and former employers. HR keeps it.
2. **Ask the candidate for the right references.** At least two **former supervisors** or leads,
   ideally from the last two jobs. Coworkers and personal references are weaker. The kit asks for
   "the name and phone number for your last supervisor" — this is the most useful reference.
3. **Use the same reference form** with the same core questions for every candidate.
4. **Prepare.** Review your interview notes and pick one or two things you want to confirm.

> **Legal:** Ask references only job-related questions. Don't ask about health, disability,
> medical leave, workers' compensation, family, religion, age, or any other protected
> characteristic, and don't ask how many sick days the person took. If a reference volunteers such
> information, don't record it or use it.

## Questions That Get Real Answers

Yes/no questions get "yes." Open, specific questions get information.

| Instead of | Ask |
|---|---|
| "Was she a good worker?" | "What were her main responsibilities? What was she best at?" |
| "Was he reliable?" | "Did he meet your expectations for attendance and punctuality? Can you give me an example?" (exclude protected leave) |
| "Was she safe?" | "Tell me about how she handled safety on the job. Did you ever have to correct her on a safety issue?" |
| "Any problems?" | "What's one area where he could improve? What did you coach him on?" |
| "Did he get along with people?" | "How did he handle a difficult customer or a disagreement with a coworker?" |
| "Was she honest?" | "Was there ever a situation where you had to rely on her word, or trust her with customer property?" |
| — | "On a scale of 1–10, how would you rate his overall performance? What would make it a 10?" |
| — | "**Would you rehire her** if you had the opening?" |

The rehire question is often the most telling. Listen for hesitation, qualifiers ("probably,"
"depending on the role"), or a long pause. Follow up gently: "It sounded like you hesitated.
Is there anything I should know to set him up for success?"

> **Tip:** Call from the angle of helping the candidate succeed: "We're planning to bring Jamie on
> as a technician. What should I know to help Jamie get off to a good start?" Supervisors are often
> more candid when they feel they're helping.

## When Companies Won't Talk

Many employers limit references to dates and title. If you get that:

- Confirm dates of employment and title, and ask whether the person is eligible for rehire
  (some will answer).
- Ask the candidate for another supervisor who has left that company, or a lead or customer contact.
- Note "policy: dates/title only" on the form. That's neutral — not a negative mark against the
  candidate.

## Documenting the Call

Record on the reference form: date, reference's name, title, company, relationship, how long they
worked together, answers to each question (short quotes), and your summary of consistencies and
concerns compared with the interview. Two documented references are the standard for our company.

## Verifying Licenses and Certifications

For any licensed role, **verify the license directly with the issuing agency** — usually the state
electrical licensing board or the state's professional licensing division — not from a photo or a
wallet card.

1. Search the state's online license lookup by license number and name.
2. Confirm the **license type and class** (for example, apprentice, journeyman, master), that it
   is **active**, the **expiration date**, and any **disciplinary actions** listed.
3. Print or save the result with the date and add it to the hiring file.
4. If the candidate holds licenses in other states (the electrician kit asks this), verify each
   that matters for the role. Don't assume reciprocity; check the rules of the state where they'll
   work.
5. Verify claimed certifications that matter for the job (for example, a lift operator card or a
   trade-school certificate) with the issuer when practical.
6. Put the license expiration on the calendar so it doesn't lapse after hire.

A candidate who misrepresents a license or certification has given you an integrity answer more
important than any interview score.

## Key Takeaways
- Get written authorization, and call at least two former supervisors using the same form.
- Ask open, specific, job-related questions, including an improvement area, a 1–10 rating, and "Would you rehire?"
- Never ask references about health, leave, or protected characteristics.
- Treat "dates and title only" as neutral, and ask for alternate references.
- Verify licenses directly with the issuing agency: class, status, expiration, and discipline.
