## Everyone Has Biases

Bias is not a character flaw; it is how human brains take shortcuts. In interviewing, those
shortcuts cost us good technicians and lead to bad hires. The structured process you've learned —
same questions, anchored scores, evidence notes, independent scoring — exists largely to counter
bias. This lesson names the common ones so you can catch them.

## The Common Interviewer Biases

| Bias | What happens | Example in our trade | Countermeasure |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Halo / horns** | One strong positive (or negative) trait colors every score | Candidate is a veteran and confident, so every answer gets an 8 | Score each answer on its own evidence, immediately |
| **Similarity ("like me")** | We rate people like ourselves higher | Same hometown, same hunting hobby, same first trade | Stick to job-related questions; avoid personal small talk |
| **First impression** | Decision made in the first minutes; the rest of the interview just confirms it | Weak handshake or nervous start | Withhold judgment until all questions are scored; nervousness usually fades |
| **Contrast** | Candidates judged against the previous candidate, not the standard | Average answer seems great after a disastrous interview | Score against the anchors, not each other; take a break between interviews |
| **Central tendency** | Everyone gets a 5 to avoid hard calls | All scores cluster at 4–6 | Use the full scale when evidence supports it |
| **Leniency / strictness** | One interviewer scores everyone high (or low) | "I'm a tough grader" | Calibrate as a team using anchored examples |
| **Confirmation** | We hunt for evidence that supports our early view | Liked the resume, so ignore a weak safety answer | Score the answer given, and probe both strengths and weaknesses |
| **Stereotyping** | Assumptions based on group membership | "Women don't like lifts," "older guys won't learn the app" | Treat these as illegal assumptions; assess each person on evidence |
| **Recency** | The last answers count more than earlier ones | Strong finish hides a weak safety answer early | Score as you go, review all scores |

## "Culture Fit" Is Not a Competency

"Not a good fit" is one of the most common, and most dangerous, reasons given for rejecting a
candidate. It can hide similarity bias or stereotyping. If you believe someone wouldn't fit, name
the **job-related behavior** behind that belief:

- Not "he's not a fit," but "in three answers he described ignoring customer requests."
- Not "she wouldn't mesh with the crew," but "she scored 3 on Teamwork; both examples were of
  working alone and avoiding the group."

If you can't name the behavior, it is probably bias.

> **Legal:** Decisions based on stereotypes about protected groups (race, color, religion, sex
> including pregnancy, sexual orientation and gender identity, national origin, age 40+, disability,
> genetic information, and others under state law) are illegal discrimination, even when no one
> intends harm. Evidence-based scoring and notes are your best protection.

## Practical Habits That Reduce Bias

1. **Prepare the questions in advance** and ask them in the same order.
2. **Skip personal small talk.** Talk about the drive in, the weather, the job.
3. **Score each answer immediately** against the anchors.
4. **Use panels** and score independently before discussing.
5. **Have the most junior interviewer share first** in debriefs so senior opinions don't anchor
   everyone.
6. **Take a five-minute break** between back-to-back interviews to reset.
7. **Review your own patterns.** Every few months, look at your scores. Do you score some groups
   consistently higher or lower? Does one interviewer always score high?
8. **Separate "likeable" from "capable."** Ask yourself: "Would I score this answer the same if it
   came from someone I didn't click with?"

## Nervous Candidates and Non-Traditional Backgrounds

Many good technicians are not polished interviewers. They may be nervous, soft-spoken, or new to
formal interviews. Help them give you real evidence:

- Explain the format and that you'll ask for specific examples.
- Rephrase a question once if they seem confused.
- Accept examples from any part of life when work examples are thin (school projects, military,
  sports teams, volunteer work, fixing cars), and score the behavior, not the setting.
- Don't confuse confidence with competence. The kit's Observe item "Confidence/enthusiasm?" is a
  secondary signal, not a competency score.

> **Tip:** Some of our best technicians were quiet in their interviews but gave specific, honest
> examples. Some of our worst hires were great talkers. The anchors and the notes keep you honest.

## Key Takeaways
- Bias is normal; the structured process is designed to counter it.
- Know the big ones: halo/horns, similarity, first impression, contrast, central tendency, confirmation, stereotyping, recency.
- "Culture fit" must be backed by a specific, job-related behavior, or it should not be used.
- Score immediately, score independently, use panels, and have junior interviewers speak first.
- Help nervous or non-traditional candidates show real evidence; don't confuse confidence with competence.
