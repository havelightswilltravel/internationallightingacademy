## Why Behavior-Based Questions Work

The best predictor of how someone will act in the future is how they **actually acted** in similar
situations in the past. Hypothetical questions ("What would you do if...?") tell you what a
candidate knows is the right answer. Behavior-based questions ("Tell me about a time when...")
tell you what they did.

Analytical Thinking, Building Relationships, Business Systems Thinking, Caution, Communication,
Customer Orientation, Decision Making, Detail-Oriented, Evaluating Alternatives, Initiative,
Interpersonal Skills, Innovation, Integrity, Listening, Organizational, Personal Effectiveness,
Problem Resolution, Resolving Conflict, Self Assessment, and Teamwork — has one or more questions
that ask for a real example. In the app, open **Hiring** to create a

Structured, behavior-based interviews (same questions, scored against a standard) predict job
performance substantially better than unstructured "let's chat" interviews. They are also fairer
and easier to defend, because every candidate is measured the same way.

## STAR: The Shape of a Complete Answer

A complete behavioral answer has four parts:

| Letter | Part | What you need to hear | Probe if missing |
|---|---|---|---|
| **S** | Situation | Where, when, who was involved | "Where were you working? When was this?" |
| **T** | Task | What the candidate was responsible for | "What was your role? What were you supposed to do?" |
| **A** | Action | What **they** did, step by step | "What did you do first? Then what? What did you say?" |
| **R** | Result | What happened, and what they learned | "How did it turn out? What would you do differently?" |

The **Action** is the most important part. Listen for "I" rather than "we." If you hear "we fixed
it," ask, "What was your part?"

## Ask for the Negative Example

The kit's instructions are clear: **look for both positive and negative examples.** If the
candidate only gives positive answers, ask for a situation that did not go well. Several kit
questions are built to do this directly:

- "Give me a specific example of a time when you used **poor** judgment and logic in solving a
  problem."
- "Everyone has made some poor decisions or has done something that just did not turn out right.
  What happened?"
- "Tell about a time when you were **not** able to build a successful relationship with a difficult
  person."
- "Tell about a time when you took responsibility for an error and were held personally
  accountable."

Why it matters: everyone makes mistakes. A candidate who can describe one honestly, own it, and
explain what they changed is showing Self Assessment, Integrity, and Personal Effectiveness — all on
our qualities list ("Recognizes their strengths and weaknesses"). A candidate who claims never to
have made a mistake is either not self-aware or not being candid.

How to ask without making the candidate defensive:

1. Normalize it: "Everybody has jobs that go sideways. I'm interested in how people handle them."
2. Be specific: "Tell me about a service call or project that didn't go the way you wanted."
3. Wait. Silence is fine; give them 10–15 seconds to think.
4. Probe the learning: "What did you do differently afterward?"

## Follow-Up Probes That Get Real Answers

Keep a short list of neutral probes and use them on every answer:

- "Walk me through exactly what you did."
- "What were you thinking at that point?"
- "What did the customer (or supervisor) say?"
- "What was the result? How do you know?"
- "If that happened tomorrow, would you do anything differently?"
- "Can you give me another example?"

Avoid **leading** probes that signal the answer you want ("You'd always lock out first, right?").
Avoid **closed** probes that invite yes/no.

> **Tip:** If a candidate can't think of an example, offer to come back to the question later. If
> they still can't answer, circle **N** for Answered and move on. Don't fill the silence with your
> own example — the candidate will just repeat it back to you.

## Watch for the Hypothetical Dodge

Candidates often answer "Tell me about a time..." with "Well, I would..." That is a hypothetical.
Redirect politely: "That makes sense. Can you tell me about a specific time you actually did that?"
The kit's "Items to Watch for" lists this first: *an answer states thoughts or opinions but does not
give a specific example.*

## Key Takeaways
- Past behavior in similar situations is the best predictor of future behavior.
- Structured interviews with the same questions and a scoring standard are more accurate and more defensible.
- Use STAR to make sure every answer has a situation, task, action, and result; focus on the candidate's own actions.
- Always get at least one negative example; honest ownership of a mistake is a strong signal.
- Use neutral, open probes and redirect hypothetical answers to real examples.
