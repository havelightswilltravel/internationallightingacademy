## Tell the Truth Early

New technicians most often quit in the first 90 days because **the job was not what they expected**.
They did not picture being called out at 2 a.m., spending a week away on a store rollout, or
carrying fixtures across a hot parking lot. A **realistic job preview (RJP)** shows the hard parts
up front so candidates who would not stay can opt out before we invest in them, and the ones who
stay arrive with eyes open.

Research on RJPs consistently shows modest but real reductions in early turnover. In our trade, the
payoff is large because the first months of training are the most expensive.

## What to Preview

Use the working conditions from your job analysis. For a lighting technician at our company these
typically include:

| Condition | How to describe it honestly |
|---|---|
| On call | "Techs rotate on call. When it's your week, you may be called nights or weekends and need to respond within the set time." |
| Odd hours and shifts | "Retail and office customers often want work done after closing or before opening. Some weeks are nights." |
| Overtime | "Busy seasons and big projects mean overtime. Here's what a typical month looks like." |
| Travel | "A few times a year you may travel for up to a few weeks on multi-site projects. Here's how lodging and per diem work." |
| Outdoor work | "Parking lot and building exterior work can mean up to 8 hours outside in heat, cold, or wind." |
| Physical work | "You'll lift and carry fixtures and materials up to about 50 lbs, climb ladders, and work from lifts." |
| Heights and lifts | "Much of the work is done from ladders, scissor lifts, and boom lifts. We train and certify you." |
| Tools and phone | "You'll build a hand-tool set on our schedule, and you'll use a smartphone for work orders and dispatch." |
| Electrical hazards | "You'll work near electrical equipment. We train you to work de-energized and lock out; energized work is only by qualified people." |

> **Legal:** Describe the conditions, then ask every candidate the same question: "Can you perform
> these functions, with or without reasonable accommodation?" Do not ask why someone might not be
> able to, and do not ask about medical conditions. See MGR-C04.

## How to Deliver an RJP

1. **In the job ad.** One short paragraph on on call, travel, and physical work.
2. **On the phone screen.** Read the conditions aloud and confirm availability.
3. **In the interview.** Show the day-in-the-life video or photos, and let a current technician
   answer questions without a manager present.
4. **Optional ride-along or shop tour** for finalists, following company insurance and safety rules
   (visitors observe only — no tools, no ladders, no work).

> **Tip:** Avoid overselling. If you say "travel is rare" and the new hire is gone for three weeks in
> month two, you have broken trust. Give real numbers from last year.

## The Training Ladder: Our Strongest Recruiting Tool

Many employers offer "on-the-job training" that means "follow Joe around." Our program is different,
and candidates should hear about it:

- **A clear ladder.** Lighting Technician I through V (the fifth is the Certified Advanced Lighting
  Technician), then an Electrical Development track of four apprentice levels and journeyman exam
  preparation for those who want to become electricians.
- **Advancement by competence, not tenure.** Each level requires passing course quizzes, a final
  exam, verified on-the-job hours, and evaluator-observed hands-on skills. A motivated tech controls
  their own pace.
- **Pay tied to grade** (where our company uses the pay scale feature). Technicians can see their
  current rate and the rate at the next grade.
- **Placement for experienced hires.** An experienced candidate does not restart at the bottom; a
  documented placement assessment can place them at the right grade.
- **A path to a license.** For candidates who want to become electricians, the electrical track is
  structured to align with a typical registered apprenticeship. (State rules decide which hours
  count; do not promise licensure.)

How to present it in an interview:

1. Show the ladder in the app on a tablet or printout.
2. Point to where this candidate would start.
3. Explain what it takes to reach the next grade and what that means for pay.
4. Connect it to the candidate's stated goals ("You said you want to be an electrician in five
   years; here's how that path works here.").

This speaks directly to "Motivated to make money and grow" and "Long term" on our qualities list.
Candidates with those qualities will light up; candidates who shrug are telling you something too.

> **Legal:** Describe pay and advancement accurately and avoid guarantees ("you'll be at LT3 in a
> year"). Describe typical timing as guidance and say advancement depends on demonstrated
> competence.

## Key Takeaways
- Early turnover often comes from surprise; a realistic job preview reduces it.
- Preview on call, odd hours, overtime, travel, outdoor work, lifting, heights, tools, and electrical hazards with real numbers.
- Ask every candidate whether they can perform the functions with or without reasonable accommodation.
- The grade ladder, competence-based advancement, pay by grade, and placement are strong recruiting advantages.
- Never guarantee promotion timing or licensure.
