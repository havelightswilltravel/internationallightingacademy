## Why Start Before the Job Ad

Most bad hires are decided before the first interview. If a manager cannot say clearly what the
job requires, every interviewer will invent their own standard, and the person who "felt right"
gets the offer. A short **job analysis** fixes that. It gives you:

- A list of the **essential functions** — the duties the job exists to perform.
- The **knowledge, skills, and abilities** needed on day one versus what our training will teach.
- Honest **working conditions** to show candidates.
- A fair, consistent yardstick for screening, interviewing, and later for performance reviews.

It also protects our company. Under the Americans with Disabilities Act (ADA), a written job
description prepared **before** you advertise is evidence of what the essential functions are.
Under equal employment opportunity rules, requirements must be **job-related and consistent with
business necessity**. Both are much easier to show when the analysis came first.

## How to Do a Job Analysis in One Afternoon

1. **Pull what exists.** Old job descriptions, the interview kit, recent work orders, and the
   program syllabus for the level you are hiring into.
2. **Watch the work.** Ride along with a strong technician for at least half a day, or interview
   two or three good performers: "What do you do in a typical week? What's hardest? What do new
   people struggle with?"
3. **List every task.** Write down what is actually done, not what you wish were done.
4. **Rate each task** on three questions:
   - How often is it done? (daily, weekly, rarely)
   - What happens if it is done badly or not at all?
   - Could another person on the crew reasonably do it instead?
5. **Sort the list** into the three buckets in the table below.
6. **Write it down** and have a second manager or HR review it.

| Bucket | Test | Lighting technician examples |
|---|---|---|
| **Essential function** | The job exists to do it; it is done often or the consequence of not doing it is serious; it cannot easily be reassigned | Work safely from ladders and lifts; carry and position fixtures and materials; drive a company vehicle to job sites (for driving roles); follow lockout/tagout; communicate with customers; complete work orders |
| **Important but trainable** | Needed to succeed, but our program teaches it | Troubleshooting ballasts and drivers; meter use; reading lighting plans; code knowledge |
| **Preference** | Helpful, not required | Prior lighting experience; a specific software; a particular trade school |

> **Tip:** The company's own candidate list says it plainly: "Knowledge of lighting and electrical is
> a plus but not necessary." That is a preference, not a requirement. Do not quietly turn it into a
> knock-out factor during screening.

## Essential Functions Are About Outcomes

Write essential functions as **what must be accomplished**, not how a particular body does it.
Compare:

| Weak (method-based) | Better (outcome-based) |
|---|---|
| "Must be able to lift 50 lbs." | "Lift, carry, and position fixtures, ballasts, ladders, and materials weighing up to 50 lbs, frequently, with or without reasonable accommodation." |
| "Must have perfect hearing." | "Communicate with customers, coworkers, and dispatch on site and by phone or text." |
| "Must be healthy." | (Remove. Replace with the specific physical tasks, such as climbing ladders and working outdoors up to 8 hours.) |

Outcome-based wording lets you assess every candidate the same way and opens the door to
accommodation discussions that the law expects.

> **Legal:** Physical requirements should be backed by what the job really demands. If a role
> rarely lifts more than 30 lbs, a 50-lb requirement can screen out candidates unfairly and is hard
> to defend. Base the number on observed work. This lesson is training, not legal advice; HR and
> counsel should approve final job descriptions.

## Requirements That Differ by Role

Our company hires into more than one level. Keep separate profiles:

- **Entry-level lighting technician (LT1 hire):** mechanical aptitude, reliability, safety
  attitude, willingness to learn. Electrical knowledge is a plus.
- **Experienced lighting technician:** proven fixture and control troubleshooting; will be placed
  at a higher grade after a placement assessment.
- **Electrician:** the required state license (journeyman or higher, as the role demands), proven
  areas of expertise, safe work history, and comfort with lifts and heights.

Do not use the electrician profile to screen entry-level technicians, and do not hire an electrician
into a licensed role without verifying the license.

## What You End Up With

A one-page job profile with: job title and level, summary, essential functions, working
conditions, day-one requirements, trainable skills, preferences, and the competencies you will
interview for. The next lesson builds the candidate profile from that page.

## Key Takeaways
- Do the job analysis before the job ad; it drives screening, interviewing, and reviews.
- Sort every task into essential, trainable, or preference, and write essential functions as outcomes.
- Physical and licensing requirements must reflect real work and be job-related.
- "Lighting knowledge is a plus" is a preference; our training program teaches the rest.
- Keep separate profiles for entry-level technicians, experienced technicians, and electricians.
