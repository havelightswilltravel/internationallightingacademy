## Every Splice Belongs in a Box

The NEC requires splices and terminations to be made inside a box, conduit body, fitting,
wiring compartment of listed equipment (such as a fixture wireway), or other approved
enclosure (Article 300). The box contains heat and sparks if a connection fails and
protects the splice from damage.

Field rules:
- **No splices hanging in the ceiling space**, inside a raceway, or buried in insulation.
- **Boxes must remain accessible** — not covered by drywall or permanently sealed above a
  hard ceiling without an access panel.
- **Every box gets a cover** (blank cover, device, luminaire canopy, or fixture wireway
  cover).
- **Unused openings** in boxes, fittings and panels must be closed with listed plugs or
  plates.

## Box Selection and Fill

Boxes come in metal (steel) and nonmetallic types, in standard sizes like 4-in square,
4-11/16-in square, octagon and device boxes. A box must be large enough for the conductors
and devices in it. The NEC box-fill rules (Article 314) assign a volume to each conductor
based on size:

| Conductor size | Volume per conductor |
|---|---|
| 14 AWG | 2.00 cu in |
| 12 AWG | 2.25 cu in |
| 10 AWG | 2.50 cu in |

Each conductor that enters and is spliced or terminated counts as one; internal cable clamps,
devices (yokes) and equipment grounding conductors add allowances. The box's volume is
stamped inside it. A 4-in square × 1-1/2-in deep steel box is about 21 cu in — roughly nine
12 AWG conductors before any other allowances. EA2 covers full box-fill calculations. At
LT2: **if a box is crammed and you cannot fold the conductors in without force, it is
probably overfilled.** Use a deeper box or an extension ring and tell your lead.

### Free conductor length

The NEC (300.14) requires at least **6 in** of free conductor at each box for splices or
connections, and where the box opening is less than 8 in in any dimension, at least **3 in**
must extend outside the opening. Don't cut conductors short — the next person needs enough
to work with.

## Wire Connectors

All splicing connectors must be **listed** (UL 486C covers splicing wire connectors) and
used within the wire size, number and type combinations printed on the package or box.

| Connector | Strengths | Watch out for |
|---|---|---|
| Twist-on ("wire nut") | Inexpensive, handles many combinations | Must be twisted until tight; skirt must cover all bare copper; check combination chart |
| Push-in | Fast, good for solid wire | Many are listed for solid conductors only — check before using stranded |
| Lever-actuated | Works with solid, stranded and fine-stranded; reusable | Larger size; must strip to the exact gauge length |
| Crimp sleeve | Permanent, compact (common for grounds) | Requires correct crimp tool |
| Split-bolt / set-screw | Larger conductors | Must be insulated; torque per instructions |

## Making a Good Twist-On Splice

1. **Verify absence of voltage** on every conductor in the box (LOTO, live-dead-live).
2. **Choose the connector** for the number and size of conductors (check the package chart).
3. **Strip** to the length shown on the package — usually about 1/2 to 5/8 in. Do not nick the
   copper; a nick creates a weak, hot spot.
4. **Align** conductor ends evenly. Pre-twisting with lineman's pliers is optional for some
   connectors and required for none, but follow the manufacturer's instructions.
5. **Twist** the connector clockwise until the conductors twist together outside the skirt
   and it is firm.
6. **Pull test** each conductor individually. Any movement means redo it.
7. **Inspect**: no bare copper below the skirt, no insulation inside the connector.
8. **Fold** conductors neatly into the box, grounds to the back, and keep splices away from
   sharp edges.

### Stranded to solid

Fixture leads are often stranded; building wire is often solid. With a twist-on connector,
let the stranded conductor extend slightly beyond the solid one so it is captured. With a
push-in connector, confirm it is listed for stranded wire, or use a lever-type connector.

> **Safety:** Loose splices are one of the most common causes of fixture failures,
> flickering, and fires. A bad splice on a neutral can cause voltage problems on other
> circuits in multiwire systems. Take the extra 30 seconds to pull-test every conductor.

## Terminating on Screws and Lugs

- Wrap solid conductors **clockwise** around a screw terminal so tightening draws the wire in.
- Only one conductor under a screw unless the terminal is listed for more.
- Do not use back-stab (push-in) holes on devices for anything but the wire size and type
  they are listed for; many techs avoid them entirely for reliability.
- Tighten to the torque marked on the equipment. The NEC (110.14) requires using a calibrated
  torque tool where a torque value is specified.

## Key Takeaways
- Splices and terminations go in accessible boxes or listed equipment wiring compartments, with covers on.
- Each conductor size has a box-fill volume; an overstuffed box needs a deeper box or extension ring.
- Leave at least 6 in of free conductor in each box.
- Use listed connectors within their printed wire combinations; check stranded-wire compatibility.
- Strip to the right length, no nicks, no exposed copper, and pull-test every conductor.
- Torque terminals to the marked value with a calibrated tool.
