## Why a Method Beats Experience Alone

Experienced techs often seem to "just know" what's wrong. What they actually have is a
method they run so often it looks automatic. Without a method, techs:
- Replace parts that weren't bad (wasting money and the customer's trust)
- Miss the real cause so the problem comes back
- Take unsafe shortcuts, such as testing in the wrong order or skipping verification
- Can't explain what they did

The method below is the company standard. It applies to a single dead troffer and to a
whole floor of flickering lights.

## The Seven Steps

| Step | Name | Key question |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Gather information | What is the problem, when did it start, what changed? |
| 2 | Verify the complaint | Can I see the problem myself? |
| 3 | Plan safely | What's the hazard, what's energized, what PPE and LOTO do I need? |
| 4 | Analyze possible causes | What could cause *this* symptom? Which are most likely and easiest to check? |
| 5 | Test and isolate | What do measurements tell me? Where exactly is the fault? |
| 6 | Repair | What is the right fix — for the failed part and for the root cause? |
| 7 | Verify and document | Does it work now under normal conditions? Have I recorded what I found? |

### 1. Gather information

Talk to the person who reported the problem and read the work order. Good questions:
- Which fixtures? All of them, some, or one?
- Is it out, dim, flickering, cycling, buzzing?
- When did it start? All the time or only sometimes (time of day, weather, when equipment
  starts)?
- Has anything changed — remodeling, new equipment, a power outage, someone "fixed" it?
- Has this happened before?

Write the answers down. Intermittent problems are often solved by the pattern the customer
noticed.

### 2. Verify the complaint

See it yourself. Flip the switch, wave at the sensor, check the time clock. Sometimes the
"problem" is a vacancy sensor that turned the lights off, a dimmer at its lowest setting,
or an HID fixture in its restrike delay. Also check: are other fixtures on the **same
circuit** working? This one check tells you whether to focus on the fixture or on the
circuit/control.

### 3. Plan safely

Before opening anything:
- Identify the voltage (panel label, fixture label, meter).
- Identify which tests require the circuit energized (voltage measurements) and which must be
  de-energized (resistance, continuity, any repair).
- Arrange access (ladder, MEWP by an authorized operator).
- Plan LOTO points and PPE.

> **Safety:** Troubleshooting often requires energized measurements, but **repairs never
> do.** Measure live with PPE and a tested meter; then lock out and verify before touching
> any conductor or component. Never "just quickly" swap a ballast with the circuit on.

### 4. Analyze possible causes

List what could cause the symptom (the next lesson gives tables). Then rank them:
- **Likely** — what fails most often? (Lamps, then ballasts/drivers, then connections and
  sockets, then controls, then circuits.)
- **Easy** — what is quick and cheap to check? (A known-good lamp beats a panel investigation.)
- **Safe** — what can be checked without energized exposure?

Start where those overlap.

### 5. Test and isolate

Use measurements, not guesses. Follow the power from the source toward the load, or use
**half-splitting** (Lesson 3) to cut the problem in half with each test. Change **one thing at
a time** and observe the result. If you change two things and it starts working, you don't
know which one fixed it.

### 6. Repair

Fix the failed component **and** ask why it failed (heat, voltage, wrong part, loose
connection, water). Replacing a driver that cooked because insulation was packed on a
non-IC fixture means you will be back.

### 7. Verify and document

Operate the system the way the customer does: from the normal switch, sensor, dimmer or time
clock. Check neighboring fixtures. Record the symptom, cause, repair and readings
(Lesson 4).

## When to Stop and Ask

Escalate to your lead when:
- Readings don't make sense or don't match the system you expected
- The fault is in a panel, feeder, transfer switch, or other equipment beyond your
  qualification
- You find hazardous conditions: missing grounds, heat damage, water in equipment, illegal
  wiring
- You've worked through the likely causes and still don't have an answer

Escalating is not failure. Guessing on energized equipment is.

## Key Takeaways
- Use the seven steps every time: gather, verify, plan safely, analyze, test and isolate, repair, verify and document.
- Check neighboring fixtures on the same circuit early — it splits fixture problems from circuit/control problems.
- Rank causes by likely, easy and safe.
- Measure energized with PPE; repair only after lockout and verification.
- Change one thing at a time, fix the root cause, and escalate when readings don't make sense.
