## Two Kinds of Work, Two Ways of Thinking

Most of what you have trained on so far is **service work**: something is broken, you go find out
why, and you fix it. A service call is a puzzle with one unknown. You arrive not knowing the
answer, you gather clues, you meter, you narrow it down, and you leave when the lights are on.

A **project** is a different animal. On a retrofit or fixture-replacement project, the answer is
already known before you leave the shop. Someone surveyed the building, counted the fixtures,
picked the kits or new fixtures, ordered the material and promised the customer a result, a
schedule and a price. Your job is not to discover what is wrong. Your job is to **deliver what was
promised, the same way, hundreds of times in a row**, without surprises for the customer or for
the office.

Techs who are great troubleshooters sometimes struggle on their first projects. That is not a
skill problem. It is a gear problem. Troubleshooting rewards curiosity and improvising. Projects
reward planning, repetition, communication and paperwork. Good senior techs learn to shift gears
on purpose.

| | Service call | Project |
|---|---|---|
| Starting point | A complaint you have to diagnose | A scope, a survey and a material list that already exist |
| Size | One fixture or one circuit | Dozens to thousands of fixtures |
| Time on site | Hours | Several shifts, nights or weeks |
| What wins | Finding the cause | Planning, flow and consistency |
| Biggest risks | Misdiagnosis, shock | Running short, damage, rework, unhappy customer, lost time |
| Who depends on you | The customer today | The customer, your crew, the office, the supplier and the recycler |
| Paperwork | One ticket | Daily recaps, counts, photos, survey changes, sign-offs |

## Why Our Company Cares About the Details

Our company has run a lot of projects: office buildings, grocery and retail stores, parking
structures, banks, schools, gas station canopies and walk-in coolers. Looking back across those
jobs, one pattern stands out. **The more details the crew had before the job kicked off, the
better the first night went.** When techs showed up already knowing where to park, who to meet,
how the material was getting there, where the recycling goes and what the customer cared about,
the project started fast and stayed smooth. When those details had to be discovered on site, or
a manager had to keep reminding the crew, the job lost hours on the first day and often never
caught up.

That is why every project at our company comes with a **project specifics sheet** (Lesson 2).
For years those sheets started with the same line in capital letters: *"Tech, read me before you
leave the shop. Also make sure you understand the attached survey before you go."* The goal of
this course is to get you to the point where you would ask every question on that sheet even if
nobody handed it to you.

## A Note on Relamp Jobs

Many of our older project sheets were written for group relamping, where a crew replaced every
lamp in a building at once. **Our company no longer does group relamping.** Aging fluorescent
and HID systems are now retrofitted with LED kits or replaced with new fixtures. The lessons in
this course still come from those older jobs, because the planning problems are the same: access,
lifts, staging, counts, recycling, customer communication and production. When you see an example
from a relamp, apply the lesson to the retrofit or fixture replacement you will actually be doing.

## The Project Mindset

Here is what shifting gears looks like in practice.

1. **Read before you roll.** Read the specifics sheet, the survey and the service order before you
   leave the shop. If anything does not make sense, ask your supervisor then, not from the job site.
2. **Plan the flow, not just the fixture.** Think about where material lives, how it gets to each
   lift, where removed parts go and who is waiting on whom.
3. **Repeat the same good install.** Every fixture should look like the sample install. Consistency
   is the product.
4. **Count everything.** Material in, material installed, material left over, old parts removed.
   Counts are how the office bills, orders and closes the job.
5. **Write it down and take the picture.** On a project, if it is not on the recap, it did not happen.
6. **Talk early.** Shortages, damage and scope surprises are small problems on day one and large
   problems on the last day.
7. **Be the professional on site.** Our company is the lighting professional on the job. That means
   we notice and write up anything that would make the finished job look unprofessional, even when
   it is not part of our scope.

> **Safety:** Projects add hazards that a single service call does not: long shifts, night work,
> several lifts in one area, material handling, and many circuits worked in a row. The safety rules
> do not change because the work is repetitive. Every circuit is locked out and verified
> live-dead-live before you touch conductors (see LT1-C06), every lift is inspected every shift, and
> fatigue is treated as a real hazard. If you are too tired to work safely, say so.

## Where This Course Fits

This course gets you ready to be a dependable crew member on a project and to start thinking like
a crew leader. Lead technicians learn to plan and mobilize large jobs in **LT5-C03 Lesson 5
(Mobilizing a Large Job)**, and the audits, scopes, takeoffs and labor estimates that create a
project are covered in **LT5-C01**. When you understand how a project was built, it is easier to
see why the details on your sheet matter.

## Key Takeaways
- A service call is a diagnosis; a project is a delivery of something already promised.
- Projects reward planning, repetition, counting, documentation and early communication.
- Our company's experience: the more details the crew had before kickoff, the better the job went.
- Read the project specifics sheet and survey before you leave the shop, and ask questions then.
- Group relamping is retired; apply the old project lessons to retrofits and fixture replacement.
- Safety rules do not relax on repetitive work. Lock out and verify every circuit, every time.
