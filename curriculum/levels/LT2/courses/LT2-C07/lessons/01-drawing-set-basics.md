## Why Print Reading Matters to a Lighting Tech

On service calls you may never see a drawing. On retrofit, renovation and new construction
jobs, drawings tell you **what goes where, what it is, and what circuit feeds it**. Reading
them correctly saves trips up the ladder and prevents installing the wrong fixture in the
wrong place on the wrong circuit. EA2-C05 covers commercial electrical drawings in depth;
this course gives you what you need for lighting work.

## How a Drawing Set Is Organized

A building's construction documents are a set of sheets grouped by **discipline**. Each sheet
number starts with a letter:

| Letter | Discipline | What a lighting tech looks for |
|---|---|---|
| G | General | Cover sheet, sheet index, code summary |
| A | Architectural | Floor plans, **reflected ceiling plans (RCPs)**, room names/numbers, ceiling heights and types |
| S | Structural | Rarely needed — sometimes for mounting high-bay fixtures |
| M | Mechanical | Ductwork and diffusers that compete with fixtures for ceiling space |
| P | Plumbing | Occasionally — sprinkler and pipe conflicts are usually on FP (fire protection) sheets |
| E | Electrical | **Lighting plans**, power plans, panel schedules, fixture schedules, one-line diagrams, details |

Electrical sheets are often numbered by type — for example, E0.x for symbols and notes,
E1.x for site, E2.x for lighting, E3.x for power, E5.x for details, E6.x for schedules —
but numbering varies by firm. **Always check the sheet index on the cover.**

## The Title Block

Every sheet has a title block, usually along the right edge or bottom. It contains:
- Project name and address
- Design firm and engineer's seal (on permitted sets)
- **Sheet title and number** (e.g., "E2.01 — Lighting Plan, Level 1")
- **Date** and **revision history**
- Drawn by / checked by
- Sometimes the scale

### Revisions

Drawings change. A revision is marked with a **cloud** around the changed area and a
**delta** (triangle) with the revision number, listed in the title block's revision table.

> **Safety:** Working from an outdated drawing can mean pulling the wrong circuit or locking
> out the wrong breaker. Confirm you have the **current** revision — ask your lead — and
> verify every circuit in the field before working on it. Drawings show design intent, not
> guaranteed field conditions.

## Scale

Plans are drawn to scale so that a measurement on paper represents a real distance.

| Scale | Meaning | Typical use |
|---|---|---|
| 1/8" = 1'-0" | 1 inch on paper = 8 feet | Floor plans for larger buildings |
| 1/4" = 1'-0" | 1 inch on paper = 4 feet | Smaller floor plans, enlarged areas |
| 1/2" = 1'-0" or larger | | Details, enlarged rooms |
| 1" = 20'-0" (engineer's scale) | 1 inch = 20 feet | Site plans |
| NTS | Not to scale | Diagrams, details |

**Watch out:** drawings printed at half size (common on job sites) are **not** at the stated
scale. Use the **graphic scale bar** on the sheet, which shrinks with the print, or measure
from known dimensions. Never scale a drawing for anything critical — use written dimensions
when they exist, and verify in the field.

### Measuring with an architect's scale

1. Find the face of the scale marked with the drawing's scale (e.g., "1/8").
2. Place zero at one point, read the distance in feet at the other.
3. Fractions of a foot are read from the subdivided section at the end of the scale.

Scale apps on phones and tablets work well with PDF drawings if calibrated to a known
dimension.

## North Arrow and Orientation

Plans show a **north arrow**. Use it with room numbers, column grid lines (letters one way,
numbers the other — e.g., "column line C-4") and fixed features (stairs, elevators) to orient
yourself in the field. Column grid references are the fastest way to describe a location to
someone else on a large floor.

## Notes

| Type | What it is |
|---|---|
| General notes | Apply to the whole sheet or the whole set ("All lighting circuits shall be 20 A, 277 V unless otherwise noted") |
| Keyed notes / sheet notes | Numbered symbols (often a number in a hexagon or circle) placed on the drawing, with the matching note in a list on the sheet |
| Abbreviations | Listed on the symbols/legend sheet: EM (emergency), NL (night light), UON or UNO (unless otherwise noted), AFF (above finished floor), TYP (typical), EX or (E) (existing), (N) new, (R) relocated, (D) demolish |

Notes often contain the most important instructions — read them **before** reading the plan.

## Key Takeaways
- Drawing sets are organized by discipline letters; lighting plans and schedules are on E sheets, ceiling plans on A sheets.
- Read the title block: sheet number, date, revision. Confirm you have the current revision.
- Half-size prints aren't to scale — use the graphic scale bar or written dimensions.
- Use the north arrow, column grid and room numbers to orient yourself.
- Read general and keyed notes and the abbreviation list before the plan itself.
- Drawings show design intent — always verify circuits in the field.
