## You Finished LT1. Now What?

You made it through Lighting Technician I. You know how to dress and protect yourself, how to set
up a ladder, how to lock out a circuit and prove it dead, how to handle lamps and ballasts, how to
work in heat and cold, how to treat a customer, and when to stop a job. That is a real
accomplishment. Most people who start in this trade do not get that far.

LT2 is where you start becoming a **technician** instead of a helper. You will learn building power
systems, meters on panels, ballasts and drivers, LED retrofits, troubleshooting and projects. The
work gets more technical, the jobs get bigger, and people start depending on you.

This course comes first in LT2 on purpose. Before we teach you a single new task, we want you to
understand two things:

1. **Your growth is your responsibility.** Our company will give you training, tools, trainers and
   chances. What you do with them is up to you.
2. **Safety is the foundation everything else stands on.** You cannot build skill on top of habits
   that will get you or somebody else hurt.

The rest of this course hits both of those hard. It is not a quick overview. Take it seriously.

## Nobody Can Do This for You

Your trainer cannot learn for you. Your supervisor cannot practice for you. The training app cannot
make you remember something you only skimmed. Your evolution as a technician is **firmly in your
hands**, and it comes down to four things you control every single day.

### 1. Work Ethic

Work ethic is not how tired you are at the end of the day. It is what you do with the time you are
paid for and the chances you are given. On a lighting crew, good work ethic looks like this:

- You arrive **early enough to be ready** at start time: boots on, truck checked, coffee finished.
  "On time" means working at start time, not pulling into the lot.
- You **look for the next thing to do** instead of waiting to be told. If the lift is moving, you are
  staging the next fixture. If the job is waiting on a shutdown, you are sorting recycle boxes or
  prepping kits.
- You **finish the job, including the boring parts**: cleanup, counts, labels, photos, paperwork.
- You **do it right the first time**, because a callback costs our company and the customer far more
  than the extra two minutes it would have taken.
- You **put the phone away** while you work. Personal calls and scrolling wait for breaks.

### 2. Commitment

Commitment is doing what you said you would do, when you said you would do it, even when nobody is
checking. It shows up in small things:

- If you say you will have the truck loaded by 6:30, it is loaded by 6:30.
- If you are going to be late or sick, you call your supervisor **before** start time, not after.
- If you start a training module, you finish it.
- If a trainer shows you a better way once, you do it that way from then on.

Our supervisors notice who can be counted on. Those are the people who get handed bigger jobs,
better crews and the chance to train others.

### 3. Attitude

You will get corrected a lot in LT2. Your trainer will stop you, redo something you did, or tell you
the way you have been doing it is wrong. How you take that matters more than almost anything else.

| Attitude that holds you back | Attitude that moves you forward |
|---|---|
| "I already know that." | "Show me again; I want to make sure I have it." |
| Getting quiet or defensive when corrected | "Got it. Why is it done that way?" |
| "That's not my job." | "What else can I help with?" |
| Blaming the material, the lift, the customer | "Here's what happened and what I'll do next time." |
| Hiding a mistake | Reporting it right away and helping fix it |

A good attitude is not about being cheerful all day. It is about being **coachable**, honest and
willing to do the work in front of you.

### 4. Investing in Yourself

The training app, the course lessons, the library and the quizzes are available to you all the time.
Your on-the-job hours teach you what you see. Study teaches you **why**, and it prepares you for
what you have not seen yet.

**Plan on about 4 hours of study a week. That time is on you.** It is the single biggest difference
between techs who move up and techs who stall. Here is one way to fit it in:

| Day | What to do | Time |
|---|---|---|
| Two weeknights | One lesson in the training app, with notes on anything you did not understand | 1 hour each |
| One weeknight | Review the week's jobs: write down one thing you learned and one question for your trainer | 30 minutes |
| Weekend | Library guide, manufacturer instructions for a product you installed, or quiz practice | 1 hour |
| Breaks during the week | Re-read your notes, look up a part number you saw | about 30 minutes total |

Bring your questions to work. When your trainer sees you have been studying, the conversations on the
job get better, and you get trusted with more.

> **Think ahead:** Before this week starts, look at your schedule and block out your 4 hours now.
> Put them in your phone calendar like any other appointment. If you wait to "find time," you won't.

## Advancement Is Earned by Demonstrated Skill

In our program you do not move up because of how long you have been here. You move up when you have
**shown** you can do the work:

- **Courses and quizzes** prove you understand the material.
- **On-the-job hours** give you real repetitions under supervision.
- **Skill sign-offs** are where an evaluator watches you do the task and checks you against written
  criteria. Critical skills must be done safely and correctly with no coaching.

The level descriptions list a typical duration, but that is only a guide. A tech who studies, asks
questions and practices on purpose can be ready sooner. A tech who coasts will take longer, no matter
how many months go by. Either way, the evaluator signs off on what they see you do, not on what you
say you can do.

That also means **you can see exactly where you stand.** Your transcript and skills list show what
you have completed and what is left. Check them at least once a month and talk to your supervisor
about which skills you can work toward next.

## Why We Start with Safety, Every Time

Here is how our company builds a technician, and how this course is laid out:

1. **Safety first.** You must have a firm grasp of what it means to be safe in your everyday tasks.
   Until safety is always in the back of your head, on every task, you are not ready to progress.
   Lessons 2 to 4 cover this.
2. **Then the task.** Once safety is automatic, we teach the task: see the whole job, think ahead,
   ask questions, check your course, keep the pace and stay aware. Lessons 5 to 9 cover this.
3. **Then speed.** Only after you have a firm grip on the safety factors and fully understand the
   task, the next step and the steps beyond, do you start pushing your pace (Lesson 8).

You will hear this order again and again in this course. It is not an accident.

## Other Habits of a Professional

These habits are part of your work ethic too. They are what customers, crew members and supervisors
judge you by.

- **Reliability and punctuality.** Be where you said you would be, ready to work. If something comes
  up, call early.
- **Fitness for duty.** Come to work rested, sober and unimpaired, with water and food for the day.
  If you are sick, hurt, on a medication that makes you drowsy, or just did not sleep, tell your
  supervisor before you climb anything. A tired tech on a ladder is a hazard.
- **Owning mistakes and near misses.** Everyone makes mistakes. Professionals report them right away,
  help fix them and learn from them. A near miss you report today can prevent an injury next month.
  Hiding one teaches nobody anything and destroys trust when it comes out, and it usually does.
- **Clean work area.** Keep your work area, the truck and the shop clean. Clutter causes trips, lost
  parts and damage, and customers judge our company by what they see.
- **Care of company equipment.** Treat tools, meters, ladders, lifts and vehicles like they cost
  money, because they do. Inspect them, use them for their intended purpose, put them back where they
  belong and report damage the same day.
- **Communication.** Tell your crew what you are doing, tell your trainer when you are stuck, and
  tell the customer what to expect. Silence on a job site causes most of the confusion.

> **Safety check:** Fitness for duty is a hazard you bring with you. Before you start any shift, ask
> yourself: Did I sleep? Did I eat? Do I have water? Am I on anything that slows me down? Am I upset
> enough to be distracted? If the answer to any of those worries you, the control is simple and comes
> first: tell your supervisor before the work starts. Do not wait until you are on a ladder.

## A Quick Self-Check

Be honest. Rate yourself 1 (not yet) to 5 (always) on each line, and write down the two lowest.
Those are your goals for the next month.

- I am ready to work at start time, every day.
- I look for the next task without being told.
- I take correction without getting defensive.
- I study about 4 hours a week.
- I report my mistakes and near misses right away.
- I know which skills I am working toward next.
- I take care of company tools and vehicles as if they were mine.

## Key Takeaways
- Your growth as a technician is your responsibility: work ethic, commitment, attitude and investing in yourself.
- Plan on about 4 hours of study a week, on your own time, and bring questions to work.
- Advancement is earned by demonstrated skill, checked by evaluators against written criteria, not by time served.
- The order is always safety first, then the task, then speed.
- Be reliable, fit for duty, honest about mistakes and near misses, clean and careful with equipment.
