## Two Habits That Keep a Job on Track

This lesson covers two habits that work together:

1. **Step back** several times during every task to check whether you are still on track, and correct
   course if you are not.
2. **Keep the pace:** keep up with or exceed the pace of the people you are working with, once you have
   earned the right to.

Stepping back keeps the work right. Pace keeps the work moving. You need both.

## Step Back Multiple Times During a Task

When you are working, your view gets narrow. You see the fixture in front of you and the screw in your
hand. That is fine for the step you are on, but it means you will not notice when the job as a whole
starts drifting: a quality problem that repeats, a crew that is slower than planned, a hazard that
crept in, a material count that will not last.

So you **step back on purpose**, several times during every task, and ask: **are we still on track?**

### When to Step Back

- **After the first one.** After the first fixture of a row, step back and look at it from the floor
  like the customer will. If it is wrong, only one is wrong.
- **At every natural break:** end of a row, lift move, break time, shutdown change.
- **Whenever something changes:** new area, new crew member, new material, store opening.
- **When something feels off:** you are behind, someone is frustrated, the same problem happened twice.
- **Before you leave:** the final step back is the walk-through before you call the task done.

### What to Ask When You Step Back

| Check | Ask yourself |
|---|---|
| **Safety** | Are all the controls still in place? Barricades still up, locks still on, the right circuits locked out for where we are now? Anything new in the area? |
| **Quality** | Does the work look right from the floor? Aligned, secure, clean, wired neatly, no fingerprints, lenses seated, no hardware left behind? |
| **Pace** | Are we where we planned to be? If not, why? |
| **Flow** | Is anyone waiting? Is the lift waiting on material? Is there a bottleneck? |
| **Material** | Will the count last? Are we using more of anything than expected? |
| **Scope** | Are we doing what the work order says? Has anything changed that the supervisor or customer needs to know? |
| **Next steps** | Is the forecast still right (Lesson 6)? |

### Correct Course

Stepping back only helps if you act on what you see. When you find a problem:

- **Fix quality problems now**, before more fixtures are done the same wrong way.
- **Fix the flow:** move the ladder plan, re-stage material, split tasks differently.
- **Fix safety gaps immediately**, before anything else.
- **Report what you cannot fix:** tell your lead or supervisor early, with the reason.

> **Safety:** If you step back and find a safety control is missing (a barricade knocked down, a lock
> that is not on a circuit you are now working on, a person under the lift), stop the work, fix the
> control, then continue. Safety corrections never wait until "after this fixture."

## Quality: Do It Right the First Time

Stepping back is a quality habit. Our company cares about quality because:

- A callback costs more than doing it right: a truck, a tech, travel time, and a customer who trusts us
  a little less.
- Some quality problems are safety problems: a fixture not secured per the instructions can fall; a
  splice outside an enclosure can fault.
- Customers judge our company by what they see when the job is done.

Do it right the first time: follow the instructions, secure every part, dress the wiring, clean the
lens, check it from the floor. Then step back and check again.

## Keep the Pace

On a crew, pace matters. Customers are paying for a job to be done in a reasonable time, our company
bid the work expecting a certain pace, and a slow crew member makes the whole crew wait.

Our expectation for you is direct:

- **Keep up with, or exceed, the pace of others** on the job.
- **If your trainer walks fast, keep up** (when it is safe to). Do not stroll back to the truck when the
  crew is waiting on a part.
- **Compete** with your trainer and the others on site. Try to finish your half of the row first. Try
  to have the next kit ready before they ask. Healthy competition makes good techs better.

### But Only After the Gate

There is a gate you must pass before you push your pace. **Pace comes only after you have a firm grip
on the safety factors and fully understand the task, the next step and the steps beyond.** Speed built
on top of shaky safety or a task you don't understand is how people get hurt and how jobs get redone.

You have passed the gate for a task when you can say yes to all of these:

- I can name the hazards and the controls for every step, without looking.
- I have done this task correctly, under supervision, enough times that I do not have to think about
  the order.
- I know the next step and the steps after it.
- My trainer agrees I am ready to work at full speed on this task.

Until then, **work at the pace you can do it right and safely**, and ask your trainer to help you get
faster.

### Where Pace Comes From

Fast technicians are not hurrying their hands. Their speed comes from:

- **Planning:** they saw the whole job (Lesson 5).
- **Forecasting:** the next part is always ready (Lesson 6).
- **No wasted motion:** tools in the same pouch pocket every time, the truck organized, one trip
  instead of three.
- **Repetition:** they have done it enough times that the steps flow.
- **Moving with purpose between steps:** walking briskly, not rushing on the ladder.

### What Never Speeds Up

Some steps take the time they take, no matter how far behind you are or how fast the crew is going:

- Lockout, tagout and live-dead-live verification.
- Ladder inspection, setup and climbing with three points of contact.
- Lift inspection, path walk and travel.
- Barricading and checking the area under you.
- Reading the instructions for a product you have not installed before.
- Securing the fixture and checking it.

If you find yourself shortening any of those to keep up, you have not passed the gate. Slow down and
tell your trainer.

> **Safety:** Never run on a job site, never climb or descend a ladder in a hurry, and never move a lift
> faster than the travel path allows to win a race. Keep up when walking is safe; never trade a safety
> step for speed. A tech who wins the race by skipping a lockout lost.

> **Safety check:** You are racing your trainer on a row of troffers and you are one fixture behind.
> The next fixture is on the edge of the locked-out area. What is the hazard? It might be on a different
> circuit. What is the highest control? Confirm which circuit feeds it and lock it out if needed, then
> verify dead. That costs you the race on this row. That is the right call.

> **Think ahead:** Pick one task you do every day and time yourself doing it the right way. Write down
> the time. Each week, look for one thing (staging, organization, a better sequence) that cuts wasted
> motion without touching a safety step, and time it again.

## Key Takeaways
- Step back multiple times during every task and ask: are we still on track? Check safety, quality, pace, flow, material, scope and next steps.
- Correct course right away: fix safety first, fix quality before it repeats, fix the flow, report what you cannot fix.
- Do it right the first time; a callback costs more than doing it right.
- Keep up with or exceed the pace of others, keep up with a fast-walking trainer when it is safe, and compete on site.
- Push your pace only after you have a firm grip on the safety factors and fully understand the task, the next step and the steps beyond.
- Pace comes from planning, forecasting and no wasted motion, never from shortening safety steps.
