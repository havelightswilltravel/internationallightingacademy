## The Job Is Always Moving

Lesson 5 taught you to see the whole job before you start. This lesson is about what happens **once
you have started.** A job is not a list you check off one item at a time. It is moving, and so are the
people, the equipment and the material. The technician who is only thinking about the step in their
hands is always a little behind.

Our standard is simple: **always be thinking about the next step, and the next seven steps.** That is
called **forecasting**, and it is one of the skills that separates technicians who run jobs from
technicians who wait to be told what to do.

## What Forecasting Looks Like

While your hands are on the current step, your head is asking:

1. What is the very next step, and is everything ready for it?
2. What will we need in the next half hour that is not here yet?
3. Who is about to be waiting, and what can they do now?
4. What could slow us down: material, equipment, access, a shutdown, another trade?
5. What will be different in an hour: the store opens, it gets dark, the lift battery runs low?
6. Is our material count on track to finish, or will we run short?
7. What does the end of the shift look like, and what do we need to start setting up for it?

Here is a real "next seven steps" list for a tech working the ground on a troffer retrofit row:

1. Hand up the kit for fixture 5 (current step).
2. Open and stage the kit for fixture 6 on the cart.
3. Move the ladder for fixture 6 and set it up while the lead finishes 5.
4. Check that fixtures 6 to 10 are on the circuit that is locked out.
5. Box the old lamps from fixtures 1 to 5 before the box fills.
6. Count kits left on the cart and in the cartons: enough for the row?
7. Ask the lead whether the next row needs a new shutdown, and give the site contact a heads-up.

None of those steps is hard. The skill is **keeping that list running in your head all day**, and
updating it every time something changes.

## No Idle Bodies on the Job

There should never be an idle body on the job. When someone is standing around, it is almost never
because they are lazy. It is because **nobody forecast what they should be doing next.** Our company
pays for every person on the crew. If two people are working and one is watching, we are paying for
three and getting two.

Forecasting is how you make sure every bit of manpower is used:

- **If you are about to be idle, say so and find the next task.** "I'm done staging, want me to start
  boxing recycle or prep the next row?"
- **If you see someone about to be idle, give them something.** Crew leads do this all day, and you
  should learn to do it too.
- **Always have a "backlog" of useful work** for gaps: breaking down cartons, boxing recycle, labeling,
  pre-assembling kits, cleaning the work area, updating counts, checking the next area for obstructions.

> **Safety:** "No idle hands" never means rushing, skipping steps or doing two things at once on a
> ladder. Forecasting is done with your head while your hands do the current step safely. If thinking
> ahead is pulling your attention off a hazard, stop thinking ahead until you are off the ladder.

## Remove the Bottlenecks

A **bottleneck** is anything that makes people or equipment wait. There are three kinds on our jobs.

### Labor Bottlenecks

People waiting on other people.

- The lift waits while the ground person searches for parts. **Fix:** the ground person pre-assembles
  kits and has the next one ready before the lift comes down.
- Two techs both wait for the lead to answer a question. **Fix:** ask your questions in a batch at a
  natural break, and learn the answer so you don't have to ask twice.
- One person does the dirty work and the clean work, so new kits get fingerprints. **Fix:** split
  gutting from installing.

### Equipment Bottlenecks

People waiting on equipment.

- One ladder for two techs. **Fix:** stage a second ladder at the next fixture ahead of time.
- The lift travels back and forth across the building. **Fix:** plan the order so the lift moves in one
  direction, and **prep the next area while the lift is moving**: move the barricade, clear
  obstructions, stage material under the next position.
- The lift battery dies midshift. **Fix:** check the charge at the start, plan the charge at break,
  and know where the outlet is.
- The meter battery dies at verification. **Fix:** check meter batteries and bring spares.

### Material Bottlenecks

People waiting on material.

- Kits are on a pallet at the far end of the building. **Fix:** stage fixtures and kits on carts near
  the work before the shift starts.
- Connectors run out halfway through. **Fix:** count at the start, track as you go, and **call for
  material before it runs out**, not after.
- The recycle box overflows and work stops while someone builds a new one. **Fix:** always have the
  next empty box built and waiting.

## Concrete Habits That Remove Bottlenecks

- **Stage fixtures and ladders ahead.** Before the current fixture is done, the next fixture's
  material and ladder should already be at the next spot.
- **Prep the next area while the lift is moving.** Move barricades, clear carts or displays (with the
  site's permission), and stage material.
- **The ground person pre-assembles kits.** Open cartons, remove packaging, install connectors or
  pre-fit parts that the instructions allow on the ground, and hand up a kit that is ready to go.
- **Call for material before it runs out.** If the count shows you will be short tomorrow, call your
  supervisor today. Midshift is better than end of shift; end of shift is far better than tomorrow.
- **Think about the end of the shift early.** Start boxing recycle and cleaning up the finished area
  before the last fixture, so the crew is not standing around while one person cleans.
- **Keep the truck organized.** A five-minute search for a part, done ten times a day, is almost an
  hour.

## Constantly Adjust

Your forecast is only as good as your last update. Things change:

- A fixture is different from the survey.
- The lift has to move to a different aisle because of a delivery.
- The store manager asks you to finish one area first.
- Someone on the crew has to leave early.
- Material arrives damaged.

When something changes, **re-forecast right away**: what is the new next step, who is going to be
waiting, what do we need now? Then tell the crew. A forecast in one person's head does not help a
crew; a forecast that is shared does.

> **Safety check:** When the plan changes, the hazards change too. Moving the lift to a different
> aisle means a new travel path, new overhead obstructions and a new barricade. Starting a new area
> may mean a new circuit to lock out and verify. Re-run the hazard-and-control check (Lesson 3) for
> every change, not just the work.

> **Think ahead:** At every break, take thirty seconds and write down your next seven steps. Compare
> them with what your trainer is planning. Where you differ, ask why. That is the fastest way to learn
> to forecast like a lead.

## Manpower Math

Forecasting is partly about numbers. Simple example, with made-up figures for teaching only:

> **Example figures only:** These numbers are made up for teaching. Use the numbers on your own work
> order or project specifics sheet.

A crew of three has 60 fixtures left in a row and about 5 hours of work time. That is 12 fixtures an
hour. The lift crew can do about 10 an hour if the ground person keeps them fed. **Forecast:** the
crew will not finish unless something changes. Options to raise with the lead: a second ladder for the
low fixtures at the end of the row, splitting gutting from installing, staging all remaining kits now.
Or, if none of that works, telling the supervisor early so the schedule can be adjusted. What you do
**not** do is make up the gap by skipping lockout, verification or quality checks.

## Key Takeaways
- Once a task starts, always think about the next step and the next seven steps: forecasting is key.
- There should never be an idle body on the job; forecast so every bit of manpower is used.
- Remove labor, equipment and material bottlenecks: stage ahead, prep the next area while the lift moves, pre-assemble kits, call for material before it runs out.
- Constantly adjust the forecast when anything changes, and share it with the crew.
- Every change in the plan is a change in the hazards; re-check them.
- Forecasting happens in your head while your hands work safely; never rush or skip steps to keep up.
