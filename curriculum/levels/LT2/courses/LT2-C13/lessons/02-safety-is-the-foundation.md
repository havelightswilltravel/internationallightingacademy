## Why We Review Before We Build

Every skill in LT2 sits on top of your LT1 safety training. If that base is shaky, everything you
add on top of it is shaky too. A technician who can install a Type B retrofit perfectly but forgets
to verify the circuit dead is not a good technician. They are an accident that has not happened yet.

So before we teach any new task, we are going back through all of it. This is not a summary for
people who were not paying attention in LT1. It is the most important lesson in LT2. Read it
slowly, and be honest with yourself about each section. At the end there is a checklist. If you
cannot say "yes, I do this every time" to every line, talk to your trainer before moving on.

**The rule for this level:** you cannot progress until safety is first and foremost on your mind in
every task. Only after safety is always in the back of your head do we start training on tasks.

## The Safety Mindset

Our company's safety rules start with one idea: **learn the safe way to do your job before you
start.** Everything else follows from that.

- **Every task, every time.** There is no such thing as "just a quick one." Most injuries in our
  trade happen on small, routine tasks, because that is when people skip steps.
- **Treat all wiring as live** until you have locked it out and verified it dead yourself, even if
  you turned it off yourself.
- **Inspect before you use.** Ladders, lifts, meters, cords, PPE and vehicles all get looked at
  before they get used.
- **Report immediately.** Unsafe conditions, injuries, near misses and damage are reported right
  away, not at the end of the shift.
- **No horseplay.** Ever.
- **Stay in your scope.** Lighting technicians work inside the lighting fixture. Anything beyond that
  needs approval and the right qualification.

## Hazard Recognition and PPE

You learned to scan every job for hazards before you start: electrical, falls, struck-by, falling
objects, heat and cold, chemicals, sharp edges, traffic, the public, and biological hazards. You
also learned that PPE is your **last** line of defense, not your first. Lesson 3 of this course
builds that into a method you will use on every task.

Your basic PPE and when it applies:

| PPE | When |
|---|---|
| Safety glasses | Always on the job, and under any face shield |
| Work gloves | Handling fixtures, lamps, sheet metal and recycle |
| Rubber insulating gloves with leather protectors | Verifying absence of voltage and any task your electrical safety program requires them for; air-tested before use |
| Hard hat (Class E) | Anything can fall on you, or you could strike your head, including in a lift or bucket |
| Hi-visibility vest | Parking lots, roads, loading docks, anywhere vehicles move |
| Hearing protection | Loud equipment and loud sites |
| Proper work boots | Every day |
| Dust mask (voluntary) | Available above ceilings and in attics; if you wear one you get the OSHA Appendix D information |

Inspect PPE every time. Damaged PPE is replaced, not "made to work."

## Electrical: Shock and Arc Flash

Electricity is the hazard that defines our trade. Review the key points:

- **Shock** can stop your heart, cause deep burns, and make you fall from a ladder or lift. A small
  shock on a ladder can cause a fatal fall.
- **Arc flash** is an explosion of energy at a fault that can burn, blind and deafen you. Panels and
  disconnects are where arc flash lives, which is why panel work has its own rules and PPE.
- **Lighting fixtures surprise people.** Our technicians have found live wiring in "dead" fixtures
  from other circuits passing through, wrong original wiring, emergency battery packs, 24-hour
  night-light circuits and shared neutrals.

### Lockout/Tagout: Our Company's Steps

You learned these in LT1, and they do not change in LT2:

1. **Identify** the equipment: which fixtures, circuits and controls.
2. **Determine availability:** get site management's approval before shutting down any lighting,
   and make sure affected people know.
3. **De-energize and tag** at the normal control and then the breaker.
4. **Lock out** with your own personal lock. Lock and tag on every point that could turn it back on.
5. **Safety review:** confirm the breaker is physically inoperative and stored energy (capacitors,
   battery packs) is discharged or isolated.
6. **Verify:** try the switch, return it to off, then test for absence of voltage at the work
   location, **live-dead-live**, with the right meter and gloves.
7. **Keep your lock and tag on** until your work is finished, then follow the removal sequence.

If the breaker cannot be locked, **stop and call your supervisor.** A tag alone is never enough for
your work, and a switch lock is only an extra warning, never the isolation point.

### Live-Dead-Live

Prove your meter on a known live source, test the circuit you are about to work on, then prove your
meter again on the known source. If the meter is not proven working before and after, a "dead"
reading means nothing. A non-contact tester is a screening tool, not verification.

## Ladders and Fall Protection

Falls are the other big killer in our trade. Review:

- **Pick the right ladder:** fiberglass near anything electrical; the right type and duty rating for
  you plus your tools and material; the right height so you never stand on the top step or top cap.
- **Inspect it** before every use: rails, rungs, feet, locks, spreaders, labels. Damaged ladders get
  tagged out and reported.
- **Set it up right:** extension ladders at 4:1 (12 ft up, base 3 ft out), extended at least 3 ft
  above a landing used for access, tied off against wind, spurs on soft ground. Stepladders fully
  open with spreaders locked.
- **Multi-position ladders:** use only the configurations shown on the label, check that every lock
  and hinge is fully engaged before you climb, and treat the large models as two-person work to
  carry and set up. Most of ours are aluminum and not electrically rated: lock out and verify first.
- **Climb right:** face the ladder, three points of contact, nothing in your hands, torso between the
  rails. Move the ladder instead of overreaching; never "walk" it.
- **Barricade below** and use a helper for long lamps and heavy fixtures.
- **Fall protection:** protect against falls at 4 ft on general work (6 ft in construction). Anchors
  rated 5,000 lb per worker, never tied off to conduit, pipe or fixtures. Know your fall clearance.
  Know the suspension trauma signs and that rescue must be fast.

## Aerial Work Platforms

- **Trained, familiarized on that model and authorized**, or you do not operate it.
- **Inspect and function-test** before every use. Red-tagged equipment is never used.
- **Scissor lifts:** guardrails are your fall protection; keep the gate or chain closed. **Boom lifts
  and bucket trucks:** harness at all times, lanyard on the platform's designated anchor.
- **Never** use a ladder on a platform, stand on the guardrails, or exceed the rated capacity.
- **Check the travel path:** floor holes, drains, slopes, cords, ice, overhead obstructions.
- **Spotter** for interior lift work, especially during business hours, and barricade the area below.
- **Power lines:** at least 10 ft from lines up to 50 kV; above that, the utility shields the lines
  first, under our policy.

## Heat, Cold and Hazardous Materials

- **Heat:** water, rest and shade; acclimatize new workers; watch each other. Confusion, hot skin or
  collapse can mean heat stroke: call 911 and cool the person right away. Attics, roofs and parking
  lots in summer are where our techs get into trouble.
- **Cold:** dress in layers, keep a dry spare set of gloves, watch for numbness and shivering, and
  remember freezer jobs last all day even in July. Ice under ladders and lift wheels is a slide waiting
  to happen.
- **Hazardous materials:** no SDS, no use. Label every secondary container. Know what you are handling
  and how to protect yourself.

## Lamps, Recycling and PCB Ballasts

- **All lamps are recycled**, including low-mercury lamps. Box them, tape both ends, label them.
  Never crush lamps unless the company has an approved, state-allowed system.
- **Broken lamp:** keep people away, ventilate, clean up by the procedure (no vacuum or broom for the
  first pass), and bag the debris for recycling.
- **Ballasts:** every removed magnetic ballast goes to the recycler. A ballast without a "No PCBs"
  label is treated as a PCB ballast, kept separate and counted. A **leaking** ballast is a stop: do not
  touch it bare-handed, keep people away and call your supervisor.

## Public Sites and Biological Hazards

- **The public is your hazard and you are theirs.** In stores, offices, schools and parking lots,
  barricade your work area, keep customers and children out from under ladders and lifts, and never
  leave tools or open fixtures unattended within reach.
- **Biological hazards** above ceilings and in attics, restrooms and parking structures: needles,
  droppings, nests, dead animals, blood and bodily fluids, insects. **Look before you reach.** Do not
  touch them; stop and call your supervisor for the right cleanup.
- **Suspected asbestos** in old ceiling tile or insulation: stop and call.
- **Unknown equipment** with warning labels: do not touch, move or work next to it until the site
  contact says it is safe.

## Tools and Meters

- Use the right tool for the job, in good condition, for its intended use. Inspect cords and
  plugs; damaged ones get tagged out.
- Use a meter rated for the voltage and category (CAT III or higher on building circuits), with good
  leads and probe guards. Prove it live-dead-live.
- Use insulated tools where your training calls for them.

## Vehicles, Trailers and Load Securement

- **Walk-around** before you drive: tires, lights, fluids, damage, mirrors.
- **Secure the load:** ladders tied at both ends, material strapped or blocked so it cannot shift or
  fly forward in a hard stop. Nothing loose in the cab.
- **Trailers:** hitch latched and pinned, safety chains crossed, lights working, the load balanced,
  and you have been trained to tow and back the trailer.
- Drive defensively, no phone, and back with a spotter or after a walk-around (get out and look).

## First Aid and Incident Reporting

- Know where the first aid kit and fire extinguisher are on your vehicle and at the site, and where
  the emergency exits are.
- For anything serious, **call 911 first.** Then your supervisor.
- **Report every injury immediately**, however small. Cuts get infected and backs get worse overnight.
- Report near misses and property damage the same way. They are how we learn without anyone getting
  hurt.

## Customer Service and Work Orders Are Safety Too

How you communicate with the site is a safety control. Telling the store manager which aisle will be
closed and for how long, getting approval before a shutdown, and writing hazards you found on the
work order all keep people safe after you leave.

## Stop Work and Calling a Job

**Anyone on our crew can stop work** when something is unsafe, and nobody gets in trouble for using
it in good faith. You stop when:

- Something changed and the plan no longer fits (a new hazard, missing equipment, weather).
- Someone asks you to skip a safety step, including a customer, a site manager or a schedule.
- You find a hazard you are not trained or equipped to handle.
- You do not have the training to do the task safely (Lesson 4).

To **call a job**, you make the area safe, tell the site contact, and call your supervisor. A job is
called with manager buy-off: your supervisor and you agree on it, and the decision is documented.
You never just walk off. You also never keep going because you feel pressure to finish.

> **Safety check:** Pick any routine task you did last week, such as changing a driver in an office
> troffer. List every hazard you can think of: shock, fall, dropped parts on someone below, sharp
> sheet metal, an occupant walking under the ladder, a second circuit in the fixture. For each one,
> name the control you used. If you cannot name a control for a hazard, you just found a gap in your
> habits. Bring it to your trainer.

## The Firm-Grasp Checklist

Answer each line honestly. "Yes" means **every time, without being reminded.**

- I check myself for fitness for duty before every shift.
- I do a vehicle walk-around and secure the load before I drive.
- I scan every job for hazards before I start and name the controls.
- I wear and inspect the PPE the task needs.
- I barricade my work area in public spaces.
- I inspect, choose and set up the right ladder, and climb with three points of contact.
- I do not operate a lift I am not authorized and familiarized on, and I inspect it first.
- I lock out with my own lock and tag and verify live-dead-live before touching conductors.
- I check for emergency packs, other circuits and stored energy in fixtures.
- I handle lamps and ballasts by the recycling and PCB rules.
- I look before I reach above ceilings and leave biological hazards alone.
- I report every injury, near miss and damage immediately.
- I stop work when something is unsafe or I am not trained for it.

If you answered "no" or "usually" to anything, that is what you work on first, before any new task.
Tell your trainer which lines they were. A good trainer will be glad you said so.

## Key Takeaways
- LT1 safety is the foundation of everything in LT2; you cannot progress until it is automatic.
- Treat all wiring as live until you lock it out with your own lock and verify it dead live-dead-live.
- Choose, inspect and set up ladders and lifts correctly, barricade below and protect the public.
- Recycle all lamps, treat unlabeled ballasts as PCB, and stop for leaks, asbestos and biological hazards.
- Report injuries, near misses and damage immediately, and use stop-work whenever something is unsafe.
- Use the firm-grasp checklist and tell your trainer about any line you cannot say "yes" to.
