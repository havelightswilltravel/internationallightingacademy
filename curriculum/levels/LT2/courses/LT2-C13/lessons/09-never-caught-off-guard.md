## Your Surroundings Are Always Changing

A job site is not a classroom. People come and go, vehicles move, weather turns, other trades start
work, power gets turned on and off, and the building's own systems run on their own schedule. The plan
you made at the start of the task was based on the conditions at the start of the task. Those
conditions will not stay the same.

**Situational awareness** means always being aware of your surroundings, watching for changes and
adjusting your work practices and speed so you are **never caught off guard.**

Here is the hard truth our company wants you to take to heart: **if you are caught off guard, you
weren't paying attention.** That is not meant to make you feel bad. It is meant to make you take
awareness as seriously as any other safety skill. When it happens, the answer is always the same:
**stop and reassess.**

## The Three Levels of Awareness

Situational awareness is more than looking around. It has three levels:

1. **Notice:** see and hear what is happening around you. The forklift, the open dock door, the
   employee near your ladder.
2. **Understand:** know what it means for you. The forklift is backing toward your barricade. The dock
   door means wind and rain on a ladder. The employee is about to walk under you.
3. **Predict:** think about what will happen next. The forklift driver may not see the barricade. The
   wind will pick up when the second door opens. The employee will want to get into the aisle you
   closed.

The goal is to get to level 3 before the change reaches you, so you can adjust ahead of time instead of
reacting.

## What to Watch For

### People
- Customers, employees, children and pets moving toward your work area.
- Someone moving your barricade, cones or ladder.
- Other trades starting work nearby, especially above or below you.
- Your own crew: where they are, what they are doing, whether anyone looks tired, hot or distracted.

### Vehicles and Equipment
- Forklifts, pallet jacks, floor scrubbers, delivery trucks, cars in a parking lot.
- Your own lift: the battery level, the travel path, what is overhead.
- Backup alarms, horns, engines starting.

### The Building
- **Power coming back on**, lights in the next area turning on, time clocks and sensors switching.
- Doors opening and closing, HVAC kicking on, sprinkler or alarm systems being tested.
- Water on the floor, a new spill, a cord across a walkway.
- Smells: burning, hot plastic, gas, chemicals.
- Sounds: buzzing, popping, cracking from a ceiling, an alarm.

### Weather and Light
- Wind picking up, rain starting, lightning, temperature climbing in an attic or on a roof.
- Darkness coming earlier than you planned on a parking lot job.
- Ice forming on the lot under the lift.

### Yourself
- Getting tired, hot, thirsty, hungry, frustrated or rushed. Your own condition is part of your
  surroundings.

## Scan Habits

Awareness is a habit you build on purpose:

- **Scan before every climb** and every time you come down: look all around the ladder or lift, up and
  down.
- **Look up before you move a lift** and look down before you lower it.
- **Look before you reach** into a ceiling, fixture or attic.
- **Every few minutes, lift your eyes** from your hands and look around the work area. Make it a
  rhythm.
- **Listen.** Keep your ears open: leave the music earbuds and headphones off while you work. Where
  hearing protection is needed, wear it and look around more often to make up for what you can't hear.
- **Keep the phone in your pocket.** You cannot be aware of your surroundings and read a screen at the
  same time.
- **Talk to your crew.** Call out changes: "Forklift coming down aisle six." "Lights just came on in the
  next room." Your crew can only adjust to what they know about.

## Adjust Your Work Practices and Speed

When something changes, you change. Examples:

| Change you notice | Adjustment |
|---|---|
| The store just opened and customers are coming into the area | Re-check barricades, slow the lift travel, add a spotter, finish the current fixture and reassess whether to keep working in that aisle |
| Wind picks up on a parking lot job | Stop lift work if it is beyond the manufacturer's limit; lower the platform; secure loose material; reassess before going back up |
| Lights in the next row come back on while you are working | Stop. Find out why. Re-verify that your circuit is still dead before touching anything |
| A forklift starts working in the next aisle | Talk to the driver or site contact, extend the barricade, keep a spotter on the ground |
| You are getting light-headed in the attic | Get out now. Water, shade, rest. Tell your partner. |
| Another trade is working above your area | Stop working below them; coordinate with their lead or the site contact |
| You smell something burning | Stop, find the source from a safe position, kill power if it is safe and in your scope, call your supervisor |

**Slowing down is an adjustment too.** When the area gets busier, the weather gets worse or you get
tired, the right move is often to slow down, add a control, or stop.

## If You Are Caught Off Guard: Stop and Reassess

Sometimes something will surprise you anyway: a person suddenly at the foot of your ladder, a light
coming on in a fixture you thought was dead, a lift alarm you didn't expect. When that happens:

1. **Stop.** Freeze the work. Make the immediate situation safe: come down, hold position, get people
   clear.
2. **Reassess.** What changed? What else might have changed that I missed? Are my controls still good?
3. **Fix the controls** before you continue: re-barricade, re-verify, move the lift, wait for the
   weather.
4. **Tell your crew and your trainer** what happened.
5. **Report it as a near miss** if anyone could have been hurt.
6. **Ask yourself what you missed.** Being surprised means something got past your awareness. Find out
   what, so it does not happen again.

> **Safety:** A light coming on in a fixture you believe is locked out is never "probably nothing."
> It may mean a second circuit, a back-feed, an emergency pack or someone who removed a lock. Stop,
> get clear of the fixture, and re-verify before anyone touches it again.

## What Kills Awareness

- **Fatigue.** Tired brains miss things. Come to work rested, and tell your supervisor if you are not.
- **Heat and dehydration.** They slow your thinking before you feel sick.
- **Rushing.** When you hurry, your focus narrows to the task in your hands.
- **Routine.** The hundredth fixture of the night is when you stop looking around. Fight it with
  deliberate scans.
- **Phones and earbuds.**
- **Frustration and arguments.** Settle it later; stay aware now.

> **Safety check:** Scenario: you are on a 10-ft stepladder in a store aisle, barricaded at both ends,
> circuit locked out. A store employee pushing a stocking cart moves your caution tape to get through.
> Hazards: the cart hitting the ladder, the employee under you, a dropped part. Controls, highest first:
> stop work and hold on, keep the employee out from under you (you cannot eliminate them from the
> store, but you can ask them to wait), restore the barricade (engineering), ask the manager to remind
> staff about the closed aisle (administrative), keep your hard hat and glasses on (PPE). Then reassess:
> should someone stand at the end of the aisle while you finish?

> **Think ahead:** Before you start each task, ask: "What is likely to change while I am doing this?"
> Store opening, shift change, weather, darkness, another trade, deliveries. Plan your adjustment now, so
> when it happens, you are ready instead of surprised.

## Key Takeaways
- Always be aware of your surroundings and watch for changes in people, vehicles, the building, weather and yourself.
- Work through the three levels: notice, understand and predict, so you adjust before the change reaches you.
- Build scan habits: before and after every climb, before moving a lift, before reaching, and every few minutes. Keep your phone and earbuds away.
- Adjust work practices and speed when conditions change; slowing down or stopping is an adjustment too.
- If you are caught off guard, you weren't paying attention: stop, reassess, fix the controls, tell the crew and report the near miss.
