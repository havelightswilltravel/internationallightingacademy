## The Situation Every Tech Will Face

Sooner or later you will be on a job and realize: **I have not been trained to do this safely.**

It might be a fixture type you have never seen, a lift model you have not been familiarized on, a
roof with no edge protection, a panel you are not authorized to open, a chemical without an SDS, or
a customer asking you to "just take a look" at something outside lighting. It might happen with your
trainer on site, or on a day you are working alone.

What you do in the next minute matters more than anything else you do that day.

## The Rule: Stop, Make It Safe, Call

If you find yourself in a situation where you do not have the training to do the task safely:

1. **STOP.** Do not start, or stop where you are. Do not "figure it out as you go."
2. **Make it safe.** Leave the area in a safe condition: close what you opened if you can do that
   safely, keep power locked out or off, barricade the area, and keep people away. Do not try to fix a
   hazard you are not trained to fix.
3. **Call your supervisor.** Explain what you found and why you are not trained for it.
4. **Wait for direction.** Your supervisor may send someone qualified, walk you through it if it is
   within your level, reschedule, or have you do other work.
5. **Suggest new or revised training** so the next tech is ready (more below).

This is not weakness, and it is not a reason to get in trouble. It is exactly what our company wants
you to do. Our first safety commandment is **learn the safe way to do your job before you start.** If
you have not learned it, you are not ready to start.

> **Safety:** The most dangerous words on a job site are "How hard can it be?" Watching a video on your
> phone in the parking lot is not training. Neither is a customer telling you "the last guy just did
> it." If you are not trained and authorized, stop and call.

## How to Recognize You Are Not Trained

Sometimes it is obvious. Sometimes it is a feeling. Treat these as signs to stop and ask:

- You have **never done this task**, or never done it on this type of equipment.
- You did it once with your trainer, but **you could not explain each step** and why.
- You do not know **what the next step is**, or what could go wrong.
- You cannot name the **hazards and the controls** for each step (Lesson 3).
- The equipment is **a model you have not been familiarized on**: a different lift, a different
  meter, a battery pack you have never seen.
- The task needs **authorization** you do not have: operating a lift, opening a panel, energized work,
  towing a trailer, working near power lines.
- The task is **outside the lighting fixture**: building wiring, panels, controls systems, anything
  your scope does not cover.
- **Conditions are different** from what you trained for: much higher, much hotter, outdoors in wind,
  in a live production area, with the public all around.
- Your **gut says something is wrong.** Listen to it.

## Examples from the Field

| What you find | What a tech without the training does | What you do |
|---|---|---|
| A boom lift on site instead of the scissor lift you were trained on | "Controls are probably similar." | Stop. Do not operate it. Call your supervisor about familiarization or a different plan. |
| A fixture fed from a central inverter or generator transfer switch | Turns off the breaker and starts work | Stop. Equipment with more than one source may need its own procedure. Call. |
| Old ceiling tile that looks like it could contain asbestos | Pushes it aside and keeps going | Stop. Leave it undisturbed. Call. |
| The site manager asks you to fix a dead outlet in the break room | "Sure, I have a tester." | Politely explain it is outside today's work order and that you will pass it to your supervisor. |
| A roof fixture near an unprotected edge | "I'll stay away from the edge." | Stop. You need a fall protection plan you were trained on. Call. |
| A retrofit kit with a wiring diagram you do not understand | Wires it the way the last kit was wired | Stop. Read the instructions, then call your trainer before you energize anything. |

## What to Say When You Call

Supervisors can help you fastest when you give them the whole picture. Use this order:

1. **Where you are** and what the task is.
2. **What you found** that you were not expecting.
3. **What you are trained for, and what you are not.** Be specific.
4. **What you have done to leave it safe.**
5. **What you think the options are**, if you have ideas.

*Example:* "I'm at the east-side store on the work order for the dock lights. The fixtures are fed
from a contactor that's on a time clock I've never seen, and there's a second set of wires in the box
that I can't identify. I've locked out the breaker on the schedule but I'm not confident that's the
only source, and I haven't opened anything else. I've coned off the dock door. Do you want me to wait
for someone, or work on the inside fixtures in the meantime?"

That call takes one minute and gives your supervisor everything they need.

## What Not to Do

- **Do not guess.** Guessing is how people get hurt and how equipment gets destroyed.
- **Do not let pressure decide for you.** Not the schedule, not the customer, not your pride, and not
  a crew member who says "just do it."
- **Do not leave without telling anyone.** Calling a job is done with the site contact informed and
  your supervisor's buy-off.
- **Do not hide it** at the end of the day. If you stopped, write it on the work order.

## Boots on the Ground Drive the Training

Our training program is built from real jobs. The people who know best what is missing are the
technicians in the field: **you.** Every time you hit something you were not trained for, that is
information our company needs. If it happened to you, it will happen to the next tech.

So after you stop and call, **suggest new or revised training.** You can do it two ways:

- Use **the feedback/suggestion button in the training app**, or
- **Tell your supervisor**, who will pass it on.

Either one is fine. Do it the same day while the details are fresh.

### What Makes a Good Training Suggestion

A good suggestion is specific enough that someone who was not there can build a lesson from it.
Include:

- **The task** you were asked to do.
- **What you ran into** that the training did not cover: equipment, conditions, a step that was
  missing or unclear.
- **Whether it is new training or a fix** to an existing lesson (name the course and lesson if you
  know it).
- **What would have helped:** a demonstration, a checklist, a photo of the equipment, a
  familiarization session, a "show me" with an evaluator.
- **A photo** of the equipment or label, if the site allows photos and there is nothing confidential
  in it.

*Example suggestion:* "Task: replace emergency ballasts in a school hallway. Ran into: fixtures had a
combination emergency driver and LED driver in one unit with a remote test switch. LT2 lessons only
cover separate emergency ballasts. Suggest: a short lesson with the wiring diagram and the test
procedure for combo units, plus a photo of the label. Photo attached."

Our curriculum is reviewed on a regular cycle, and field suggestions are one of the main inputs. You
may see your suggestion turn into a lesson that the next group of new techs takes.

### Near Misses Count Too

If you started a task and only then realized you were not trained for it, that is a **near miss.**
Report it the same way you report any near miss. Nobody is punished for an honest near-miss report.
It is how we fix the gap before it hurts someone.

> **Safety check:** Think of the last time you felt unsure on a job. What was the task? What hazard were
> you not sure how to control? Which level of the hierarchy was missing: did you need a way to
> eliminate the hazard, a better piece of equipment, a procedure you had not learned, or PPE you did
> not have? Write it down. If it still is not covered in your training, submit it as a suggestion
> this week.

> **Think ahead:** Before every job, look at the work order and ask: "Is there anything on this list I
> have not been trained to do?" If yes, call your supervisor **before** you leave the shop. Finding the
> gap at the shop is a phone call. Finding it on site can be a wasted trip or an injury.

## Key Takeaways
- If you do not have the training to do a task safely: stop, make it safe, call your supervisor, and wait for direction.
- Signs you are not trained include a new task or model, missing authorization, out-of-scope work, changed conditions and not knowing the next step.
- Give your supervisor the whole picture: where, what you found, what you are and are not trained for, what you made safe.
- Never let schedule, customers, pride or other people push you into a task you are not trained for.
- Boots on the ground drive training: suggest new or revised training through the feedback/suggestion button in the training app or by telling your supervisor.
- Make suggestions specific, and report a task you started without training as a near miss.
