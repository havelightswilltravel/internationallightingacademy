## Two Questions Before Every Task

Before you touch anything on any task, you answer two questions:

1. **What can hurt someone here?** (the hazards)
2. **What are we doing about each one, starting with the strongest fix?** (the controls)

This lesson teaches you a method for answering the second question that safety professionals
everywhere use: the **hierarchy of controls**. Once you learn it, you will use it on every task for
the rest of your career, and from now on every task lesson in this program will ask you to.

## The Hierarchy of Controls

Controls are ranked from most effective to least effective. You always start at the top and work
down. PPE comes **last**.

| Order | Control | What it means | Lighting examples |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Elimination** | Physically remove the hazard so it is not there at all | De-energize and lock out so there is no voltage at the fixture; do the work from the floor instead of at height (lower the fixture, pre-assemble on the ground); schedule the work when the store is closed so there is no public under you |
| 2 | **Substitution** | Replace the hazard with something less dangerous | Use a scissor lift instead of a tall extension ladder; a platform ladder instead of a stepladder for long work at one spot; a less hazardous cleaner; a lighter fixture or pre-wired kit when the scope allows |
| 3 | **Engineering controls** | Isolate people from the hazard with physical means | Barricades and caution tape, lift guardrails, lockout devices, insulated tools, ladder levelers, lights for night work, ventilation fans, probe guards on meter leads |
| 4 | **Administrative controls** | Change the way people work: procedures, training, schedules, signs, communication | LOTO procedure, live-dead-live, spotter, work-rest schedule in heat, pre-task brief, warning signs, telling the site manager which aisle will be closed, authorization to operate a lift |
| 5 | **PPE** | Protect the person when the hazard is still there | Safety glasses, gloves, rubber insulating gloves, hard hat, hi-vis vest, hearing protection, harness in a boom lift |

**Why PPE is last:** PPE only works if it is the right kind, in good condition, worn correctly, every
second. A barricade works even when you forget about it. Removing the voltage works even if your
gloves have a pinhole. That is why you look for a way to remove or isolate the hazard first.

**Why it is not "pick one":** real tasks almost always use several layers at once. Locking out
removes the voltage, the lock is a physical device, the procedure and the test are administrative,
and you still wear gloves to verify. The order tells you **where to start looking**, so you never
jump straight to "wear gloves and be careful."

> **Safety:** "Be careful" is not a control. If the only thing between you and a hazard is your own
> attention, you have not controlled it yet. Go back up the list.

## The Method: Step, Hazard, Control

For every task, do this before you start. With practice it takes a couple of minutes.

1. **Break the task into steps**, start to finish (Lesson 5 goes deeper).
2. **For each step, name the hazards.** Think about energy (electrical, gravity, stored energy, heat),
   the environment (height, weather, traffic, attic, cooler), people (public, other trades, your
   crew), and materials (glass, mercury, PCBs, sharp metal, weight).
3. **For each hazard, choose controls from the top down.** Ask in order: Can we eliminate it? Can we
   substitute something safer? Can we guard or isolate it? What procedure, training or communication
   do we need? What PPE?
4. **Check what is left.** Is the remaining risk acceptable? Are you trained and authorized for every
   step? Do you have every control on hand?
5. **If the answer to step 4 is no, do not start.** Fix it or call your supervisor (Lesson 4).

Now let us walk through five lighting tasks you will see in LT2.

## Worked Example 1: Relamping in an Occupied Store

**The task:** Replace failed lamps in six troffers over a sales aisle in a store that is open.

| Step | Hazards | Controls (highest first) |
|---|---|---|
| Plan the shutdown | Lights off over shoppers; staff unaware | **Elimination:** ask whether the work can be done before opening or after close so no public is present. **Administrative:** get the manager's approval for the shutdown and agree on which aisle closes and for how long. |
| Close the aisle | Customers, carts, children under the ladder | **Engineering:** barricade both ends of the aisle with cones and caution tape. **Administrative:** a second person or store staff redirects shoppers if needed. |
| Lock out | Shock from the fixture; other circuits; night-light circuit | **Elimination:** de-energize at the breaker. **Engineering:** your lock. **Administrative:** tag, LOTO procedure, live-dead-live at the fixture. **PPE:** gloves and eye protection for verification. |
| Climb and open | Fall; lens door swinging down; sharp edges | **Substitution:** platform ladder if it fits the aisle. **Engineering:** stepladder fully open and locked, on a clean floor. **Administrative:** three points of contact, control the door. **PPE:** work gloves, safety glasses. |
| Remove and replace lamps | Broken glass, mercury | **Administrative:** hand lamps down, never toss; box removed lamps right away. **PPE:** gloves, glasses. Broken lamp procedure if one breaks. |
| Restore and clean up | Re-energizing while someone is in a fixture; trip hazards | **Administrative:** confirm everyone is clear, remove your lock, test the lights. Remove barricades last, after the area is clean. |

Notice where we started: before anything else, **can this be done when the store is closed?** That
one question removes the biggest public hazard entirely.

## Worked Example 2: Exterior Pole Light at Night in a Parking Lot

**The task:** A crew replaces a failed fixture head on a 25-ft parking lot pole after dark, using a
lift operated by an authorized crew member, with you as the ground person.

| Step | Hazards | Controls (highest first) |
|---|---|---|
| Plan | Darkness; traffic; power lines; weather | **Elimination:** ask whether the work can be done in daylight or with that part of the lot closed. **Administrative:** check the weather; no lift work in high wind or lightning. Confirm overhead lines and keep at least 10 ft from lines up to 50 kV. |
| Set up | Vehicles driving through; pedestrians | **Engineering:** cones and a barricaded zone around the pole and lift, the vehicle parked to shield the work area with beacons on, work lights. **PPE:** hi-vis vest. |
| Position the lift | Soft ground, curbs, slopes, light-pole bases, overhead branches | **Administrative:** walk the path with a light first; follow the lift's ground and slope limits; spotter. **Engineering:** outrigger pads where the lift calls for them. |
| De-energize | Shock; lot lights on a time clock or photocell; HID capacitor | **Elimination:** lock out the lot lighting circuit. **Administrative:** check for time clocks and contactors, verify live-dead-live at the pole handhole or fixture, allow capacitors to discharge and verify. **PPE:** gloves and eyes for verification. |
| Work at height | Falls; dropped tools and parts | **Engineering:** platform guardrails, tool lanyards. **Administrative:** nobody stands under the platform. **PPE:** harness in a boom lift or bucket, hard hat for the ground person. |
| Lower old fixture | Weight, pinch points | **Administrative:** plan the hand-off; use a line or lower it in the platform, not by hand over the side. **PPE:** gloves. |

As the ground person, you are not "just watching." You are a control: you keep vehicles and people
out of the zone, you watch the lift and the overhead lines, and you have the next part ready.

## Worked Example 3: High-Bay Retrofit from a Scissor Lift

**The task:** Retrofit 40 high-bay fixtures at 28 ft in a warehouse with forklifts working nearby.

| Step | Hazards | Controls (highest first) |
|---|---|---|
| Plan | Forklift traffic; product racking; heavy fixtures | **Elimination:** ask the site to close or reroute traffic in the bay where you are working, or schedule the work for a shift when forklifts are not running. **Administrative:** agree on the zone with the site contact. |
| Inspect lift | Mechanical failure | **Administrative:** pre-use inspection and function test; red-tagged lifts are never used. Only authorized, familiarized operators run it. |
| Move the lift | Floor holes, drains, cords, racking, overhead pipes, sprinkler heads | **Engineering:** barricade the lift's area. **Administrative:** walk the travel path, lower the platform to travel where the manufacturer requires, spotter. |
| Lock out | Shock; multiple circuits in a row | **Elimination:** lock out every circuit feeding the fixtures in the zone. **Administrative:** verify live-dead-live at each fixture before opening it. |
| Remove and install | Fall from the platform; dropped parts; heavy fixture | **Engineering:** guardrails with the gate closed; lower heavy fixtures with the lift, not by hand down a ladder. **Administrative:** two people for heavy fixtures; never stand on the rails or use a ladder on the platform. **PPE:** hard hat for everyone below, gloves, glasses. |

## Worked Example 4: Emergency Ballast Change

**The task:** Replace a failed emergency battery ballast in a troffer in an office hallway.

| Step | Hazards | Controls (highest first) |
|---|---|---|
| Plan | Hallway loses its emergency lighting while you work | **Administrative:** tell the site contact that the fixture's emergency function is out until the work is done; finish and test the same visit. |
| Lock out | Unswitched hot feeding the charger; **battery stays energized after the breaker is off** | **Elimination:** lock out the breaker feeding both the switched and unswitched legs, and **disconnect the battery connector** before touching any wiring. **Administrative:** verify live-dead-live on every conductor, including the output leads to the lamps or LED. |
| Replace | Shock from the battery pack output; sharp edges | **Administrative:** follow the manufacturer's wiring diagram exactly; keep the battery disconnected until the end. **PPE:** gloves, glasses. |
| Test | Fixture wired so it does not work in an outage | **Administrative:** reconnect the battery last, restore power, check the charge indicator, run the test switch, and label the date per the instructions. |

The hazard most people miss here is the **stored energy in the battery**. The breaker off does not
make an emergency fixture dead.

## Worked Example 5: Working in an Attic

**The task:** Replace a failed recessed fixture housing reached from an attic above a small office.

| Step | Hazards | Controls (highest first) |
|---|---|---|
| Plan | Heat; nobody knows you are up there | **Elimination:** ask whether the work can be done from below (many housings can be serviced through the ceiling opening), or early in the morning before the attic heats up. **Administrative:** tell someone where you are and set check-in times; water with you. |
| Access | Falling through the ceiling between joists; scuttle-hole ladder | **Engineering:** a walk board or plywood across the joists; ladder set up and extended properly at the scuttle. **Administrative:** step only on framing or the board. |
| Move to the fixture | Nails through the roof deck, low trusses, insulation, wildlife, droppings, suspect vermiculite | **Administrative:** look before you move or reach, with a good light. Stop and call your supervisor for suspected asbestos (vermiculite) or droppings and nests. **PPE:** hard hat or bump cap, long sleeves, gloves, glasses; dust mask is available and encouraged (voluntary). |
| Lock out | Shock; other circuits through the junction box | **Elimination:** lock out and verify live-dead-live at the fixture. |
| Work | Heat illness; poor lighting | **Engineering:** portable fan and work light. **Administrative:** short work periods, breaks outside the attic, water, a partner who checks on you. |

## Common Mistakes

- **Jumping straight to PPE.** "I'll wear gloves" before asking whether the circuit can be locked out.
- **Thinking only about electricity.** The fall, the forklift and the heat hurt just as many people.
- **Planning once.** Hazards change during the task: the store opens, it starts raining, another
  trade shows up. Re-run the method when anything changes (Lesson 9).
- **Accepting a control you do not have.** If the plan needs a barricade and you did not bring cones,
  the plan is not done.
- **Planning for yourself only.** Think about your crew, the public and the people who will use the
  building after you leave.

> **Think ahead:** For tomorrow's first task, write three columns in your notebook: Step, Hazard,
> Control. Fill them in before you leave the shop, and show your trainer. Doing it on paper now is how
> you will do it in your head in six months.

## The Pre-Task Brief

On our crews, the method above is said out loud in a short **pre-task brief** before work starts.
By the end of LT2 you will lead one yourself (skill LT2-S24). A good brief covers:

1. The task and the whole sequence, start to finish.
2. The hazards, step by step.
3. The controls, in hierarchy order.
4. Confirmation that everyone is trained and authorized for their part.
5. The equipment and material needed, and where it is staged.
6. The next steps after this task.
7. When we would stop.

## Key Takeaways
- Before every task, name the hazards and choose controls using the hierarchy of controls.
- The order is elimination, substitution, engineering, administrative, then PPE last.
- Start at the top: can the hazard be removed or the work done another way? "Be careful" is not a control.
- Use several layers together, and re-run the method whenever conditions change.
- Watch for hidden hazards: emergency batteries, other circuits, the public, forklifts and attic heat.
- Say it out loud in a pre-task brief, and do not start if a control is missing.
