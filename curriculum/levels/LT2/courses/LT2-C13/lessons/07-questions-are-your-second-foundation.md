## Safety First, Questions Second

Safety is the foundation of a technician. **Questions are the secondary foundation.** Almost every
skill you will build in this program starts with a question: why is it done this way, what happens
next, what could go wrong, what does the customer want?

Techs who ask a lot of questions learn fast, make fewer mistakes and get trusted sooner. Techs who do
not ask questions stop growing. Here is the rule our company wants you to live by:

**If you're not asking questions, you're not evolving.**

## Ask Question After Question

There is no limit. Ask question after question, all day, every day. You are not bothering your
trainer; teaching you is their job, and your questions show them what you understand and what you
don't. A trainer who hears no questions has to guess, and guessing is how new techs end up doing a
step wrong for months before someone notices.

### Ask Even When You Think You Know

This is the part new techs skip. You think you know the answer, so you don't ask. But "I think" is
not "I know." **Ask even when you think you know, to verify you haven't missed something.** It takes
five seconds:

- "I'm going to lock out breaker 14 for this row. Is that the only circuit feeding it?"
- "The kit says ground the bracket to the fixture body. Same as the last one, right?"
- "We're going to do the east row next, then the dock?"

Half the time the answer is "yes, you've got it," and you learn that your thinking is right. The
other half of the time you just caught a mistake before it happened.

### Question Everything You Don't Have Full Working Knowledge Of

"Full working knowledge" means you could do it right, explain why each step is done, and handle it if
something goes wrong. If you do not have that for a task, a tool, a part or a procedure, **question
it** until you do:

- A new product: "What's different about this driver from the one we used last week?"
- A procedure: "Why do we verify at the fixture and not just at the panel?"
- A site rule: "Why does this store want a spotter when the other one didn't?"
- A number: "Where does the 4:1 ladder angle come from?"
- An instruction from someone else: "Can you show me what you mean?"

## How to Ask Good Questions

Some questions get better answers than others. A good question is:

- **Specific.** Not "How do I do this?" but "Do these sockets need to be replaced for the Type B kit, or
  are they already non-shunted?"
- **Shows your thinking.** "I think we lock out breaker 14 and 16 because the panel schedule shows two
  circuits for this area. Is that right?" This lets your trainer correct exactly the part you have
  wrong.
- **Asked at the right moment** (below).
- **Repeated back.** When you get an answer, say it back in your own words: "So: battery
  disconnected first, wire it, reconnect the battery last, then test. Got it."
- **Written down.** If you need the answer again tomorrow, write it in your notebook or phone
  tonight. Asking the same question three times tells your trainer you are not listening; asking it
  once and writing it down tells them you are.

### Questions That Do Not Help

- **Questions you could answer yourself** in a minute by reading the work order or the instructions
  you have in your hand. Read first, then ask what is still unclear.
- **Questions that are really complaints:** "Why do we always have to box the lamps?" Ask "What
  happens if lamps aren't boxed and labeled?" instead, and you'll learn something.
- **Questions you don't listen to the answer for.**

## When to Ask

| When | Why | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Before the job (at the shop)** | Answers are easiest to get and change the most | "What's the access like? Do we need the long ladder?" |
| **Before each task** | Sets up the hazards, controls and sequence | "What's the full scope here, start to finish?" |
| **Before each new step** | Confirms you know the next step | "After the sockets, do we test or move on?" |
| **When something is different** | Different conditions mean different hazards | "This fixture has a second set of wires. What is it?" |
| **When you are about to do something you can't undo** | Cutting, drilling, re-energizing, removing a lock | "Ok to cut the old whip back to here?" |
| **At natural breaks** | Batch your non-urgent questions | "I wrote down three questions from this morning..." |
| **After the job** | Turn the day into learning | "What would you have done differently on that one?" |

**Safety questions are always the right time.** If you think something is unsafe, ask immediately,
even if it interrupts.

**Pick your moment for non-urgent questions.** Do not distract someone who is verifying a circuit,
moving a lift or on a ladder with both hands full. Wait until they finish the step, then ask.

## Who to Ask

| Who | Ask them about |
|---|---|
| **Your trainer or crew lead** | How to do the task, the sequence, the next step, why we do it this way |
| **Your supervisor** | Scope changes, anything you are not trained for, schedules, material shortages, customer requests outside the work order |
| **The site contact** | Access, building hours, where to stage and store material, the location of panels, who else is working, site rules |
| **Manufacturer instructions and labels** | How the product is wired and installed; the instructions are often required for the installation to meet its listing |
| **The training app and library** | Procedures, background and reference material, especially during your study time |
| **Other experienced techs** | Tips and tricks, how they handled something similar; confirm with your trainer if it differs from what you were taught |

> **Safety:** If two people give you different answers on a safety question, do not pick the easier
> one. Stop and ask your supervisor, and follow the stricter answer until you hear back.

## Question Bank: What a Professional Asks

Use these until they become habits.

**Before you leave the shop**
- What is the full scope of work, and what does done look like?
- Is there anything here I haven't been trained to do?
- What equipment, ladders or lifts do we need, and are they on the truck?
- How much material do we have, and is it enough?

**On arrival**
- Who is the site contact, and what are the site's rules?
- Where are the panels, and what controls this lighting?
- When is the area busiest, and what must look normal by opening?

**Before each task**
- What are the hazards, and what controls are we using?
- What is the next step, and the step after that?
- What could go wrong, and when would we stop?

**During the job**
- Are we on track?
- Is anyone about to be waiting?
- What changed, and what does that change about the hazards?

**After the job**
- What went well? What would we do differently?
- Is there anything I should study tonight?

> **Safety check:** Scenario: your trainer says "go ahead and do the next row," and you are not sure
> whether it is on the same circuit. The hazard is shock from a fixture you think is dead. The highest
> control, locking out and verifying the right circuit, depends on a question you have not asked yet.
> Ask it: "Same circuit as this row, or do we need another lockout?" Then verify anyway.

> **Think ahead:** Keep a running "question list" in your notebook or phone during the day. At each
> break, look at it: answer what you can from the instructions, and ask the rest. At the end of the
> week, the list shows you exactly what to study.

## If You Feel Awkward Asking

Many new techs worry that asking questions makes them look like they don't know anything. The
opposite is true. On our crews, the techs who **don't** ask are the ones who worry supervisors, because
nobody can tell what they actually understand. Every experienced tech you admire got there by asking a
lot of questions. Ask.

## Key Takeaways
- Questions are your secondary foundation: if you're not asking questions, you're not evolving.
- Ask question after question, and ask even when you think you know, to verify you haven't missed something.
- Question everything you don't have full working knowledge of.
- Ask good questions: specific, showing your thinking, at the right moment, repeated back and written down.
- Know who to ask: trainer for the task, supervisor for scope and training gaps, site contact for the site, instructions for the product.
- Safety questions are always the right time; pick your moment for the rest.
